# The $100 Billion Jurisdiction War: How Wall Street Derivatives Law Collided With State Casinos, Tribal Sovereignty, and the Supreme Court

On Sunday afternoons inside living rooms and sports bars across the United States, millions of Americans open their smartphones to speculate on the outcome of NFL games. For years, those wagers flowed almost exclusively through state-licensed sportsbooks like DraftKings and FanDuel, heavily taxed by state treasurers and strictly geofenced within state borders. But over the past twenty-four months, a parallel, federally regulated financial apparatus has emerged to capture billions of dollars in transaction volume. On platforms operated by Kalshi, Robinhood, and Crypto.com’s North American Derivatives Exchange (Nadex), users are not officially placing "bets." Instead, they are purchasing all-or-nothing "binary event contracts"—cleared through designated contract markets overseen by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) in Washington, D.C.

That distinction in legal nomenclature has now triggered the most consequential federalism and regulatory showdown in American gambling history. A statutory collision between the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) of 1936 and the historic police powers of individual states has divided the federal judiciary, creating a direct circuit split that landed on the docket of the U.S. Supreme Court in September 2026. At its core, the dispute asks a multi-billion-dollar constitutional question: Did Congress, in passing the 2010 Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act, create a federal statutory preemption scheme that displaces state-level sports gambling regulation and tribal gaming sovereignty?

The crisis reached a flashpoint over the summer of 2026 following two irreconcilable appellate rulings. On April 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit delivered a landmark 2–1 victory to prediction markets in *KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty* (172 F.4th 220). Writing for the majority, Circuit Judge David J. Porter, joined by Chief Judge Michael A. Chagares, held that sports-related event contracts qualify as "swaps" under 7 U.S.C. § 1a(47) of the CEA. Because Congress vested the CFTC with "exclusive jurisdiction" over swap agreements under 7 U.S.C. § 2(a)(1)(A), the Third Circuit held that both field preemption and conflict preemption bar state gaming regulators from enforcing local gambling bans against federally licensed exchanges. The ruling affirmed a preliminary injunction against Mary Jo Flaherty, Interim Director of the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement (DGE), shielding federal sports derivatives across New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware from state gaming licensing requirements.

In a sharp dissent, Senior Circuit Judge Jane Richards Roth argued that the majority had severed statutory interpretation from historical context, warning that expanding the definition of financial swaps to encompass the point spread of athletic competitions subverts more than two centuries of traditional state police powers over gambling.

That reasoning prevailed across the country four months later. On August 28, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit broke directly with the Third Circuit in *KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad* (No. 25-7516). In a unanimous 3–0 decision authored by Circuit Judge Ryan D. Nelson, joined by Circuit Judge Bridget S. Bade, with a concurrence by Circuit Judge Kenneth K. Lee, the Ninth Circuit affirmed an order dissolving a preliminary injunction in Nevada. The panel held that sports event contracts are likely not swaps under the CEA because they do not involve an event or contingency associated with a demonstrable financial, economic, or commercial consequence. Concluding that the contracts fall outside the statutory definition of swaps, the Ninth Circuit held that the CEA does not preempt the Nevada Gaming Control Board from enforcing state gaming laws against the exchange.

Nineteen days later, on September 16, 2026, the Ninth Circuit delivered another critical ruling regarding the limits of federal exchange trading in *Blue Lake Rancheria v. Kalshi, Inc.* (No. 25-7504). Federally recognized tribes, including the Blue Lake Rancheria and the Chicken Ranch Rancheria of Me-Wuk Indians, brought suit against Kalshi and its retail trading partner, Robinhood, alleging that offering sports event contracts to individuals on reservation land without tribal authorization violated the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA) of 1988 (25 U.S.C. § 2701 et seq.). 

Writing for a unanimous panel that included Chief Judge Mary H. Murguia and Circuit Judge Richard A. Paez, Circuit Judge M. Margaret McKeown rejected Kalshi’s argument that CEA swap registration displaces IGRA. Holding that the contracts constitute unauthorized Class III gaming when accessed from tribal territory, Judge McKeown wrote: "A platform may reshuffle the cards, but it cannot change the hand. Labeling a wager on athletic prowess an 'event contract' does not alter its fundamental nature under the law."

The diverging appellate mandates have fractured the legal foundations of sports wagering. On September 2, 2026, the State of New Jersey and the DGE petitioned the U.S. Supreme Court for a writ of certiorari, urging the justices to overturn the Third Circuit’s *Flaherty* decision. Within days, retail platforms Robinhood and Crypto.com filed separate certiorari petitions challenging the Ninth Circuit’s *Assad* ruling, requesting consolidated review to prevent an unworkable patchwork of criminal liability and state-by-state prohibitions across the fifty states. Concurrently, Kalshi petitioned for an en banc rehearing in the Ninth Circuit, setting up a high-stakes race to the Supreme Court's docket.

Beneath the statutory interpretations lies an existential fiscal dispute over state tax revenues. When the Supreme Court invalidated the Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act (PASPA) in *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018), it returned sports betting authority to the states, generating critical tax streams. In New York, mobile sportsbooks are taxed at 51% of gross gaming revenue (GGR)—a framework that yields more than $1.2 billion annually for public education, youth sports, and problem gambling initiatives. Pennsylvania levies a 36% tax on GGR, while New Jersey collects 14.25% from online operators.

By contrast, designated contract markets registered with the CFTC are subject only to standard corporate income taxes and nominal federal regulatory transaction fees that equate to fractions of a cent per executed contract. For retail volume and institutional market makers, trading on CFTC-regulated platforms eliminates the 51% state tax overhead entirely. Regulators, including New York Attorney General Letitia James, have argued in state enforcement actions that classifying event contracts as commodities creates an unlegislated tax haven that could drain tens of billions of dollars from state budgets.

In court filings, prediction platforms have defended their product suite by citing the commercial hedging utility of event contracts. Under federal commodities jurisprudence, derivatives exist to allow commercial enterprises to manage risk: agricultural producers hedge crop prices, and airlines hedge fuel volatility. Kalshi and industry trade groups submitted declarations arguing that sports event contracts provide authentic hedging utility: a sports bar in Philadelphia hedging lost beverage revenue if the home team is eliminated from playoff contention; a commercial ticket broker hedging unsold postseason inventory; or an outdoor entertainment venue hedging event cancellations.

Trading activity among retail participants, however, presents a different operational profile. In public retail investing forums on Reddit's r/wallstreetbets and specialized wagering communities, users routinely trade NFL and collegiate event contracts as short-duration speculative instruments, colloquially termed "0DTE sports options." The appeal for retail traders centers heavily on cost efficiency: traditional sportsbooks extract a 5% to 8% margin through bookmaker odds, whereas peer-to-peer event markets operate with tight bid-ask spreads and permit users to close positions dynamically prior to match settlement. As one trader summarized the prevailing sentiment on r/wallstreetbets: "Why would I let DraftKings rob me with an 8% house vig when I can scalp Kalshi's fourth-quarter binary contracts like they're tech calls?"

This operational overlap with sports wagering has drawn intense scrutiny from consumer protection authorities. Under state sports betting regimes, participation is strictly limited to individuals aged 21 and older, supported by centralized self-exclusion lists, mandatory cooling-off periods, and state-funded treatment hotlines. In contrast, under the CEA and CFTC regulations, derivatives trading accounts are accessible to individuals aged 18 and older, without statutory mandates for problem-gambling self-exclusion registries. While former Vice President Kamala Harris and congressional leaders previously voiced apprehension over algorithmic speculation among young adults, regulatory appointments under President Donald Trump’s administration have emphasized market access and capital formation, leaving the jurisdictional boundaries to be resolved through federal litigation.

When the Supreme Court reviews the petitions for its 2026–2027 term, the dispute will force a clash between textualist statutory construction and constitutional federalism.

Textualists on the court will examine the definition of a swap under 7 U.S.C. § 1a(47)(A)(v), which encompasses any transaction "dependent on the occurrence, nonoccurrence, or the extent of an occurrence of an event or contingency associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." Because professional athletic competitions yield measurable economic impacts on television networks, stadium vendors, and local commerce, the exchange platforms argue that the plain text unambiguously places these instruments under exclusive CFTC jurisdiction.

Conversely, proponents of state sovereignty—guided by the principles articulated by Justice Samuel Alito in *Murphy v. NCAA*—will weigh whether Congress intended to displace centuries of state police power over gaming through general definitions in a 2010 financial reform act. State regulators contend that under the Major Questions Doctrine and the presumption against preemption, an explicit statutory mandate from Congress is required to federalize sports gambling.

As the high court considers the petitions, the regulatory architecture of American sports wagering remains geographically split. In the Third Circuit, event contracts operate under federal commodities protections; in the Ninth Circuit and across tribal territories, those identical transactions face state prohibitions and tribal gaming violations. The Supreme Court now faces the task of settling whether an athletic wager, when cleared as a financial derivative, remains subject to the sovereign authority of the states.
