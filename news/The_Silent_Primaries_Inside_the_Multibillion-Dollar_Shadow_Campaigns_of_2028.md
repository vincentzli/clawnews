# The Silent Primaries: Inside the Multibillion-Dollar Shadow Campaigns of 2028

Under the pristine skies of Sun Valley, Idaho, where the global financial elite gathers annually for the Allen & Company conference, the first true shots of the 2028 presidential campaign were fired not on a debate stage, but in a private Q&A session. Citadel CEO Ken Griffin, the Miami-based hedge fund titan who injected over $100 million into the 2024 election cycle, sat across from New York Times columnist Andrew Ross Sorkin. Asked about the looming succession battle to replace President Donald Trump—who is constitutionally barred from seeking a third term under the 22nd Amendment—Griffin did not hesitate. He declared his unequivocal support for Secretary of State Marco Rubio over sitting Vice President JD Vance, revealing that he had actively warned Trump against selecting Vance as his running mate in 2024. 

Griffin’s public declaration exposed a brewing, multi-billion-dollar shadow primary that is rapidly dividing the Republican donor class. For months, President Trump has engaged in a favorite pastime at Mar-a-Lago: pitting his top two lieutenants against each other in front of Palm Beach megadonors. While Trump has publicly floated a Vance-Rubio ticket as "unbeatable," behind closed doors he has been testing their support. The rivalry is no longer confined to the drawing rooms of Florida. 

According to national polling data released by Emerson College Polling in May 2026, the Republican primary has entered a statistical dead heat. Vice President Vance, who held a dominant 52% to 20% lead over Rubio in February 2026, has seen his advantage evaporate. The May survey placed Vance at 36% and Rubio at 35% among likely Republican primary voters. Spencer Kimball, the executive director of Emerson College Polling, noted that "the Republican primary has shifted significantly since February... the potential 2028 contenders now compete evenly." 

This statistical tie is underpinned by a stark demographic and ideological divide. Rubio leads Vance 41% to 35% among Republican primary voters over the age of 50, drawing support from the party’s traditionalist establishment and Wall Street donors who favor his hawkish internationalism. Conversely, Vance maintains a 37% to 26% lead among voters under 50, cementing his status as the champion of the populist "New Right" and the Silicon Valley tech donors who have flocked to his Rockbridge Network. While Rubio has publicly downplayed his ambitions, stating he would step aside if Vance runs, his allies are quietly building the infrastructure for a national campaign.

On the Democratic side, the shadow primary is even more fragmented, with analysts predicting a field that could easily exceed 20 candidates. In the same May 2026 Emerson College survey, former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg led hypothetical matchups with 18% support, closely followed by California Governor Gavin Newsom at 16%. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez stood at 11%, while former Vice President Kamala Harris and Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro were tied at 10%, followed by Kentucky Governor Andy Beshear at 9%. 

Despite the early polling order, Buttigieg has emerged as the most active campaigner on the ground. At the National Action Network convention in April 2026, when Reverend Al Sharpton asked if he would seek the presidency in 2028, Buttigieg replied, "You save me a seat. I'll be there." Operating through a sophisticated network of political allies, the former South Bend mayor has spent 2026 traversing key midterm battlegrounds, making stops in Georgia, Pennsylvania, Wisconsin, Nevada, and New Hampshire. He has also ventured into deep-red territory, traveling to Oklahoma and Montana—where he promoted a ballot initiative to restrict corporate campaign contributions—while maintaining his policy credentials at high-profile events like the Mackinac Policy Conference.

Meanwhile, Governor Newsom is leveraging his Campaign for Democracy PAC, which he launched using $10 million in leftover gubernatorial campaign funds. Newsom, whose second term as California Governor ends in January 2027, has embarked on a national tour designed to boost Democratic candidates in statehouse and congressional races, with a heavy focus on Nevada and the South. In an interview with CBS News Sunday Morning, Newsom admitted he would be "lying" if he said he wasn't considering a 2028 campaign, adding that a final decision would come after the November midterms. 

Yet, the most dramatic entry into the Democratic conversation has come from Arizona Senator Mark Kelly, whose political stock soared following a direct clash with the Trump administration. In November 2025, Kelly and five other veteran lawmakers released a video titled "Don't Give Up the Ship," reminding U.S. military personnel of their legal duty to refuse illegal orders. President Trump immediately denounced the video as "seditious behavior," while Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth labeled the lawmakers the "Seditious Six," initiating grade determination proceedings to demote Kelly from his retired rank of Navy Captain and slash his military pension.

Kelly struck back in January 2026, filing a federal lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia. In February 2026, a federal judge granted an injunction blocking the Pentagon's demotion efforts, calling the move unconstitutional retaliation. The legal victory turned Kelly into a national cause célèbre for the Democratic base, triggering a massive fundraising surge. In a February 2026 appearance on BBC’s Newsnight, Kelly confirmed he was "seriously considering" a 2028 presidential bid.

As both parties march toward the November 2026 midterm elections, these shadow campaigns represent more than mere posturing. They are the mechanisms through which the next generation of American political leadership is being financed and forged, transforming the midterms into a proxy war for 2028.
