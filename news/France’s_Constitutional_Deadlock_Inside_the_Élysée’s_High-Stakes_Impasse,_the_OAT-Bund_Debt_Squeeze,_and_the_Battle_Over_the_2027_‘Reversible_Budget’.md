# France’s Constitutional Deadlock: Inside the Élysée’s High-Stakes Impasse, the OAT-Bund Debt Squeeze, and the Battle Over the 2027 ‘Reversible Budget’

In the corridors of the Palais Bourbon and the offices of the Élysée Palace, French governance has entered an unprecedented state of institutional siege. Ever since President Emmanuel Macron’s snap legislative elections of June and July 2024 fractured the 577-seat National Assembly into three mutually hostile ideological blocs, France has cycled through three prime ministers in less than two years. The conservative Michel Barnier fell in December 2024 after Marine Le Pen’s National Rally (RN) joined the left-wing New Popular Front (NFP) to pass a historic motion of no confidence against his austerity budget. Centrist veteran François Bayrou followed in September 2025, his minority government collapsing after attempting to push through €44 billion in fiscal consolidation. Today, Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu oversees a deeply vulnerable administration, tasked with managing a €3.2 trillion debt load amid the most volatile fiscal showdown of the Fifth Republic.

The immediate flashpoint centers on the national budget for 2027, which Lecornu’s administration has formally designated as a "reversible budget." Formulated less than eight months before the April–May 2027 presidential election, the fiscal blueprint deliberately omits long-term structural reforms. The explicit objective is to establish a transitional framework that can be readily altered or rescinded by whichever political majority takes power in the spring of 2027. However, the proposal has drawn severe backlash across the political spectrum and intensified anxiety across European sovereign debt markets. 

France’s macroeconomic indicators highlight the scale of the vulnerability. The public deficit, which reached 5.8% of GDP in 2024 and stayed at 5.4% in 2025, is projected by Fitch Ratings to remain elevated at 5.2% in 2026 and 5.5% in 2027. Total public debt stands at roughly 113% of GDP, exceeding €3.22 trillion. In sovereign bond markets, the spread between the French 10-year OAT (Obligations Assimilables du Trésor) and the German 10-year Bund has stabilized near 90 basis points, periodically matching or exceeding the yields demanded for Italian and Spanish sovereign paper. 

The arithmetic of the 577-seat National Assembly leaves the Lecornu cabinet with no organic legislative majority. The chamber is fractured into three primary factions: the New Popular Front controls roughly 190 seats, Macron’s centrist Ensemble coalition holds approximately 160 seats, and the National Rally alongside Éric Ciotti’s Union of the Right for the Republic commands 142 seats. The remaining seats are divided between the traditional right (Les Républicains) and unaffiliated lawmakers. Passing any substantive measure without concessions requires invoking Article 49.3 of the French Constitution—a mechanism that permits the government to adopt bills without a parliamentary vote but exposes the Prime Minister to immediate censure motions.

This deadlock has placed Article 12 of the French Constitution back at the center of national debate. Article 12 grants the President the prerogative to dissolve the National Assembly, subject to a mandatory twelve-month waiting period between dissolutions. With the one-year moratorium from the July 2024 ballot expiring in July 2025, opposition forces have stepped up concerted pressure on Macron to dissolve parliament once again.

Marine Le Pen, president of the RN parliamentary group, has labeled the sequence of minority governments an "unsustainable political masquerade," insisting that returning to the electorate is the only viable path to stability. "France cannot remain hostage to an isolated executive that ignores the National Assembly," Le Pen stated in a floor address, warning that the RN would not hesitate to support motions of censure against the 2027 budget. RN President Jordan Bardella reinforced this position, arguing that "a second dissolution is the democratic obligation of a president whose political project has been rejected."

On the left, Mathilde Panot, leader of the La France Insoumise (LFI) parliamentary group within the NFP, has declared that the "countdown on the Macron presidency is running out." Panot reiterated that the left will table joint or parallel censure motions against any budgetary package that preserves Macron’s 2023 pension changes or fails to introduce substantial wealth and corporate windfall taxes. 

Within the Élysée Palace, presidential advisers continue to push back firmly against the prospect of invoking Article 12 before 2027. "A second dissolution carries enormous systemic risk without offering any guarantee of a workable majority," an Élysée official familiar with strategy discussions explained. "Calling snap elections in this climate could simply deliver an absolute majority to the National Rally or further polarize the assembly, accelerating the very fiscal crisis the government is trying to contain."

Credit rating agencies and sovereign debt traders have responded to the political stalemate with strict scrutiny. Following S&P Global’s downgrade of France to 'A+/A-1' in late 2025 and Moody’s downgrade to 'Aa3', Fitch Ratings affirmed France’s rating at 'A+' with a stable outlook in August 2026, explicitly noting that persistent political fragmentation restricts the scope for structural consolidation. On financial forums and market commentary feeds on X, analysts and institutional desks have zeroed in on the rising risk premium, with one widely circulated bond commentary summarizing the sentiment: "With French OATs trading around a 90-basis-point spread over Bunds, markets are no longer treating France as core euro-area credit; the political discount is fully priced in."

The Lecornu government now faces a narrow corridor. If the executive relies on Article 49.3 to enact the 2027 "reversible budget," a unified censure vote combining the NFP and the RN would command well over the 289 votes needed to bring down the cabinet. For President Macron, such an outcome would force a pivotal choice: install a fourth minority administration in under two years, or invoke Article 12 to trigger snap elections that could reshape France’s political and economic trajectory just months before the presidential ballot of 2027.
