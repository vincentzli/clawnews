# The Pickaxe Divide: Inside the High-Stakes Oval Office Clash Over Iran’s Unbustable Nuclear Bunker

Just hours before entering the Oval Office for a high-stakes 90-minute summit on July 28, 2026, U.S. President Donald Trump made a telephone appearance on Fox & Friends that laid bare the widening strategic rift between Washington and Jerusalem. Confronted with reports that Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was arriving with a folder of fresh intelligence on Iran’s deeply buried nuclear enrichment facility south of Natanz, Trump was visibly irked. "I know exactly what's going on at Pickaxe," the President declared, referring to the site by its English translation. "It's not a big problem. I don't need Bibi to tell me that. Bibi's telling me that because he wants me to stay involved. Why do you have to announce to the world?"

This public friction set the tone for the first face-to-face meeting between the two leaders since the joint U.S.-Israel military campaign against Iran commenced on February 28, 2026. The two men were in Washington to attend the funeral of the late U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham at the Washington National Cathedral. Graham had passed away on July 11, 2026, at the age of 71 due to an aortic dissection, and his sister, Darline Graham, had been appointed to fill the remainder of his Senate term. But while the memorial service was a moment of public unity, the subsequent bilateral session in the Oval Office exposed a deep divergence over the endgame of a conflict that has dragged into its fifth month.

At the center of this strategic divide is "Pickaxe Mountain"—known in Persian as Kuh-e Kolang Gaz La. Located approximately 1.5 kilometers south of the main Natanz nuclear complex, the site has been under construction since 2020, following the sabotage of an above-ground centrifuge assembly plant. Satellite imagery analyzed by David Albright of the Institute for Science and International Security and Jeffrey Lewis of the James Martin Center for Nonproliferation Studies shows extensive tunneling into the Karkas range. The facility is buried between 100 and 140 meters beneath solid volcanic rock.

The geological reality of the mountain presents an almost insurmountable obstacle for conventional military planning. The mountain is composed primarily of andesite, a volcanic rock capable of withstanding pressures between 21,000 and 43,000 pounds per square inch. To neutralize such a target, the U.S. military relies on the GBU-57 Massive Ordnance Penetrator, a 30,000-pound precision-guided bomb. However, the GBU-57 is only rated to penetrate approximately 7.9 meters of medium-strength rock. According to nonproliferation experts, even consecutive strikes on the same coordinate would fail to reach the deep underground chambers. At best, a military strike would collapse the tunnel entrances, delaying operations by months or years as Iran excavated the portals, but failing to destroy the centrifuges inside.

For Netanyahu, leaving Pickaxe Mountain untouched is an unacceptable risk. Israeli military planners argue that a diplomatic resolution that leaves Iran's advanced enrichment infrastructure intact inside an unassailable mountain fortress is no resolution at all. Following the joint strikes on February 28, which resulted in the death of Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, Iran’s Assembly of Experts appointed his son, Mojtaba Khamenei, as his successor on March 8. Mojtaba has remained entirely in hiding, avoiding all electronic devices to evade U.S. and Israeli intelligence, and even skipping his father’s state funeral from July 3 to 9. Yet through state channels, he has maintained a hardline stance. The Israeli defense establishment believes that without destroying Pickaxe Mountain, the campaign’s gains will evaporate as Iran reconstitutes its program deep underground.

Trump, however, is facing severe domestic pressure to find a diplomatic exit. The war has severely strained the U.S. economy, pushing gasoline prices past $4.00 per gallon and dragging consumer confidence to record lows. The upcoming November 2026 midterm elections are less than 100 days away, and Republicans are facing a painful political backlash from voters connecting the conflict to their pocketbook issues. The cost of the war has already reached $37.5 billion, as Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth testified before the Senate Appropriations Committee on July 21. Hegseth appeared to defend the administration’s $88 billion supplemental funding request, which has faced fierce resistance from Senate Appropriations Committee Vice Chair Patty Murray and other Democrats who argue the Pentagon has not been transparent about its expenditures.

This public friction highlights the widening tactical divide between Washington's search for an exit strategy and Jerusalem's push for a decisive military conclusion. While Trump had mused during a July 21 Oval Office meeting with Lebanese President Joseph Aoun that "we'll be hitting that area pretty soon, and very heavily," his subsequent comments indicate a desire to leverage that threat for a negotiated deal rather than a prolonged military entanglement. Trump’s frustration with Netanyahu’s public briefing attempts reflects a belief that the Israeli Prime Minister is attempting to lock the U.S. into an open-ended war.

Following the 90-minute Oval Office meeting—which was attended by a high-level U.S. delegation including Vice President JD Vance, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Defense Secretary Hegseth, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and Special Envoy Steve Witkoff—both sides attempted to project alignment. White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt called the discussions "positive and productive," while Trump posted on Truth Social that "many important subjects were discussed." Netanyahu characterized the session as "excellent" and "one of the best," declaring the alliance to be "strong as a granite wall."

Yet, the diplomatic window-dressing was quickly punctured. Tzipi Hotovely, who assumed office as the head of the National Public Diplomacy Directorate on May 5, 2026, issued a statement clarifying that the Pickaxe Mountain facility had not even been discussed during the meeting, dismissing the pre-summit media reports as "fake news." This denial triggered a storm on social media. On X.com and Reddit’s r/CredibleDefense, military analysts and users debated whether the site was truly off the table or if the public omission was a tactical move to keep Iran guessing. Some users pointed out that by denying the briefing took place, the Israeli government was attempting to shield Netanyahu from the appearance of being publicly rebuked by the U.S. President. Others argued that the administration's reluctance to strike the mountain reflects a tacit admission of the limits of conventional American military power. As the midterms approach, the question remains whether Trump can secure the diplomatic off-ramp he desires before the pressure of the unbustable mountain forces his hand.
