# The Silent Silos: How the Death of New START Plunges the World into a Nuclear Blind Spot

The deep missile silos scattered across the windswept plains of Great Falls, Montana, and the dense, birch-choked forests of Kozelsk, Russia, have fallen completely silent. For the first time in more than half a century, the United States and the Russian Federation are operating without a single bilateral legal constraint on their strategic nuclear arsenals. The expiration of the New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START) on February 5, 2026, has quietly dismantled the last remaining guardrails of the post-Cold War nuclear order. What remains is a dangerous verification vacuum, where the world’s two preeminent nuclear superpowers must now peer at each other through the distorted lens of intelligence estimates and satellite imagery, raising the risk of a catastrophic miscalculation to its highest point in decades.

Originally signed in 2010 by Barack Obama and Dmitry Medvedev, and subsequently extended in 2021 by Joe Biden and his administration—with then-Vice President Kamala Harris—New START established strict, verifiable limits on the strategic forces of both nations. The treaty capped deployed strategic nuclear warheads at 1,550 and limited deployed launchers—including intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and heavy bombers—to 700. A further cap of 800 deployed and non-deployed launchers was instituted. Although Russia suspended its formal participation in the treaty in February 2023 in response to Western support for Ukraine, both sides initially continued to declare that they were respecting these caps. However, the failure to negotiate a successor framework before the February 2026 deadline has officially erased these legal boundaries.

The road to the treaty's demise was paved with divergent diplomatic agendas and a breakdown in trust. In late 2025, Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed a last-minute, one-year political commitment to maintain the numerical caps without formal verification mechanisms. The U.S. administration, led by President Donald Trump, flatly rejected the offer. U.S. officials argued that a voluntary agreement without on-site inspections was a hollow promise, especially given Russia's suspension of compliance in 2023. On the day the treaty expired, President Trump took to social media to criticize the old framework, stating: "Rather than extend 'NEW START' (A badly negotiated deal by the United States that, aside from everything else, is being grossly violated), we should have our Nuclear Experts work on a new, improved, and modernized Treaty that can last long into the future."

The Trump administration's decision reflected a strategic pivot away from bilateral treaties that officials argue fail to account for modern threat landscapes. The U.S. position has shifted toward demanding a comprehensive, modernized agreement. Washington insists that any successor treaty must regulate Russia's large stockpile of non-strategic (tactical) nuclear weapons—estimated by the Pentagon to be between 1,000 and 2,000 warheads—and must bring China into the arms control framework. 

This trilateral demand has met a wall of resistance in Beijing. The Chinese Ministry of Foreign Affairs has repeatedly rejected U.S. pressure to join trilateral negotiations, maintaining that the U.S. and Russia, which together possess approximately 90% of the world's nuclear weapons, must make deep, drastic, and legally binding cuts to their own arsenals before other states participate. Beijing asserts that its nuclear arsenal is kept at the "minimum level required for national security" and is governed by a strict "no first use" policy. However, U.S. intelligence suggests a different reality: China is rapidly expanding its strategic forces, constructing vast new silo fields in Yumen and Hami, with the Pentagon projecting Beijing's arsenal could grow to 1,000 operational warheads by 2030.

Meanwhile, Moscow has increasingly linked nuclear arms control to its broader security demands and claims of territorial sovereignty. Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov has repeatedly stated that strategic stability cannot be negotiated in isolation from the "hybrid war" waged by NATO against Russia in Ukraine. Furthermore, Moscow has insisted that if China is forced into arms control talks, Washington’s nuclear-armed allies—Great Britain and France—must also have their strategic forces capped. Following the treaty's expiration, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov warned that the global security architecture is rapidly eroding. In a June 2026 press briefing, Peskov declared that nuclear deterrence has become the "only" guarantee preventing a direct clash between major powers.

The immediate fallout of the treaty's expiration is not a sudden, massive surge in warhead production, but rather the total collapse of transparency. Under New START, the U.S. and Russia maintained an unprecedented level of visibility into each other’s nuclear forces. The treaty mandated 18 on-site inspections per year, allowing military inspectors to physically count warheads and examine delivery systems. It also required twice-yearly exchanges of detailed data and daily telemetry notifications. Over the fifteen-year lifespan of the treaty, the two nations exchanged more than 328,000 notifications detailing the status, location, and movement of every single strategic missile, bomber, and submarine.

With these mechanisms gone, both militaries must rely entirely on National Technical Means—primarily spy satellites, electronic eavesdropping, and human intelligence. Arms control analysts warn that this reliance on remote intelligence introduces dangerous ambiguity. In professional national security forums and online defense communities, the implications of this "blind spot" are hotly debated. On Reddit’s r/CredibleDefense, users have analyzed the loss of verification, with one commentator noting: "Without data exchanges, every routine maintenance cycle at an ICBM silo or submarine base looks like a potential breakout. Suspicion forces you to plan for the absolute worst-case scenario, which is how arms races start." Another user in r/geopolitics remarked: "The treaty was already a hollow shell since 2023, but codifying its death means there is no longer a legal baseline to return to. We are flying blind into a tripolar nuclear environment."

This lack of visibility significantly increases the risk of catastrophic miscalculation. During the Cold War, false alarms of incoming missile strikes occurred multiple times due to technical glitches or misread satellite data; disaster was averted only because protocols and communication channels remained open. Today, in an era of heightened geopolitical friction and rapid technological advances in hypersonics and artificial intelligence, the margin for error is virtually non-existent. A routine Russian military exercise near Kozelsk or a U.S. bomber deployment to Europe could easily be misread by satellite analysts as a preparation for a preemptive strike, prompting a preemptive response.

As the summer of 2026 progresses, the path forward remains highly uncertain. While the U.S. National Nuclear Security Administration continues to modernize its nuclear triad—including the deployment of the Sentinel ICBM to replace the aging Minuteman III—and Russia maintains its high-readiness posture, the diplomatic channels that once managed these existential threats are silent. The expiration of New START has not yet triggered a visible surge in deployed warheads, but it has officially removed the guardrails. Without a new treaty framework that accounts for the realities of a tripolar nuclear world, the global community is left to navigate the most complex security environment since the dawn of the nuclear age with its eyes wide shut.
