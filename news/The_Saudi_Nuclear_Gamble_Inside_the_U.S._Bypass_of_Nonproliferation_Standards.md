# The Saudi Nuclear Gamble: Inside the U.S. Bypass of Nonproliferation Standards

On the afternoon of July 22, 2026, U.S. Secretary of Energy Chris Wright and Saudi Minister of Energy Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman formally signed a peaceful nuclear cooperation framework. Known as a Section 123 Agreement, the pact establishes a decades-long, multi-billion-dollar framework allowing American companies to supply civil nuclear reactors, fuel, and technology to the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. Behind the diplomatic handshakes and the administration's assurances, however, lies a highly controversial geopolitical maneuver that bypasses long-standing nonproliferation guardrails. Rather than requiring Riyadh to adhere to the "gold standard" of nuclear cooperation—which explicitly prohibits uranium enrichment and plutonium reprocessing on domestic soil—the agreement opens a path for domestic enrichment, utilizing a presidential national security waiver to sidestep the requirement for the International Atomic Energy Agency’s (IAEA) Additional Protocol.

The signing of the agreement comes at a moment of acute regional instability. The United States and Iran are currently locked in an active, escalating military conflict that commenced on February 28, 2026, following U.S.-Israeli airstrikes that killed high-ranking Iranian officials. As of today, the U.S. military has conducted 12 consecutive nights of airstrikes against Iranian military targets. The Strait of Hormuz remains heavily blocked by Iranian forces targeting commercial tankers, and the U.S. Department of Defense has estimated the conflict's cost to the United States at $37.5 billion. Amid nightly exchanges of fire and threats by President Donald Trump to launch airstrikes against Iran's fortified "Pickaxe Mountain" nuclear facility near Natanz, the administration’s decision to export civil nuclear capabilities to Saudi Arabia has triggered immediate bipartisan congressional pushback.

Critics argue that the deal creates a dangerous precedent for regional nuclear proliferation. Under the terms of the signed documents, Saudi Arabia is not mandated to sign the IAEA's Additional Protocol, the global benchmark for intrusive, unannounced site inspections. Instead, monitoring will rely on bilateral safeguards. The agreement also provides a pathway for Saudi Arabia to eventually build a domestic uranium enrichment facility, subject to the results of a joint U.S.-Saudi study designed to evaluate its viability. Nonproliferation advocates point out that this is a severe regression from the precedent set by the 2009 U.S.-UAE nuclear agreement, in which the Emirates explicitly renounced domestic enrichment and reprocessing.

Congressional opposition has emerged immediately. Representative John Garamendi and Senator Edward Markey, co-chairs of the bicameral Nuclear Weapons and Arms Control Working Group, have characterized the administration's decision as a "recipe for disaster." In their view, providing enrichment technology to Riyadh increases the risk of the Kingdom pursuing a nuclear weapon, especially given Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman’s public warning that if Iran develops a bomb, Saudi Arabia will follow suit. Former Vice President Kamala Harris has also raised concerns, stating that bypassing standard IAEA protocols undermines decades of U.S. leadership on nonproliferation. In response, Markey and Representative Brad Sherman have vowed to use the legislative process to challenge the deal, pointing to their reintroduced "No Nuclear Weapons for Saudi Arabia Act," which seeks to mandate an affirmative vote from Congress for such agreements.

However, proponents of the deal defend it as a strategic necessity. Administration officials and congressional allies argue that if the United States does not partner with Riyadh, the Kingdom will turn to Russia or China for its nuclear infrastructure, depriving the U.S. of critical oversight. Furthermore, Saudi Arabia's massive energy needs—driven by a domestic push to power artificial intelligence data centers and diversify away from oil—mean that a civilian nuclear program is inevitable. By securing U.S. involvement, proponents argue, the U.S. maintains long-term oversight of the Kingdom's nuclear lifecycle and secures multi-billion-dollar contracts for American firms.

Under Section 123 of the Atomic Energy Act, the agreement will now enter a 90-day review period of continuous session in Congress. Because the administration utilized a national security waiver (specifically citing provisions from Section 1264 of the Fiscal Year 2020 National Defense Authorization Act) to submit the deal without the Additional Protocol, it does not require affirmative congressional approval to enter into force. Instead, it will automatically take effect after the 90-day period unless Congress passes a joint resolution of disapproval. Given that the current Republican-controlled Congress is generally supportive of the strategic partnership, and that overriding a presidential veto would require a two-thirds majority in both chambers, legislative efforts to block the deal face steep hurdles.

For the Middle East, the timing of the U.S.-Saudi agreement could not be more volatile. With Iran's nuclear program increasingly threatened by direct U.S. military action and the Strait of Hormuz closed to shipping, the introduction of a U.S.-backed nuclear pathway in Riyadh represents a profound realignment of regional power. Whether the joint study on enrichment leads to a domestic Saudi capability or serves as a diplomatic bargaining chip, the July 22 agreement has permanently shifted the boundaries of nuclear diplomacy in the Middle East.
