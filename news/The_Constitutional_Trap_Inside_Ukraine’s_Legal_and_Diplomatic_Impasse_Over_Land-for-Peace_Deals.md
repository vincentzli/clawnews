# The Constitutional Trap: Inside Ukraine’s Legal and Diplomatic Impasse Over Land-for-Peace Deals

Across the diplomatic corridors of Washington, Geneva, and European capitals, negotiations aimed at halting Europe’s largest land war have increasingly coalesced around the shorthand of the "Korean scenario": freeze active hostilities along Ukraine's 1,000-kilometer front line, erect a fortified demilitarized zone, and defer final sovereignty questions to an open-ended diplomatic track. Pushed by political architects around U.S. President Donald Trump—most explicitly detailed by Vice President JD Vance, who outlined a framework that would establish a fortified demilitarized line of demarcation while freezing Ukraine's integration into NATO—the formula is framed in Western policy circles as an operational necessity. Inside Kyiv’s government quarter on Bankova Street, however, this diplomatic blueprint collides with an intractable reality: Ukraine’s domestic constitutional architecture explicitly forbids the concessions demanded to enact it.

The primary diplomatic friction stems from an asymmetrical understanding of the stakes. While Western mediators frequently treat territory as an operational variable subject to armistice lines, Moscow demands formal, irreversible legal capitulation. In his June 2024 address at the Russian Foreign Ministry, Russian President Vladimir Putin set forth the Kremlin's non-negotiable preconditions for an armistice: Ukrainian forces must completely withdraw from the entirety of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson provinces to their administrative boundaries—requiring Kyiv to unilaterally abandon fortified regional capitals and industrial hubs it currently controls, including Zaporizhzhia city and Kherson—while securing international de jure recognition of all four territories, alongside Crimea, as integral parts of the Russian Federation.

For Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, signing an instrument that cedes sovereign territory is legally impossible under domestic statute. Under Article 73 of the Constitution of Ukraine, any alteration to the nation's borders can be decided "exclusively by an All-Ukrainian referendum." The executive branch possesses no constitutional power to alter national boundaries through treaties or ceasefire protocols. Yet exercising this popular mechanism is legally blocked during the war: Article 19 of Ukraine’s Law on the Legal Regime of Martial Law explicitly bars the execution of national referendums, presidential ballots, and parliamentary elections for the duration of martial law. 

This statutory barrier is reinforced by fundamental constitutional safeguards. Article 157 of the Constitution explicitly forbids any amendment of the foundational charter while martial law or a state of emergency is in effect, and bars any revisions directed against the territorial integrity of the state. Article 17 defines the preservation of territorial indivisibility as an essential function of the state. Legal scholars and parliamentarians in Kyiv have noted that any executive signature ratifying the surrender of sovereign soil would directly trigger criminal liability under Article 110 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine for infringing on territorial integrity, and Article 111 for state treason, subjecting the presidency to constitutional challenge and judicial prosecution.

Even in the theoretical event that martial law were rescinded to facilitate a national vote, holding a legally compliant plebiscite remains logistically impossible. More than six million Ukrainians remain registered as refugees across Europe under temporary protection directives, an estimated four million citizens are internally displaced, and roughly 20% of the sovereign population resides under foreign military occupation. Under Ukraine's Law on All-Ukrainian Referendums, enacted in 2021, a legitimate ballot demands verified voter lists, transparent counting mechanisms, and unimpeded campaigning—conditions that cannot be met across Russian-occupied cities such as Mariupol, Melitopol, or Luhansk.

Public polling underscores that any unilateral territorial compromise risks triggering immediate domestic political destabilization. Longitudinal studies conducted by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS) demonstrate a resolute societal barrier. While protracted attritional warfare has widened the share of Ukrainians open to pragmatic de facto operational pauses—with approximately 38% indicating tolerance for a frozen front line if accompanied by binding Western defense treaties—formal territorial surrender remains universally opposed. Over 70% of respondents categorically reject granting de jure recognition to Russian annexations, and more than 75% oppose any ceasefire predicated on withdrawing Ukrainian forces from government-held territories in the south and east. As KIIS Executive Director Anton Hrushetskyi observed, Ukrainian society draws an unyielding distinction between a temporary military ceasefire designed to preserve life and a formal renunciation of sovereign title, which citizens regard as national destruction.

This domestic posture is matched by diplomatic resistance among NATO’s European frontline states. Officials in Poland, the Baltic nations, Finland, and the Czech Republic have warned that formalizing border revisions through force dismantles the core prohibition of aggressive war enshrined in Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter and undermines UN General Assembly Resolution ES-11/4, which affirmed the invalidity of Russia's annexations with 143 votes. Frontline states argue that creating a recognized precedent of territorial conquest would destabilize European security, exposing frontier zones like the Suwałki Corridor to future incursions. Czech President Petr Pavel articulated this distinction following his September 2024 statements to The New York Times, emphasizing that while the military reality may result in Ukrainian lands remaining under temporary Russian control for an extended period, the democratic world must adhere to the precedent of the 1940 Welles Declaration: maintaining strict, indefinite non-recognition of illegal territorial conquests.

Facing Western pressure to compromise and an unyielding legal baseline at home, Bankova Street has made its red lines clear. In remarks to French media, Zelenskyy addressed the limits of presidential power directly: "Those in power have no official right to give up their territories." Armed forces personnel, combat veteran networks, and civil society groups have repeatedly warned that any political settlement bypassing the constitution would trigger a severe legitimacy crisis in Kyiv.

As external powers seek an exit ramp along the 1,000-kilometer front, the legal mechanics of the Ukrainian state remain immovable. Framed during the establishment of the post-Soviet republic to protect against partition, Ukraine's constitutional provisions leave zero room for executive land concessions. So long as Moscow insists upon formal territorial transfers and international recognition, any proposed armistice modeled on the Korean Peninsula remains an unworkable proposition.
