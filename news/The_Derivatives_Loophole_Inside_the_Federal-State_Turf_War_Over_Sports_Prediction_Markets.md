# The Derivatives Loophole: Inside the Federal-State Turf War Over Sports Prediction Markets

On August 26, 2026, Connecticut Attorney General William Tong stood before a bank of microphones in Hartford, flanked by Governor Ned Lamont and Department of Consumer Protection (DCP) Commissioner Bryan T. Cafferelli, to announce a sweeping lawsuit against the prediction market operator Kalshi. The state’s complaint, filed in Hartford Superior Court and quickly removed by Kalshi to federal court, targets the platform’s newly launched sports event contracts—which allow users to trade on point spreads, game winners, and player statistics. To Tong, the platform’s business model represents a flagrant circumvention of local law. "These are unlicensed wagers that bypass state consumer protections and laws designed to prevent problem gambling," Tong declared, rejecting the company’s assertion that its contracts are sophisticated financial instruments. "They are not magically shielded by federal law." 

The lawsuit is the latest escalation in a high-stakes legal battle that has been building since December 2025, when Connecticut’s DCP first issued a cease-and-desist order to Kalshi. The platform countered by filing its own federal lawsuit to block state enforcement. However, on August 10, 2026, U.S. District Judge Vernon Oliver denied Kalshi’s motion for a preliminary injunction, followed by a denial of an emergency injunction pending appeal on August 15, 2026. Judge Oliver ruled that Kalshi’s sports event contracts do not qualify as "swaps" under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) and that federal law does not preempt Connecticut’s authority to regulate them as gambling. Kalshi’s Head of Litigation, Jovy Dedaj, criticized the ruling, calling the state’s enforcement efforts "arbitrary and inconsistent."

The legal conflict highlights a deeper economic tension between state budgets and financial technology. State regulators argue that prediction markets bypass the rigorous licensing, responsible gaming frameworks, and significant tax levies imposed on legalized sportsbooks since the repeal of PASPA in 2018. Conversely, proponents of prediction markets argue that state enforcement actions act as protectionist barriers designed to shield state-licensed sports betting monopolies from competition by cheaper, exchange-based financial alternatives.

This local skirmish is part of a nationwide regulatory fracture. Just days earlier, on August 19, 2026, Kalshi was forced to implement geofencing in Washington State following a final order from a King County Superior Court judge, who ruled the platform violated the Washington Gambling Act. By September 2, 2026, the company must institute a comprehensive multi-source geofencing block for all Washington residents. Meanwhile, in Michigan, Ingham County Circuit Court issued a temporary restraining order carrying a crushing $120,000-per-day fine if Kalshi fails to use a licensed third-party geolocation provider to block Michiganders from placing sports-related wagers. 

At the heart of the dispute is a fundamental clash of legal definitions. Kalshi operates as a Designated Contract Market (DCM) regulated by the federal Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Under the CEA, DCMs are permitted to "self-certify" contracts, allowing them to list new markets without prior agency approval. Kalshi argues that because its sports contracts are traded on a CFTC-regulated exchange and tied to outcomes with real-world economic consequences—such as sponsorships, ticket sales, and team valuations—they are legally defined as "swaps." Under this interpretation, the CFTC maintains exclusive jurisdiction, rendering state gambling laws preempted. 

The platform’s legal position is anchored in a landmark April 6, 2026, decision by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. In *KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty* (No. 25-1922), a panel of judges issued a divided 2-1 ruling affirming a preliminary injunction against Mary Jo Flaherty, the Interim Director of the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement. The majority opinion, written by Judge David J. Porter and joined by Chief Judge Michael A. Chagares, held that sports-related event contracts qualify as swaps and that the CEA preempts state gambling laws as applied to these contracts. In a dissenting opinion, Judge Jane Richards Roth argued that states have historical police powers over gambling regulation and that state laws do not necessarily frustrate the CEA's goals. While the ruling created a massive federal shield for prediction markets operating in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware, its reach is geographically limited.

The federal government has aggressively defended its turf. Under the current administration, the CFTC has intervened in several states to protect its exclusive jurisdiction over derivatives markets, warning that state-level enforcement undermines a unified federal regulatory framework. CFTC Chairman Michael Selig, confirmed by the Senate in December 2025, has consistently argued that the agency is the sole arbiter of what constitutes a compliant futures or swaps contract on a Designated Contract Market, pushing back against what the commission characterizes as local encroachment.

Yet, as Kalshi pushes deeper into mainstream sports, the public backlash has grown. In 2026, Kalshi announced commercial partnerships with five Major League Baseball teams: the Atlanta Braves, Boston Red Sox, Los Angeles Dodgers, San Diego Padres, and San Francisco Giants. The move triggered a storm of skepticism on social media. On Reddit's r/sportsbook and r/politics, users quickly pointed out the hypocrisy of sports leagues embracing prediction markets while states sue to shut them down. "It's just sports betting with extra steps," one Reddit user commented, echoing a sentiment shared by thousands who see the platform’s derivatives branding as a convenient legal loophole. Another user on X.com noted that if the CFTC's preemption argument holds, it could create a massive loophole for unregulated gambling under the guise of financial swaps.

As the legal battleground shifts to the Second Circuit Court of Appeals and potentially the U.S. Supreme Court, the outcome will define the boundaries of federal derivatives regulation and state gambling authority for decades to come. With conflicting rulings between the Third Circuit and local district courts, the financial and gaming sectors are watching closely. The stakes are immense: either a new era of federally shielded, nationwide prediction markets, or a fragmented landscape where states retain absolute power over what happens within their borders.
