# **Off-Price Mirage: The Legal Windfall Masking Ross Stores’ Cyclical Peak**

When Ross Stores Inc. reported its second-quarter fiscal 2026 earnings on August 20, 2026, the retail market witnessed what initially appeared to be an unmitigated triumph. The Dublin, California-based off-price giant posted earnings per share of $2.66, vastly exceeding the consensus Wall Street estimate of $1.95 and its own internal guidance of $1.85 to $1.93. Almost immediately, shares of the retailer jumped 8% in post-market trading to settle near $239.04, reflecting a market eager for defensive consumer plays in an environment characterized by persistent macroeconomic anxiety. 

Yet, stripping away the regulatory and legal noise reveals a more complex financial narrative. The headline EPS beat was heavily distorted by a massive, one-time $253 million tariff refund recognized during the 13-week period ending August 1, 2026. This rebate, stemming from duties previously paid under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA), contributed approximately $0.60 per share to Ross’s quarterly earnings and was responsible for 405 basis points of the company's reported 610-basis-point operating margin expansion. 

Without this government windfall, Ross’s core operating margin expanded by 205 basis points. While this was still higher than the company's initial projection of 130 to 150 basis points, it raises fundamental questions for investors: Is this core margin expansion sustainable as the retail sector faces mounting inventory and freight pressures, or does it represent the absolute peak of the off-price retail cycle?

The underlying performance of the business was undeniably strong. Total sales rose 13% year-over-year to $6.26 billion, driven by a 10% surge in comparable-store sales. This traffic-led growth allowed Ross to significantly outperform its closest peers. During the same period, TJX Companies reported a consolidated comparable sales increase of 4% (with its core Marmaxx division lagging at just 1% due to execution missteps), while Target posted a 3.8% comp increase and Walmart delivered a muted 2.6% increase in U.S. comparable sales, its slowest rate in six years. 

According to Ross's Chief Executive Officer James Conroy, the blowout comps were driven by a robust trade-down effect as middle-income families sought relief from sticky inflation. "We are seeing broad-based strength across all demographic cohorts," Conroy noted during the earnings call, pointing to the company's decision to raise its full-year fiscal 2026 EPS guidance to a range of $8.61 to $8.77, up from its previous projection of $7.50 to $7.74. Ross also increased its new store opening target for the year to 115 locations, up from 110, after opening 35 Ross Dress for Less and 12 dd's DISCOUNTS stores in the second quarter. 

But on Fintwit and Reddit's r/stocks, a fierce debate is raging over whether this traffic is a temporary structural shift or a cyclical top. "Ross is eating everyone's lunch because middle-class shoppers are officially tapped out," wrote one widely-shared post on r/stocks. "They're trading down from Macy's and Kohl's to the off-price rack." 

Conversely, contrarian commentators on StockTwits are pointing to the stock's valuation. Trading at $239.04, Ross carries a trailing price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 28.9x, which is significantly above its five-year median. Bearish option volumes surged in the days leading up to the release, with some traders warning that the stock is "priced for perfection." Wells Fargo, which downgraded Ross to "Equal Weight" earlier in the summer, has highlighted that double-digit comps are historically unsustainable and set up exceptionally difficult year-over-year comparisons for 2027. While Truist Securities raised its price target to $310 and Citigroup maintained a Buy rating with a $290 target, UBS kept its Neutral stance with a $239 target, noting that the core operating margins, when adjusted for the tariff refund, may have peaked.

This margin expansion was analyzed in detail by Ross's Chief Financial Officer William W. Sheehan II. The 205-basis-point underlying improvement was driven by a 110-basis-point expansion in merchandise margin—buoyed by lower markdowns and strong pricing power—and 100 basis points of lower distribution costs, which benefitted from the timing of packaway inventory and productivity gains. Occupancy leveraged by 25 basis points due to the high sales volume. However, these gains were partially offset by a 10-basis-point increase in domestic freight costs due to rising fuel prices, and a 5-basis-point deleverage in buying costs. 

Crucially, the timing of packaway inventory (which fell to 36% of the mix from 38% in the prior-year period) helped lower immediate warehousing costs but indicates Ross is running with leaner reserves. Total inventories actually rose 18% during the quarter to support the traffic, suggesting that any sudden drop in consumer demand could lead to inventory bloat and aggressive promotional markdowns, erasing the core margin gains.

Beyond the operational debate lies the massive structural distortion of the tariff refund itself. The $253 million refund recognized by Ross is part of a broader $166 billion refund process administered by U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) through its Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) system. This historic payout follows the U.S. Supreme Court's landmark 6-3 decision on February 20, 2026, in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump*, 607 U.S. 229 (2026). The Court, led by Chief Justice John Roberts, ruled that the President lacked the constitutional authority under the IEEPA to unilaterally impose tariffs, which is a power reserved for Congress under Article I. Justices Sonia Sotomayor, Elena Kagan, Neil Gorsuch, Amy Coney Barrett, and Ketanji Brown Jackson joined the majority, while Justice Brett Kavanaugh penned a dissent, joined by Clarence Thomas and Samuel Alito. The tariffs were officially terminated on February 24, 2026.

Major corporations have reaped enormous windfalls from the ruling. Alongside Ross, Walmart reported receiving $2.9 billion in refunds in Q2, Target received $994 million, Home Depot took in $730 million, and TJX Companies secured $331 million. However, these windfalls have created a new legal headache. Over 100 consumer class action lawsuits, including the high-profile *Glase v. Walmart Inc.*, have been filed against these retailers. The plaintiffs argue that because the retailers passed the tariff costs on to consumers through higher shelf prices, retaining the government refunds constitutes "double recovery" and "unjust enrichment." 

While defense attorneys argue that only the "importer of record" has standing to recover customs duties and that the voluntary payment doctrine protects retail pricing, the litigation remains unresolved as of August 2026. If courts certify these classes, the retail industry could face substantial liabilities that would immediately wipe out the Q2 margin gains.

Furthermore, the macro environment remains highly volatile. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh are closely monitoring the inflationary impact of this massive corporate cash injection. To offset the revenue loss, the Trump administration has already utilized Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974 to implement a temporary 10% tariff. For discount retailers, who operate on razor-thin margins and rely heavily on cheap imported goods, this ongoing tariff tug-of-war makes long-term valuation models a moving target. If new trade restrictions are enacted, the off-price sector’s core margins will be the first to contract, turning today's record profits into tomorrow's margin squeeze.
