# The Purge of the EAC: How a Supreme Court Ruling Paralyzed America's Election Watchdog

When the emails landed in the inboxes of Thomas Hicks and Benjamin Hovland on the evening of Thursday, July 9, 2026, the message was brief, formal, and decisive. Signed by a White House personnel official, the messages informed the two Democratic commissioners of the U.S. Election Assistance Commission (EAC) that their services were terminated, effective immediately. Simultaneously, Christy McCormick, the agency’s sole remaining Republican commissioner, resigned at the request of the administration. Combined with the empty seat left by Republican Donald Palmer when he resigned on April 29, 2026, to join the Heritage Foundation, the removal of the commission's active leadership was total. In a single evening, the independent, bipartisan federal agency responsible for safeguarding the nation's voting infrastructure was left without a single sitting commissioner.

The immediate consequence of the removals is operational paralysis. Established by the Help America Vote Act (HAVA) of 2002 in the wake of the 2000 Florida recount, the EAC is legally structured to prevent partisan capture. By law, the commission has four members, appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate, with no more than two from the same political party. Crucially, the agency requires an affirmative vote from a quorum of at least three commissioners to take any official action, establish policy, or approve major guidelines. By clearing out all three active commissioners, the administration has legally paralyzed the commission's voting and policy-making authority just four months before the critical 2026 midterm elections.

The EAC is not a typical federal bureaucracy. While it does not run elections directly—a power reserved for state and local authorities—it serves as the central clearinghouse for the nation’s election infrastructure. It is responsible for certifying voting systems, distributing federal HAVA grants, and maintaining the national mail voter registration form. Without a quorum, the agency cannot formally update its Voluntary Voting System Guidelines (VVSG), approve new voting machines, or authorize new allocations of federal security grants. While the agency's career staff, led by Executive Director Brianna Schletz since March 2024, can manage day-to-day administrative functions, any policy decision or major certification is effectively frozen.

For state election directors, the loss of a quorum creates immediate, concrete vulnerabilities. Under HAVA, many states legally require their voting systems to be certified by the EAC. If a machine manufacturer releases a critical security patch or software update to address a newly discovered vulnerability, that update cannot be formally certified by the EAC without a commission vote. State officials are left with a difficult choice: run uncertified software, potentially violating state law, or run vulnerable systems, risking security breaches during the midterms.

Furthermore, the funding pipeline is blocked. In fiscal year 2024, Congress appropriated $55 million in HAVA Election Security Grants. That funding faced significant cuts in fiscal year 2025, and the President’s budget request for fiscal year 2026 proposed approximately $17 million for the EAC—a nearly 38% reduction from its previous $27 million operating budget level. Without commissioners to vote on distribution formulas and approve disbursements, any remaining federal security funds cannot be distributed to states struggling with rising administrative costs.

The White House quickly moved to defend the removals, framing them as a necessary alignment of executive branch agencies. In an official statement, the administration asserted that the President "reserves the right to remove individuals that may not be totally aligned with the important task of securing America’s elections and ensuring every legal vote is counted."

The legal engine behind this sweeping executive action is *Trump v. Slaughter* (No. 25-332), a landmark 6–3 Supreme Court decision handed down on June 29, 2026. Written by Chief Justice John Roberts and joined by the conservative majority, the ruling struck down the 91-year-old precedent established in *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States* (1935). For nearly a century, *Humphrey’s* had protected commissioners of independent agencies from being fired by the President without "cause"—defined as inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance. The case arose after President Trump fired Democratic FTC Commissioners Rebecca Kelly Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya early in his second term in 2025. Slaughter challenged her removal, but the High Court ruled that statutory "for-cause" removal protections for officials who exercise executive power violate the constitutional separation of powers. While the Court preserved limited removal protections for the Federal Reserve in a companion case, *Trump v. Cook*, it effectively dismantled the independent status of the rest of the federal administrative state.

Armed with the *Slaughter* ruling, the White House targeted the EAC. Tensions between the independent commission and the executive branch had been building since March 2025, when President Trump signed Executive Order 14248, titled "Preserving and Protecting the Integrity of American Elections." The order directed the EAC to modify the national mail voter registration form to require documentary proof of U.S. citizenship. It also instructed the agency to withhold federal HAVA funds from states that did not comply with specific, federally mandated ballot receipt deadlines.

However, the EAC's bipartisan structure resisted the mandate. In October 2025 and January 2026, federal judges issued permanent injunctions blocking the enforcement of the citizenship documentation requirements, ruling that the President lacked the unilateral authority to alter election procedures established by HAVA. Because the Democratic commissioners—and at times even Republican members—chose to adhere to the federal court rulings rather than the White House directives, the administration viewed the agency as a source of bureaucratic obstruction.

"The administration was frustrated by the EAC's refusal to bypass federal courts and unilaterally rewrite the national voter registration form," said a senior congressional staffer familiar with the discussions. "The *Slaughter* decision gave them the ultimate weapon to clear the deck."

The response from Democratic leaders was swift and fierce. Senate Minority Leader Chuck Schumer condemned the mass firing as a "brazen attempt to seize control of our elections before a single vote is cast." He accused the White House of "gutting the independent agency that certifies voting systems and helps election officials run secure elections," vowing that "Senate Democrats will fight this power grab at every turn." Senator Mark Warner of Virginia echoed the alarm, calling the purge an "extraordinary step" that "should concern every American, regardless of party." Warner added that the sudden removal of all commissioners "demands an immediate explanation" and raises profound concerns about direct political interference in election institutions.

The anxiety is felt deeply at the state level. Oregon Secretary of State Tobias Read, who serves on the EAC's Standards Board, issued a blistering statement. "It's dangerous, it's reckless, and frankly it's desperate," Read said. "The president is looking for any opportunity to seize control over the upcoming election because he is afraid of facing real accountability from voters."

On social media platforms, the firings sparked an immediate storm. In forums like Reddit's r/politics, the discussion focused heavily on whether the upcoming midterms could be thrown into chaos. Some users warned of a "soft coup," arguing that the vacancy would allow the administration to install handpicked loyalists who would refuse to certify election machines or force restrictive voter registration guidelines onto states without Senate confirmation. Others pointed out the immediate fiscal pressure the agency faces, noting that the administration is intentionally fostering a "resource gap" to weaken federal oversight.

Conversely, conservative commentators and legal organizations, including the Heritage Foundation and the Federalist Society, defended the terminations. They argued that the removals are a long-overdue application of the "unitary executive theory," which posits that the President must have absolute control over all executive branch agencies. From their perspective, the EAC had become an unaccountable body that shielded itself behind bipartisan protocols to obstruct the administration's stated mandate to secure the vote.

As the country moves closer to the November 2026 midterms, the EAC remains a hollowed-out shell. Brianna Schletz and the career staff are left to manage the day-to-day work, but the agency's primary engine is cold. The legal and political battle over who controls the machinery of American voting has entered uncharted territory, and the guardrails that have governed federal elections for a generation have just been dismantled.
