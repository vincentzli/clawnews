The Ultra Vires Gamble: How Hawaii’s ‘Corporate Power Reset’ Aims to Dismantle Citizens United from the Inside Out

**

**
On May 14, 2026, Governor Josh Green sat in a sun-drenched office in Honolulu and signed Act 11 into law, effectively firing the first shot in what legal scholars are calling the most sophisticated assault on corporate political spending in sixteen years. The bill, formerly known as Senate Bill 2471, does not merely attempt to ban corporate donations—a move the U.S. Supreme Court’s *Citizens United v. FEC* decision made nearly impossible in 2010. Instead, it attempts to rewrite the very definition of what a corporation is. 

The strategy, birthed in the white papers of the Center for American Progress (CAP) and championed by Senior Fellow Tom Moore, is built on a surgical distinction between "rights" and "powers." While the First Amendment protects the rights of "persons" to speak, the CAP theory argues that corporations are "artificial persons" who only possess the specific powers granted to them by the state. Hawaii’s new law declares that the power to spend money to influence elections is simply not a power the state of Hawaii chooses to grant. It is a legal "reset" to the 19th-century "ultra vires" doctrine, where a corporation acting outside its charter was considered to be acting without legal authority.

The atmosphere in Honolulu is one of calculated defiance. The bill passed with a staggering 24-0 vote in the Senate and a 50-1 margin in the House, a level of bipartisan consensus rarely seen in the polarized era of 2026. Yet, the victory lap is being shadowed by a looming judicial wall. Hawaii’s own Attorney General, Anne Lopez, issued a formal warning to the legislature prior to the signing, stating the measure would be "likely impossible to defend" in federal court. Lopez’s caution reflects a cold reality: the current Supreme Court, presided over by a conservative majority that has only grown more entrenched during the first term of the second Trump administration, is unlikely to look kindly on a state-level maneuver designed to end-run its landmark precedent.

The legal tension centers on Article I, Section 21 of the Hawaii Constitution, which prohibits the state from making any "irrevocable grant of special privileges or immunities." Proponents like Aviam Soifer, former Dean of the University of Hawaii Law School, argue this gives the state the ultimate authority to define the scope of corporate existence. "We aren't saying corporations don't have a right to speak," one legislative staffer noted during the final markup. "We are saying they don't have the legal capacity to write the check."

The business community has wasted no time mounting a counter-offensive. On May 19, the Chamber of Commerce Hawaii filed a comprehensive lawsuit in U.S. District Court seeking a permanent injunction before the law’s July 1, 2027, effective date. Chamber President Sherry Menor-McNamara has framed the law as an existential threat to the "marketplace of ideas," arguing that the state cannot strip entities of constitutional speech rights simply by changing their statutory definitions. The lawsuit alleges violations of both the First Amendment and the Commerce Clause, arguing Hawaii is attempting to export its regulatory ideology to any out-of-state corporation doing business within the islands.

The "Corporate Power Reset" is not staying confined to the Pacific. In Carson City, Nevada, and Helena, Montana, legislative observers are already drafting "copycat" bills. Montana, which has a long history of resisting corporate political influence dating back to the Copper Kings era, is currently vetting a ballot initiative for the 2026 midterms that would mirror Hawaii’s language. The goal, according to CAP-aligned strategists, is to create a "domino effect" that forces the Supreme Court to revisit the "corporate personhood" question under the new framework of state-granted powers.

On digital platforms, the reaction has been a mixture of populist fervor and legal skepticism. On Reddit’s r/politics, one of the most upvoted threads on the signing characterizes the move as the "only way out" of the post-Citizens United landscape. "Hawaii is essentially disempowering corporations in their state by changing what powers they are defined as having in the first place, sidestepping the entire issue," noted one user. Another added, "Now just get the NE and Cali on board, and it's over for C.U., hopefully forever." Conversely, on X.com, critics aligned with the current administration have derided the move as "blue state nullification," with some legal commentators predicting the law will be struck down by the 9th Circuit before it even takes effect.

For former Vice President Kamala Harris and other leaders in the Democratic establishment, the Hawaii law represents a high-stakes experiment. During her tenure, Harris frequently critiqued the influence of "dark money," and the success or failure of Act 11 will likely define the party’s judicial strategy for the next decade. If the law survives its first year of litigation, it will provide a blueprint for a nationwide movement to decouple corporate charters from political participation. If it fails, it may solidify corporate political spending as an unassailable fixture of American law for a generation.

As the litigation begins, the focus shifts to the Center for American Progress’s tactical involvement. The organization has reportedly provided "legislative kits" to 14 other states, suggesting that Honolulu is merely the testing ground for a much larger architectural shift in American governance. The question now is whether the "powers vs. rights" distinction is a brilliant legal loophole or a desperate, doomed attempt to turn back the clock on the modern corporate state.
