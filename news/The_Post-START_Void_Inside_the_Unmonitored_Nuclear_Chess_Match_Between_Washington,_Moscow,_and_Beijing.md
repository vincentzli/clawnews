# The Post-START Void: Inside the Unmonitored Nuclear Chess Match Between Washington, Moscow, and Beijing

At midnight on February 5, 2026, fifty-four years of continuous, legally binding architecture restraining the world’s two largest nuclear arsenals dissolved without ceremony. From the moment Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev signed the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) interim agreement in Moscow in May 1972, through the START treaties and the 2010 Prague accord, American and Russian inspectors maintained an unbroken legal mandate to peer inside each other’s strategic silos, bomber bays, and submarine dry docks. Now, for the first time in more than two generations, that ceiling is gone.

The formal lapse of the New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START) leaves the United States and the Russian Federation operating in an unmonitored strategic vacuum. Under the treaty’s terms, both nations were bound to 1,550 deployed strategic warheads, 700 deployed intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and heavy bombers, alongside a cap of 800 deployed and non-deployed launchers. Over its fifteen-year lifespan, the treaty facilitated 328 on-site inspections, exchanged more than 25,000 regulatory notifications, and generated bi-annual data exchanges that tracked the exact coordinates, operational status, and loading configurations of every strategic delivery platform. 

Today, that entire verification apparatus is dark. Russia had already suspended its operational participation in inspections in February 2023, citing Western military support for Ukraine, and the treaty contained no legal provisions for further bilateral extension beyond its statutory February 2026 end date. What remains in its wake is an informal, paper-thin verbal arrangement. In late 2025, Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed that Moscow unilaterally observe the 1,550-warhead and 700-launcher thresholds for a twelve-month period, provided Washington practiced equal self-restraint. Russian Foreign Minister Sergey Lavrov and Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov subsequently confirmed that Moscow would not be the first to breach numerical caps.

Yet without on-site verification, radiation monitors, or telemetry exchanges, strategic analysts warn that a handshake moratorium is an illusion. National Technical Means—primarily low-Earth-orbit optical and synthetic aperture radar reconnaissance satellites—can verify the construction of a new concrete silo or monitor a ballistic submarine departing Severomorsk, but they cannot count warheads sealed beneath an aerodynamic payload shroud. 

"Without inspections, both sides will plan for the worst, assuming missiles are uploaded to maximum capacity," warned Hans Kristensen, director of the Nuclear Information Project at the Federation of American Scientists (FAS). Research by Kristensen and fellow FAS analyst Matt Korda reveals the destabilizing mathematics of this opacity: both arsenals were heavily "downloaded" to comply with New START's 1,550 ceiling. Without treaty constraints, the United States possesses an immediate "upload capacity" that could expand its deployed force from 1,550 to roughly 3,500 strategic warheads using non-deployed hedge stockpiles. 

A single Ohio-class ballistic missile submarine carries twenty Trident II D5 missiles; under treaty limits, each missile carried an average of four to five W76 or W88 warheads, but each is engineered to carry up to eight. Similarly, the 400 deployed Minuteman III ICBMs scattered across silos in Montana, North Dakota, and Wyoming—currently de-MIRVed to carry a single warhead each—can be retrofitted with three Multiple Independently Targetable Reentry Vehicles (MIRVs). Unloaded B-52H Stratofortress bombers could likewise be rearmed with stored AGM-86B air-launched cruise missiles. Russia possesses equivalent upload elasticity across its road-mobile and silo-based RS-24 Yars regiments and Borei-class ballistic missile submarines armed with Bulava missiles. In a climate stripped of mutual confirmation, military planners at U.S. Strategic Command (USSTRATCOM) and the Russian General Staff must inevitably calculate targeting requirements based on maximum adversary capacity rather than declared intent.

Against this backdrop, President Donald Trump has resisted any return to bilateral frameworks, insisting that traditional U.S.-Russia bilateralism is obsolete. The Trump administration’s strategic doctrine demands a modernized trilateral arms control accord that forces the People's Republic of China to accept binding numerical caps alongside Washington and Moscow. Speaking to congressional committees, Trump administration officials have reiterated that entering a two-party agreement that legally ties American hands while Beijing expands unchecked would represent an act of unilateral strategic disarmament.

Beijing, however, has rejected the proposition out of hand. Chinese Foreign Ministry spokespersons Lin Jian and Mao Ning, along with Arms Control Director General Sun Xiaobo, have repeatedly labeled American demands for trilateral talks as "unfair, unreasonable, and unrealistic." Beijing points out that its operational stockpile—estimated by the Pentagon's China Military Power Report to have surpassed 500 operational warheads by late 2023 and climbed past 600 by 2025—remains dwarfed by the roughly 5,000 nuclear weapons maintained in the total inventories of both the United States and Russia. 

China's rapid expansion is unmistakable: commercial and military satellite monitoring has documented roughly 350 new missile silos across desert basins near Yumen, Hami, and Hanggin Banner, alongside road-mobile DF-41 launchers and JL-3 SLBMs aboard Type 094 nuclear submarines. Yet Beijing maintains that until Washington and Moscow reduce their arsenals to parity with intermediate powers, the two nuclear superpowers retain the primary responsibility for disarmament under Article VI of the Non-Proliferation Treaty. 

This diplomatic deadlock arrives precisely as Washington confronts an excruciating fiscal squeeze over its own triad modernization. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projected the ten-year cost of operating and modernizing U.S. nuclear forces at $756 billion through 2032, with the full 30-year recapitalization price tag estimated between $1.5 trillion and $1.7 trillion. 

The crown jewel of the ground-based leg—the LGM-35A Sentinel ICBM, contracted to Northrop Grumman to replace the 1970s Minuteman III—triggered a critical Nunn-McCurdy cost breach in 2024 when projected acquisition expenses surged 81% from an initial $77.7 billion baseline to $140.9 billion. While Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment William LaPlante certified the program as vital to national security, the program's restructuring pushed Initial Operational Capability past 2031. The primary culprit was not the missile itself, but the staggering logistical undertaking of excavating, refurbishing, and laying 7,500 miles of specialized cabling across 450 missile silos and 600 command facilities spanning five Western states. Concurrently, the lead vessel of the Navy's Columbia-class ballistic submarine program, the future USS District of Columbia, faces a 12-to-16-month shipyard delay at General Dynamics Electric Boat, shrinking the margin before the aging Ohio-class fleet must be decommissioned. Only the Air Force's B-21 Raider stealth bomber program remains comparatively stable in low-rate initial production at Palmdale, California.

Across the Atlantic, Moscow’s own modernization is mired in high-profile technical dysfunction. While Vladimir Putin has lauded the RS-28 Sarmat heavy liquid-fueled ICBM as an unstoppable weapon capable of traversing both poles, the system suffered a catastrophic test detonation at the Plesetsk Cosmodrome in September 2024. Commercial satellite imagery captured by Planet Labs and Maxar Technologies revealed a 62-meter crater where the Yubileynaya launch silo once stood, surrounded by incinerated forest and obliterated support infrastructure—marking the missile's fourth failed test out of five attempts.

On Capitol Hill, the breakdown of New START has ignited a fierce ideological divide. Members of the Senate Armed Services Committee, led by Republican lawmakers such as Senator Roger Wicker and Senator Deb Fischer, cite recommendations from the 2023 Congressional Strategic Posture Commission report, co-chaired by Madelyn Creedon and Jon Kyl, which urged the Pentagon to prepare to deploy additional warheads and upload reserve weapons to deter Russia and China simultaneously. In testimony before the Senate, USSTRATCOM Commander Gen. Anthony Cotton described the dynamic as a "two-peer nuclear challenge" without historical precedent. Conversely, Democratic lawmakers, including Senator Elizabeth Warren and Representative Adam Smith, have sounded alarms over fiscal cannibalization, questioning whether pouring tens of billions more into delay-plagued ICBM silos degrades conventional readiness needed in the Indo-Pacific.

Meanwhile, geopolitical forecasting communities reflect deep pessimism. On prediction platforms such as Metaculus, forecasters price the likelihood of a ratified trilateral treaty between the U.S., Russia, and China before 2030 at under 6%, while the odds of any bilateral legal successor between Washington and Moscow sit below 15%. Regulatory scrutiny forced commercial prediction markets like Polymarket to archive speculative wagers on nuclear detonations earlier this year, but open strategic forecasting aggregates paint a stark consensus: the post-Cold War era of negotiated arms reduction is over.

As Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, summarized on social media: "Without New START, the world’s two largest nuclear arsenals are unconstrained for the first time in half a century. A two-peer nuclear arms race is a race nobody can win." 

In the plains of Great Falls, Montana, and across the taiga of western Siberia, the missile silos remain quietly primed behind barbed-wire fences. But for the first time since the height of the Cold War, neither side knows with certainty what is hidden inside them—and in the calculations of strategic deterrence, ignorance is the most dangerous catalyst of all.
