# The Palisades Firestorm and the Battle for City Hall: How a Wildcard Insurgency Reshaped the Los Angeles Mayoral Runoff

The Palisades wildfire of January 2025 did more than char the dry hillsides of West Los Angeles; it reshaped the city's political landscape. As Los Angeles barrels toward a highly competitive mayoral runoff on November 3, 2026, the aftermath of that disaster has created an unprecedented nexus of litigation and political rivalry. The race has narrowed to a choice between incumbent Mayor Karen Bass and progressive challenger City Councilmember Nithya Raman, but the fallout from the primary’s wildcard candidate, reality television personality Spencer Pratt, remains a central factor in the campaign. 

In the nonpartisan primary held on June 2, 2026, Bass secured first place with 34.3% of the vote, while Raman, who represents the 4th City Council District, finished second with 29.0%. Pratt, running as a Republican with the backing of President Donald Trump, garnered 25.5% before being eliminated. Because no candidate secured a majority, the top two advanced. The primary results revealed a deeply fractured electorate, with Pratt capturing a massive block of moderate, independent, and conservative voters who were united by dissatisfaction with the city’s handling of infrastructure, public safety, and homelessness.

The defining undercurrent of this election is a massive class-action lawsuit against the city of Los Angeles and the Department of Water and Power (DWP) over the Palisades wildfire. Among the thousands of plaintiffs seeking damages for property loss and emotional distress is Kenneth Bass, the brother of Mayor Karen Bass, alongside his wife, Cindy, who lost their Malibu home in the fire. In an unusual political dynamic, Pratt, who also lost his Pacific Palisades home in the fire and sued the city in January 2025, teamed up with the mayor’s brother in the ongoing litigation. The plaintiffs allege systemic negligence, pointing to evidence that the Santa Ynez Reservoir was empty during the wildfire, causing critical water pressure failures that hampered firefighting efforts. Although a spokesperson for Mayor Bass dismissed the litigation as nothing new due to the size of the plaintiff pool, the optics of the mayor’s own brother suing her administration alongside her most vocal political opponent has provided ammunition to critics.

The primary’s conclusion did not bring calm to City Hall. Due to California’s mail-in ballot processing laws, the vote count dragged on for nearly a week. Pratt initially held a narrow lead on election night, but as late-arriving mail-in ballots were tabulated, Raman surged ahead, leading the Associated Press to project her second-place finish on June 8, 2026. On June 12, Pratt released a video titled "Saving LA – Phase III," in which he conceded the election but declared "war" on the city's political establishment. Now free from the constraints of campaign finance laws, Pratt promised to continue his mission to expose the city's leadership. In the video, he referred to Bass and Raman as "dumb and dumber" and "corrupt communists," while labeling the runoff "the machine protecting the machine." Pratt also claimed to possess incriminating recordings of one of the candidates that would force her to "resign in shame," though he has not yet released the tapes. The tension escalated further in mid-June when a fire broke out at Pratt's Pacific Palisades office building. Pratt publically labeled the incident "very suspicious" and suggested it was political retaliation, though fire officials stated the cause remains new and under investigation and the building superintendent expressed skepticism.

The runoff has now set up a stark ideological referendum on the city's fiscal management and its response to homelessness. Mayor Bass, seeking a second term, has built her platform around her signature "Inside Safe" initiative. Launched in December 2022, the program has spent over $300 million to clear street encampments and transition individuals to temporary housing, primarily leased motels. Bass argues that street homelessness has decreased by approximately 18% during her administration, and she has secured crucial establishment support, including endorsements from California Governor Gavin Newsom and former Vice President Kamala Harris.

Raman, running as an insurgent progressive, has mounted a sharp critique of Inside Safe’s efficacy and fiscal sustainability. She has characterized the program as a "very, very expensive intervention," pointing out that renting motel rooms costs the city upwards of $80,000 per person annually—funds she argues would be better spent on rental vouchers and permanent supportive housing. To back her critique, Raman points to data showing that approximately 40% of Inside Safe participants have returned to unsheltered homelessness. She has proposed an ambitious goal of reducing homelessness by 50% ahead of the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics by shifting the city’s resources toward rental vouchers, shared housing models, and street medicine teams. Raman has pointed to her own council district, where she claims an engagement-led strategy achieved a 54% reduction in encampments over a three-year period.

The battle for the mayor’s office will now be decided by who can win over the 25.5% of voters who supported Pratt. These voters, representing a mix of Westside homeowners angry over wildfire response and moderate independents frustrated by the slow pace of housing development, hold the key to the election. As November 3 approaches, the campaigns of both Bass and Raman are actively courting this wildcard constituency, turning the runoff into a high-stakes debate over whether Los Angeles needs a steady hand to manage its existing programs or a radical shift in how it builds housing and manages its bureaucracy.
