# The Liquid Death of a $130 Myth: Inside the Proven Industries Legal Collapse

In the world of physical security, the distance between a "virtually indestructible" $130 trailer hitch lock and a total corporate meltdown is exactly the width of a 12-ounce aluminum can. By May 2026, the legal saga of Proven Industries versus Trevor McNally has been enshrined in marketing textbooks and law school curricula as the ultimate cautionary tale of the digital age. It was a conflict that began with a sledgehammer and ended with an admission under oath that redefined the limits of corporate litigation in the face of the "Streisand Effect."

The genesis of the crisis dates back to late 2024, when Proven Industries, a Florida-based manufacturer of high-end security hardware, launched an aggressive marketing campaign. Their promotional materials featured the company’s signature hitch lock surviving brutal sledgehammer strikes, accompanied by a defiant challenge to online skeptics. The message was clear: their product was the final word in trailer security. However, the internet’s collective gaze soon turned toward Trevor McNally, a former Marine and lock-sport YouTuber known for his "McNallyOfficial" channel, where he habitually bypassed "high-security" locks in seconds, often using little more than household items.

In early 2025, McNally accepted the challenge. In a video that eventually garnered over 181,000 upvotes on the r/nextfuckinglevel subreddit—triggering a tidal wave of consumer skepticism—McNally demonstrated a devastatingly simple vulnerability. Casually sipping from a juice box and swinging his legs in a manner that would later become a focal point of federal litigation, McNally cut a thin shim from an aluminum Liquid Death water can. Within seconds, he slid the makeshift tool into the lock’s mechanism, bypassing the $130 hardware with a metallic click that echoed across the industry.

Rather than addressing the technical flaw, Proven Industries chose the path of maximum resistance. In May 2025, the company filed an eight-count federal lawsuit in the Middle District of Florida. The complaint was an extraordinary display of legal aggression, alleging copyright infringement, false advertising, and trade libel. Most remarkably, the lawsuit targeted McNally’s presentation style. Proven’s legal team argued that McNally’s "childlike leg-swinging" and the presence of a juice box were deliberate attempts to make the bypass look easier than it was, creating a "false and misleading impression" that their premium security product was "child's play."

The escalation was swift and personal. Proven Industries’ owner, Ron Lee, reportedly initiated a series of DMCA takedowns against McNally’s content. More troubling were reports that Lee had sent cryptic messages to McNally and accidentally sent a text meant for a third party to McNally’s wife—an incident the YouTuber characterized as a clumsy attempt at intimidation. 

McNally’s response was a masterstroke of transparency. He filmed himself retrieving a brand-new, factory-sealed Proven lock from a secure Amazon locker and shimming it open in one continuous, unedited shot. This "Amazon locker video" effectively neutralized the company's claim that he was using "tampered" or "altered" locks.

The legal battle reached its tipping point in June 2025 during a preliminary injunction hearing before Judge Mary Scriven. The proceedings took a turn for the surreal when a Proven Industries engineer, under cross-examination, admitted under oath that after watching McNally’s video, he had been able to replicate the shim bypass himself on a company lock. The engineer’s admission—that a layman could bypass their "indestructible" lock using a piece of trash—gutted the company’s claim of false advertising.

By July 2025, facing a massive public backlash and a judge skeptical of their fair-use arguments, Proven Industries voluntarily dismissed the lawsuit. An attempt to seal the court records to prevent further public embarrassment was denied.

Today, in May 2026, the fallout remains visible. As the United States navigates the second year of the second Trump administration, the Proven Industries case has become a symbol of a broader movement toward consumer transparency. The lock industry has seen a pivot away from "invincibility" marketing toward more nuanced security ratings. Proven Industries, once a dominant name in the niche, saw its market share crater as "Liquid Death" became a punchline among trailer owners. 

"They tried to sue a man for being right," noted one prominent Reddit commenter during the height of the 2025 backlash. "And in the process, they spent thousands of dollars to prove to everyone that their $130 lock is basically an expensive paperweight." The case stands as a stark reminder: in the age of viral verification, the gavel is no match for a well-placed shim and a juice box.
