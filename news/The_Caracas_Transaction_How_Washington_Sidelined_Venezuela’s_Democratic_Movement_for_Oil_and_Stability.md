# The Caracas Transaction: How Washington Sidelined Venezuela’s Democratic Movement for Oil and Stability

On the humid evening of June 28, 2026, the gray hull of the USS *Fort Lauderdale* (LPD 28) glided into the port of La Guaira, marking the first time a U.S. warship had docked in Venezuelan waters in decades. Onboard were elements of a U.S. humanitarian deployment, authorized by President Donald Trump, which sent approximately 2,000 U.S. military personnel, alongside the USS *Billings* (LCS 15), C-17 and C-130 cargo planes, MV-22 Ospreys, and CH-47 Chinooks, to assist in search-and-rescue operations. Just four days earlier, on June 24, 2026, northern Venezuela had been devastated by a catastrophic seismic doublet—a magnitude 7.2 foreshock followed 40 seconds later by a magnitude 7.5 mainshock—which killed over 6,100 people and left 24,000 homes completely uninhabitable. Yet, beneath the veneer of disaster relief, the military presence underscored a stark geopolitical reality: the United States was now actively collaborating with the regime it had spent years trying to overthrow.

This unexpected alignment is the direct consequence of "Operation Absolute Resolve." On January 3, 2026, U.S. special operations forces launched a surgical strike that captured President Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, whisking them to the United States to face federal narcoterrorism charges. In the power vacuum, the country’s Supreme Court quickly swore in Delcy Rodríguez, the former Executive Vice President, as Venezuela's acting president. 

Rodríguez’s administration has operated under significant U.S. influence, but Washington’s strategy has pivoted from promoting an immediate democratic overhaul to securing transactional stability. The Trump administration has prioritized stabilizing Venezuela’s oil sector to curb global supply shocks. On January 9, 2026, the U.S. enacted Executive Order 14373, establishing the "Foreign Government Deposit Funds" (FGDF) to hold all proceeds from Venezuelan crude sales in U.S. custodial accounts, shielding them from commercial creditors while using them to cover the costs of the U.S. military intervention and reconstruction. 

Under Washington's supervision, Rodríguez has executed corporate-friendly energy reforms, reducing royalties and easing fiscal terms to boost production to 1.2 million barrels per day. The U.S. Treasury subsequently removed Rodríguez from its sanctions list, signaling a normalization that has sidelined the country's traditional democratic leaders. Edmundo González Urrutia, the 76-year-old diplomat widely recognized as the winner of the 2024 election, remains in exile in Spain, calling for free elections that critics argue Washington seems increasingly uninterested in enforcing. Similarly, María Corina Machado, the opposition's most popular figure, has been written out of the transition process entirely.

Instead of Machado, the U.S. has thrown its weight behind Dinorah Figuera, head of the 2015 National Assembly. On August 12, 2026, Figuera’s delegation concluded a first round of talks in Caracas with Jorge Rodríguez, president of the National Assembly and brother of the acting president. The two sides agreed on minor judicial reforms for appointing Supreme Court justices and pledged to coordinate efforts to retrieve 31 tons of gold frozen at the Bank of England to fund earthquake recovery. 

Sidelined and in exile, Machado has attempted to force her way back into the diplomatic conversation. In mid-August 2026, FARA filings revealed that Machado retained veteran Republican lobbyist Ed Rogers and the Washington-based BGR Group to lobby Congress and the White House. The move has drawn sharp criticism from analysts. "She wanted a lobbyist because she's been essentially written out of the script of U.S. policy in Venezuela," observed Miguel Tinker Salas, a professor of Venezuelan history at Pomona College and non-resident fellow at the Quincy Institute. "I think it's an act of desperation. What she wants from Trump is to become a player again in Venezuelan politics, to reassert herself as the dominant opposition figure."

Machado's lobbying push has triggered a fierce backlash online, particularly on Reddit, where critics and supporters are locked in a polarized debate. On subreddits like r/geopolitics and r/neoliberal, critics accuse Machado of selling out Venezuela’s sovereignty. "Machado is basically offering U.S. multinational corporations a fire sale on future state assets and PDVSA oil reserves just to buy her way back into Washington's good graces," wrote one user. Others criticized her ties to BGR Group, which has historically represented oil giants like BP and Valero, arguing that her privatization agenda would turn the country into a corporate client state. 

Conversely, supporters of the democratic opposition have used online platforms to vent their frustration at Washington's pragmatism. "The U.S. is prioritizing cheap oil and stable production under a Chavista leftover like Delcy Rodríguez over actual democratic reforms," argued a popular post. "They used Maduro’s capture to install a compliant regime and lock up the oil revenues, leaving the democratic mandate of González and Machado in the dust."

The tension between stability and democracy is further complicated by the domestic environment. In mid-August, Delcy Rodríguez announced the release of 130 political prisoners under a U.S.-backed "Program for Peace and Democratic Coexistence." U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio praised the release as a "crucial step" toward national reconciliation. However, local human rights group Foro Penal quickly disputed the numbers, verifying only 72 to 78 actual releases and noting that hundreds of political prisoners remain behind bars under a "revolving door" system of political detention. As U.S. cargo planes continue to land at Simón Bolívar International Airport and U.S. refiners welcome tankers of Venezuelan crude, the prospects for a genuine democratic transition are being steadily replaced by a managed, oil-rich peace.
