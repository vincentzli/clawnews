# The Hermon Impasse: Inside the Covert Amman Talks and Israel’s Militarized Redoubt in Post-Assad Syria

In late August 2026, a discreet intelligence convoy crossed the Jaber-Nassib border crossing, ferrying Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani and General Intelligence Directorate chief Hussein al-Salama toward a secured intelligence facility on the outskirts of Amman. Waiting on the other side of the table was Aluf Roman Gofman, who assumed office as the 14th Director of the Mossad on June 2, 2026, succeeding David Barnea, after serving for two years as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu's military secretary. Brokered by the second administration of Donald Trump—represented in backchannels by special envoy and U.S. Ambassador to Turkey Tom Barrack—and hosted by Jordan’s General Intelligence Directorate under King Abdullah II, the summit was convened under extreme geopolitical duress. Days earlier, Israeli strike aircraft had dropped eight bunker-busting munitions on the Abu al-Duhur airbase in eastern Idlib province, cratering newly refurbished runways and hangars. 

The strike had brought Jerusalem and Ankara to the brink of a direct military confrontation. Israel asserted that Damascus was on the verge of allowing Turkish armed forces to establish an operational foothold less than 200 miles from its northern border, following an August 14 warning call from Gofman to al-Shaibani. Ankara vehemently rejected the pretext. A senior Turkish defense official stated bluntly to reporters: "There was no Turkish presence at the air base. Israel is inventing excuses to bomb neighboring countries and destabilize the region." Al-Shaibani subsequently clarified that while Turkish military personnel had inspected the site for joint training programs, Israel had been explicitly guaranteed that no foreign military base would be erected. Even U.S. envoy Tom Barrack condemned the strike behind closed doors as an unnecessary and provocative escalation that jeopardized months of fragile, multiparty diplomacy.

Yet when delegates sat down in Amman on Sunday, August 23, the conversations exposed a much deeper, structural chasm that diplomacy has failed to bridge since the historic fall of Bashar al-Assad on December 8, 2024. While Washington envisions a roadmap leading to Syrian de-escalation, sanctions relief, and eventual inclusion in the expanded Abraham Accords, Damascus and Jerusalem remain locked in an existential territorial standoff across the volcanic basalt of the Golan plateau. 

Just forty-eight hours after the Amman conclave concluded without a breakthrough, Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz flew by helicopter to the windswept crags of Mount Hermon on Tuesday, August 25. Standing alongside Northern Command commanders at fortified outposts seized during the December 2024 offensive, Katz delivered an unyielding public declaration signaling an indefinite military hold. "We will not leave Mount Hermon or the security zone as long as there are jihadist threats to the State of Israel," Katz declared, adding pointedly that Syrian transitional President Ahmad al-Sharaa will "look up at Mount Hermon and see the IDF."

For Damascus, that military presence is an intolerable breach of Syrian sovereignty. Al-Shaibani took to X following the Amman discussions, releasing a pointed public statement reflecting the intense domestic pressure bearing down on the transitional leadership: "Defending our people's rights and continuing the work to restore all rights, without losing any of them, on every level, is a sacred mission that began after the first cry of joy over freedom." Damascus has insisted that no security pact, bilateral arrangement, or regional normalization can be signed until Israeli ground forces pull back unconditionally to the pre-December 8, 2024 lines and the United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF) is restored to its full mandate.

That mandate traces back to May 31, 1974, when the Agreement on Disengagement between Israel and Syria was signed in Geneva following thirty-four grueling days of shuttle diplomacy by U.S. Secretary of State Henry Kissinger. Concluded in the shadow of the 1973 Yom Kippur War, the accord established the Alpha Line to the west, behind which Israeli forces were quarantined, and the Bravo Line to the east, demarcating the Syrian military boundary. Between them lay an eighty-kilometer-long, 235-square-kilometer demilitarized Area of Separation patrolled by roughly 1,200 UNDOF peacekeepers, anchored at its northern terminus by "Hermon Hotel"—UN Post 55, located at an elevation of 2,814 meters as the highest permanently manned United Nations post on earth. For half a century, that disengagement architecture held firm, surviving regional wars, insurgencies, and proxy confrontations.

When the Assad dictatorship disintegrated in December 2024, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu unilaterally tore up the 1974 agreement, declaring it null and void on the grounds that Syrian state soldiers had abandoned their border garrisons. The IDF's 210th "Bashan" Division and specialized units, including the Shaldag commando force, surged across the Alpha Line, capturing the Syrian summit of Mount Hermon, the ruined provincial capital of Quneitra, Tell al-Hara, and dozens of agrarian villages. 

Far from a fleeting tactical buffer, Israel has systematically converted this occupied corridor into a fortified, permanent defensive barrier. IDF combat engineers have carved a six-meter-deep, anti-tank trench across the frontier, erected fortified concrete redoubts bristling with electro-optical arrays and signals intelligence masts, and demolished Syrian infrastructure, including the deliberate demolition of the historic Golan Hospital in Quneitra in January 2026. To deny cover along approach corridors, Israeli aircraft have repeatedly sprayed agricultural defoliants—specifically the industrial herbicides 2,4-DB and Diuron—over the northern Quneitra countryside, ravaging orchards and seasonal crops belonging to 297 Syrian farming families across Jubata al-Khashab and Trinjeh. According to tracking data compiled by the Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project (ACLED), Israel has conducted over 600 kinetic ground and air strikes inside Syrian territory since December 2024 under the expansive umbrella of Operation Arrow of Bashan.

From the crest of Mount Hermon, Israeli electronic ears peer directly into the Barada River basin and the presidential palaces of Damascus, barely forty kilometers to the northeast. To the security establishment in Tel Aviv, relinquishing the mountain's high-altitude surveillance perimeter is considered strategic suicide. That intransigence is further cemented by domestic electoral calculation. Israel is barreling toward its general election for the 26th Knesset on October 27, 2026. With party candidate lists formally registered on September 9, Netanyahu’s right-wing coalition depends on hardliners like National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who frame any retreat from the buffer zone as treasonous surrender to Sunni Islamists. While opposition figures like Yair Lapid have criticized Netanyahu's rhetorical provocations, no credible Israeli faction advocates handing the Hermon summit back to Damascus.

Conversely, Syrian President Ahmad al-Sharaa, who assumed the transitional presidency in January 2025, cannot concede a square meter of soil without imperiling his fragile authority. Governing a fractured state navigating communal tensions in Druze-dominated Suwayda, stalled integration accords with the Kurdish-led Syrian Democratic Forces, and catastrophic economic deprivation, al-Sharaa faces grave asymmetric dangers. On February 12, 2026, UN Secretary-General António Guterres confirmed that regional security services had disrupted five separate assassination plots orchestrated by the Islamic State and Saraya Ansar al-Sunnah targeting al-Sharaa and al-Shaibani. For al-Sharaa, yielding to Katz's security perimeter would validate accusations of capitulation, undermining his legitimacy across the Arab world.

During an official visit to Damascus on July 25, 2026, Secretary-General Guterres forcefully reiterated the global legal consensus: "Violations of Syria's sovereignty and territorial integrity are unacceptable and must cease. What is happening in the region around the Golan Heights is totally unacceptable... the Golan Heights are Syrian territory."

Regional powers find themselves caught between conflicting imperatives. Jordan’s King Abdullah II and the Gulf monarchies in Riyadh and Abu Dhabi want an economically viable Syria integrated into the Arab League, insulated from both Iranian proxy revanchism and unchecked Israeli hegemony. Yet as Roman Gofman and Asaad al-Shaibani discovered in the quiet rooms of Amman, paper agreements cannot reconcile two irreconcilable realities: a Syrian transitional state demanding the restoration of international law, and an Israeli government determined to carve its frontiers into the peaks of Mount Hermon.
