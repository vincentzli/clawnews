# Inside Washington’s Venezuelan Mandate: How Oil Cash, Parallel Diplomacy, and Disaster Reconstruction Are Reshaping Post-Maduro Power

Six months after U.S. special operations forces executed "Operation Absolute Resolve" in the pre-dawn hours of January 3, 2026—apprehending Nicolás Maduro and his wife Cilia Flores from their Caracas compound—the expected American boots on the ground never materialized. Instead, power over South America’s largest oil reserves has consolidated into a subterranean financial and diplomatic framework managed directly from Foggy Bottom and the U.S. Department of the Treasury.

Under the direction of U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Washington has established a hands-on mechanism of conditional liquidity that dictates the day-to-day operations of acting President Delcy Rodríguez. Rather than dismantling the existing state apparatus, the Trump administration chose to engage directly with Rodríguez and key regime officials to prevent civil breakdown and maintain security. Through a ring-fenced mechanism, proceeds from Venezuelan energy exports flow directly into U.S. Treasury-controlled accounts, which are then released to Caracas under strict General Licenses tied to specific operational benchmarks. By early July 2026, this leverage culminated in a landmark reform of Venezuela’s hydrocarbon laws, stripping state oil enterprise PDVSA of its historic monopoly and opening downstream refining, distribution, and marketing to foreign private capital.

This pragmatic realpolitik, however, has triggered intense political friction within Venezuela’s opposition movement. On June 18, 2026, Dinorah Figuera, leader of the 2015–2020 National Assembly, returned to Caracas from exile to establish a formal rapprochement with National Assembly President Jorge Rodríguez. The two sides announced that starting August 1, 2026, twin 10-member delegations will begin formal transition negotiations aimed at appointing new Supreme Court justices, restructuring the National Electoral Council (CNE), and setting a binding election roadmap.

Yet, this U.S.-backed negotiation track conspicuously excludes María Corina Machado, the popular opposition leader. U.S. strategic planners privately expressed concern that placing Machado at the center of the immediate transition could ignite armed resistance within Venezuela’s military officer corps and security apparatus. While Machado’s supporters criticize the arrangement as a betrayal of democratic legitimacy, Washington officials have favored Figuera’s institutional, technocratic framework as the safest path toward state stability.

Online debates reflect this deep tactical divide. On X and political forums such as r/politics, critics argue that Washington is prioritizing oil access over democratic principles, with one observer noting that "bypassing Machado to keep Delcy Rodríguez in place is pure transactional realpolitik disguised as stabilization." Conversely, market analysts on r/wallstreetbets point to the rapid restructuring of PDVSA debts and general license expansions, arguing that "securing oil flows and treasury accounts is the only pragmatic way to prevent total state collapse."

The delicate political balance was severely tested on June 24, 2026, when twin earthquakes struck central Venezuela, resulting in nearly 5,000 deaths and destroying vital power grids, roads, and water systems. The catastrophic seismic event turned political restructuring into an urgent crisis of humanitarian survival. International aid agencies struggled to deploy resources across damaged supply corridors, forcing both Jorge Rodríguez and Dinorah Figuera to expand their August 1 agenda to include joint disaster relief and emergency infrastructure restoration.

As the August 1 deadline approaches, Venezuela’s post-Maduro order remains a high-stakes experiment in international oversight. Between Marco Rubio’s financial levers in Washington, Delcy Rodríguez’s administrative management in Caracas, and Dinorah Figuera’s technocratic dialogue, the country's immediate future hinges on whether this fragile framework can deliver both economic recovery and genuine democratic transition.
