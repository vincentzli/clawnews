# Partition by Earthen Berms: How the U.S.-Brokered Gaza Peace Plan Died on the 'Yellow Line'

Along a 23-kilometer (14-mile) stretch of upturned soil, the physical reality of a partitioned Gaza Strip is hardening into place. Since early May, military engineering units of the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) have worked continuously, deploying heavy bulldozers to construct a massive system of earthen berms, deep trenches, and fortified checkpoints. This structure follows what military maps call the "Yellow Line"—a ceasefire demarcation zone originally established as a temporary buffer under United Nations Security Council Resolution 2803. But as the summer of 2026 progresses, the temporary line is taking on an air of permanence. IDF forces have expanded their direct administrative and security footprint to cover approximately 70 percent of the Gaza Strip's territory, confining the enclave's remaining civilian population to shrinking pockets in the south.

This territorial transformation marks a critical breakdown of the ambitious peace plan negotiated in October 2025. Endorsed under UNSC Resolution 2803, the U.S.-brokered agreement was championed by President Donald Trump to transition Gaza from active conflict to technocratic self-governance. The initial ceasefire held through the winter, but the transition to Phase 2 of the agreement has completely stalled by July 2026. The core of the diplomatic deadlock lies in a familiar, intractable debate: the sequencing of demilitarization versus Israeli withdrawal.

On July 6, 2026, Hamas attempted to comply with the governance transition requirements and force a diplomatic breakthrough. The group announced the dissolution of its Government Emergency Committee in Gaza and declared it was transferring all local administrative, municipal, and civil authority to the newly formed National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG). The NCAG is a body of Palestinian technocrats led by Chief Commissioner Ali Shaath, a civil engineer. The move was presented by regional mediators as a step toward meeting international demands for a non-partisan, administrative administration.

However, the transition was instantly blocked at the border. Israeli Foreign Minister Gideon Saar ordered a blockade on the entry of NCAG officials, who remain based in Cairo. Saar dismissed the governance transition as a cosmetic trick designed to evade the non-negotiable requirement of disarming the militant group. Saar argued that as long as Hamas retains its weapons, any technocratic administration would merely act under its directives, comparing the arrangement to political models seen elsewhere in the region where armed groups operate parallel to formal state institutions.

The international oversight body tasked with managing this transition—the Board of Peace (BoP)—has been unable to resolve the impasse. Directed by High Representative Nickolay Mladenov under an initiative chaired by President Trump, the BoP was intended to serve as the transitional administration for the enclave, coordinating reconstruction funds and overseeing the deployment of the International Stabilization Force (ISF). Under the terms of the peace plan, the ISF is to be comprised of troops from Morocco, Kosovo, Albania, and Kazakhstan. While Morocco signed a formal legal agreement in July 2026 to contribute military and police personnel alongside a field hospital, the broader deployment of the multinational force has stalled. Without a consensus on disarmament, Mladenov’s forces have been unable to initiate their full deployment or halt the construction of the IDF's Yellow Line barrier.

Faced with this geopolitical gridlock, the BoP has retreated to a limited pilot project in southern Gaza near Rafah. This localized initiative involves creating a temporary settlement with portable housing, overseen by the NCAG and secured by a small detachment of Moroccan personnel. But the pilot project does little to address the wider humanitarian crisis engulfing the rest of the Strip.
