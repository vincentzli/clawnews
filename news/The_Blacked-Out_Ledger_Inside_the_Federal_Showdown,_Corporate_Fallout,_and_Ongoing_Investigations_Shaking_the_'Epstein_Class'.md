# **The Blacked-Out Ledger: Inside the Federal Showdown, Corporate Fallout, and Ongoing Investigations Shaking the 'Epstein Class'**

On July 20, 2026, French police discovered the body of 68-year-old modeling scout Daniel Siad in his home in Colombes, a suburb of Paris. Though his attorney stated the cause of death was a heart attack, the official investigation into the circumstances remains open, drawing intense public scrutiny and adding pressure to the ongoing inquiry into the associates of Jeffrey Epstein. Siad, a Swedish-French national born in Algeria, was identified in declassified Department of Justice files released earlier this year as a recruiter who funneled young women into Epstein’s network across Europe and South Africa, with his name appearing over 2,000 times in internal communications. His death occurred just five days after Kathryn Ruemmler, the former White House Counsel and outgoing Chief Legal Officer of Goldman Sachs, testified in a closed-door congressional hearing in Washington, D.C., regarding her past communications with Epstein.

These parallel developments represent the latest friction in the ongoing legal and political battles surrounding the disclosure of the "Epstein files." Under the second Trump administration, the release of these documents has ignited intense political conflict, pitting federal agencies against journalists and exposing the relationships maintained by prominent corporate figures.

The legislative battle began in November 2025, when Congress passed the Epstein Files Transparency Act, requiring the Department of Justice to release all files pertaining to the investigation and prosecution of Epstein within 30 days. Upon the December deadline, the DOJ, then led by Attorney General Pam Bondi, released an initial batch of documents. Over 500 pages were completely blacked out, and within 24 hours, 16 files were removed from the public website. Furthermore, faulty digital redaction methods allowed the public to recover hidden text, which exposed the personal information and identities of several victims while keeping the names of some associates obscured.

The disclosure errors drew immediate criticism from advocates and lawmakers. In February 2026, attorneys representing more than 200 survivors asked federal judges Richard M. Berman and Paul Engelmayer to order the immediate shutdown of the DOJ’s website, calling the release a major violation of victim privacy. Vice President JD Vance subsequently acknowledged the administrative difficulties in a media appearance, stating that the administration had "screwed up the comms" regarding the release.

In April 2026, President Trump removed Pam Bondi as Attorney General, and Todd Blanche assumed the role of Acting Attorney General. In June 2026, Trump nominated U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York Jay Clayton as Director of National Intelligence; the Senate confirmed Clayton the following month. Simultaneously, journalist and legal analyst Katie Phang filed a lawsuit against Blanche in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, alleging that the DOJ had failed to comply with the Transparency Act. Phang argued that while the DOJ claimed full compliance after releasing 3.5 million pages by March 2026, prosecutors had identified over 6 million responsive pages, leaving more than 2.5 million pages withheld.

On June 25, 2026, District Judge Emmet G. Sullivan granted a preliminary injunction in *Phang v. Blanche*, noting that by failing to respond to Phang's arguments, the Attorney General had conceded to violating the act. Sullivan ordered the DOJ to produce the documents or justify the redactions by July 1. Hours before the deadline, Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward Jr. requested a delay, citing privacy concerns. In response, on July 2, 2026, Judge Sullivan ordered the DOJ to submit the unredacted documents for an *in camera* review, clearing the way for the potential disclosure of previously redacted names of Epstein's associates.

As these legal battles proceeded, the corporate fallout intensified. Kathryn Ruemmler, who served as White House Counsel under Barack Obama from 2011 to 2014 before joining Goldman Sachs, resigned her position as Chief Legal Officer in February 2026, effective at the end of June. Congressional records revealed that Ruemmler exchanged numerous emails with Epstein between 2014 and 2018, referring to him as "wonderful Jeffrey" and "sweetie," and soliciting his advice on career opportunities. The records also showed that Epstein gifted Ruemmler luxury items, including a $9,400 Hermès bag and a $1,300 Hermès Apple Watch. Despite public scrutiny, Goldman Sachs increased Ruemmler's compensation package to $25 million in 2025. In her July 15 testimony before the House Oversight Committee, Ruemmler defended her actions, characterizing Epstein as a "masterful liar."

The fallout also affected Jes Staley, the former group CEO of Barclays and former head of JPMorgan Chase's investment bank. In July 2025, the UK's Financial Conduct Authority permanently banned Staley from senior roles in the financial sector and fined him £1.1 million for misleading regulators about his relationship with Epstein. During those proceedings, Staley admitted to a consensual sexual encounter with a member of Epstein's staff in New York in 2010. Following the disclosure, Staley's wife, Deborah Nitzan Staley, filed for divorce on March 13, 2026. The unsealed files also revealed that Epstein had assisted Staley's daughter, Alexa, with academic admissions at Columbia University. Staley now faces continued pressure from U.S. senators over revelations that he was named as a backup executor of Epstein's estate and had shared confidential bank information with him.

JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon is also facing congressional scrutiny. While Dimon testified under oath in 2023 that he had no direct knowledge of Epstein's accounts, Staley has disputed this, claiming he discussed Epstein's business with Dimon. Senators are pressing Dimon regarding JPMorgan's compliance oversight during the years Epstein was a client. This pressure coincides with a $5 billion lawsuit filed against JPMorgan and Dimon by Donald Trump in January 2026, alleging the bank closed his accounts for political reasons.

The scale of the connections exposed in the documents has led congressional representatives, including Ro Khanna, Thomas Massie, and Senator Jon Ossoff, to coin the term the "Epstein class" to describe the wealthy and well-connected individuals accused of operating with institutional impunity. This systemic concern was echoed by a United Nations Human Rights Council panel on February 17, 2026, which stated that the operations detailed in the files suggest a "global criminal enterprise" that may have committed "crimes against humanity."

With the death of Daniel Siad in France and the ongoing judicial review of the redacted documents in Washington, the legal battles over the remaining files continue to develop, highlighting the conflicts between government transparency, victim privacy, and institutional accountability.
