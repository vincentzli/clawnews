# The Marmara Silence: Inside X’s Geoblock of Turkey’s Imprisoned Candidate and the Battle for the Digital Opposition

On the evening of August 3, 2026, the digital campaign of Turkey’s most prominent political prisoner vanished from the Turkish internet. Visitors inside the country attempting to access `@CAOIletisim11`—the official account of the Presidential Candidacy Office for jailed Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu—were met with a blank screen and a notice indicating the account had been withheld in response to a local legal demand. The geoblock, which restricted a campaign channel with 226,000 followers, came in the wake of a July 29, 2026, order issued by the Istanbul 8th Criminal Judgeship of Peace under case number 2026/6804. Citing Article 8/A of Turkey’s Law No. 5651, the court declared the account a threat to "national security and public order," acting on a request from the Istanbul Chief Public Prosecutor’s Office. 

The geoblock is the latest chapter in an ongoing digital conflict between the administration of Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan and his chief political rival. İmamoğlu has been held in Marmara Prison since March 23, 2025, following his detention on charges ranging from corruption to leading a criminal organization—allegations his supporters and international human rights organizations have condemned as politically motivated. In May 2025, just two months after his arrest, Turkish authorities successfully blocked İmamoğlu’s main personal account, which had nearly 10 million followers. Denied his primary digital channel, İmamoğlu’s team adapted by launching a succession of campaign handles like `@CAOIletisim`. As each handle was blocked by judicial decree, the team updated the username, adding digits to bypass the restrictions. The block on `@CAOIletisim11` represents the latest administrative strike in this sequence.

For X, the compliance triggered immediate public scrutiny. On August 3, the platform’s Global Government Affairs account released a statement explaining its decision: "Although Turkey's laws force us to comply with the order, we are challenging the order in court and, in the spirit of full transparency, are sharing the court order." The company argued that partial geoblocking was the only viable alternative to a total service ban or severe bandwidth throttling by Turkey's Information and Communication Technologies Authority (BTK). Under Turkish law, non-compliance with Article 8/A orders can result in BTK reducing a platform's bandwidth by up to 90 percent, effectively rendering it unusable for the country's population.

Digital rights organizations and legal analysts challenged this rationale. In early 2026, the Istanbul-based Freedom of Expression Association (İFÖD) published a comprehensive report titled *Digital Compliance Regime: Social Network Providers and the Illusion of Transparency in Turkey*, authored by law professor Yaman Akdeniz and researcher Ozan Güven. The report analyzed how global tech platforms have integrated themselves into Turkey's domestic censorship apparatus. Akdeniz, who tracks internet restrictions through İFÖD’s EngelliWeb project, has argued that companies like X, Google, and Meta have prioritized commercial survival over user rights, describing the regulatory environment as a "digital compliance regime." In response to X's latest legal challenge, Akdeniz expressed skepticism, noting that filing appeals in Turkish courts has become a routine administrative step that rarely yields reversals, while the platforms continue to enforce the government's political boundaries.

The geoblock also highlighted the deep realignment occurring within Turkey's opposition. İmamoğlu was originally selected as the presidential candidate for the Republican People’s Party (CHP) through a party primary in March 2025. However, on May 21, 2026, an Ankara regional appeals court annulled the November 2023 CHP leadership congress that had elected Özgür Özel as chairman, reinstating former leader Kemal Kılıçdaroğlu. The ruling fractured the main opposition. Denied leadership of the CHP, Özel and 90 other lawmakers resigned from the party, and on July 24, 2026, officially launched the *Yeni Parti* (New Party). The split shifted the parliamentary balance of power: the New Party became the second-largest bloc in the 600-seat Turkish Parliament with 91 deputies, while Kılıçdaroğlu’s CHP was reduced to 44. Despite the split, the New Party immediately endorsed the jailed İmamoğlu as their presumptive presidential candidate, making his candidacy office the central node of the new political movement's operations.

The restriction of `@CAOIletisim11` ignited a debate regarding the ethics of corporate compliance. On forums like Reddit’s r/europe, users debated the pragmatism of X's policy. Critics pointed to Elon Musk's public statements defining himself as a "free speech absolutist," arguing that X's compliance in Turkey and India contrasts with its confrontational legal battles in jurisdictions like Brazil. Other commentators defended X's geoblock as a necessary compromise. They argued that a complete platform ban would deny Turkish citizens access to one of the few remaining spaces for public discourse, leaving them entirely reliant on state-run media. 

While X pursues its appeal through the Turkish legal system, the prospects for a reversal remain low. According to İFÖD data, Turkish courts of peace rarely overturn blocking orders requested by prosecutors, particularly those involving high-profile political figures. For İmamoğlu, whose university diploma was annulled by Istanbul University in 2025 to challenge his constitutional eligibility, the digital blockade further isolates his campaign. Deprived of his physical freedom in Marmara Prison and stripped of his digital voice by corporate compliance, the candidate’s run for the presidency must now rely on an opposition that is increasingly fragmented and legally embattled.
