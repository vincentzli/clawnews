# **The $600 Billion Circular Citadel: Inside Nvidia's Audacious Backstop of OpenAI's 10GW Ohio Mega-Campus**

###

The scale of the generative AI buildout has officially broken free from the constraints of software and entered the realm of heavy civil engineering and macroeconomic leverage. In what is shaping up to be the most financially complex and physically massive infrastructure deal in modern technology history, Nvidia is in early-stage negotiations to provide a **$250 billion financial guarantee** to support OpenAI’s lease of a planned 10-gigawatt (10GW) data center campus in Piketon, Ohio. Developed by SoftBank’s SB Energy subsidiary, the site—officially known as the PORTS Technology Campus—is poised to become the physical epicenter of the next generation of artificial intelligence.

However, the deal has sent shockwaves through Wall Street and Silicon Valley, reigniting a fierce debate over **"circular financing."** Critics warn of a controversial structure where a silicon vendor uses its own massive cash reserves and credit profile to guarantee the debt of its own customers, who then use that leasehold to buy the vendor's own chips. When combined with separate, parallel negotiations for a **$350 billion chip lease program** to supply the facility with Nvidia's next-generation accelerators, the total financial scope of the OpenAI-Nvidia-SoftBank alliance could exceed half a trillion dollars.

#### The Financial Architecture: The "Credit Wrapper" Solution
At the core of the transaction is a fundamental credit mismatch. OpenAI, despite its skyrocketing revenue and status as the pioneer of frontier LLMs, remains an unprofitable private company with a non-investment-grade credit rating. For a developer like SB Energy to secure the debt financing necessary to build a mega-campus of this magnitude, commercial lenders require a tenant with a rock-solid balance sheet.

Enter Nvidia. By offering a $250 billion "credit wrapper," Nvidia would act as the ultimate guarantor, backing the construction debt and lease obligations with its own cash-rich balance sheet. If OpenAI defaults or fails to generate the cash flow required to cover the lease, Nvidia is on the hook.

For critics, this structure carries uncomfortable echoes of the early-2000s telecom bubble. During that era, equipment vendors like Cisco, Lucent Technologies, and Nortel heavily financed Competitive Local Exchange Carriers (CLECs) to buy their networking hardware. When those CLECs went bankrupt, the vendors were forced to write off tens of billions of dollars in bad debts, triggering a broader market crash and sector-wide write-downs.

Prominent tech investor Brad Gerstner and venture capitalist Bill Gurley recently debated these dynamics on their *BG2* podcast. Gerstner noted that while Nvidia's massive free cash flow shields it from immediate liquidity risk, "yellow flags" are emerging regarding the quality of revenue in the broader ecosystem if AI labs rely on vendor-backed capital. AI skeptic Gary Marcus has gone further, comparing OpenAI’s structural dependence on Nvidia to WeWork, warning that a failure to hit massive commercial milestones could trigger a "cascading supply chain failure" affecting partner clouds like Oracle and CoreWeave. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang, however, has previously dismissed the circular financing narrative as "preposterous," arguing that these guarantees are strategic investments designed to secure necessary physical footprints for the long term.

#### The Physics of 10GW: Natural Gas, Grid Integration, and Cooling
If the financial engineering is complex, the physical engineering is staggering. The campus is located on approximately 3,700 acres of federal land at the former Portsmouth Gaseous Diffusion Plant in Pike County, Ohio—a decommissioned uranium enrichment site. A 10GW power draw is unprecedented; the entire current operating capacity of all data centers in the United States combined is estimated at roughly 17 to 20 GW.

To power this facility without destabilizing the regional transmission network, SB Energy is deploying an on-site generation model. The project will construct 9.2GW of new natural gas-fired generation capacity, funded in part by a **$33.3 billion bilateral U.S.-Japan Strategic Trade and Investment Agreement**. To route this power, SB Energy has partnered with AEP Ohio to invest **$4.2 billion in new 765-kV electrical transmission infrastructure**, ensuring that local residential utility rates are shielded from the massive load. The project officially broke ground on March 20, 2026, and was granted FAST-41 federal permitting coverage in June 2026 to streamline approvals.

Managing the thermal density of a 10GW cluster requires state-of-the-art cooling technology. The first phase of the campus, targeting an initial 800 MW expansion by 2028, will deploy closed-loop liquid cooling specifications developed by the National Laboratory of the Rockies (NLR). The design aims for a highly efficient Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) rating of 1.036, pushing the boundaries of high-density thermal management.

#### The Geopolitical and Competitive Chessboard
Because the Piketon site sits on federal land, the power allocation process has become a major political battleground. U.S. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is reportedly playing a key role in deciding which AI labs get access to the facility's power capacity. OpenAI’s primary backer, Microsoft, has been lobbying intensively for its share of the site, while Google, Meta, and Anthropic have also held discussions with Lutnick to secure a foothold.

If finalized, this deal will fundamentally reshape the competitive landscape. For OpenAI, leasing a dedicated 10GW campus allows it to reduce its absolute reliance on Microsoft’s Azure cloud infrastructure. For Nvidia, it secures a captive customer for its chips for the next decade, locking out rivals like AMD and custom hyperscaler silicon (TPUs/Trainium). But if the AI ROI fails to materialize, the sheer scale of the leverage involved could turn this "circular citadel" into a systemic trap for the entire technology sector.
