# The Unitary State: How the Supreme Court Reshaped the Executive Branch and the Border in a Single Week

In a historic final week of its term, the U.S. Supreme Court has handed down a series of landmark rulings that fundamentally consolidate the executive authority of the Trump administration, while drawing a razor-thin boundary around the independence of the Federal Reserve. Over four days of high-stakes decisions, the conservative majority dismantled nearly a century of administrative law and stripped judicial protections from hundreds of thousands of immigrants. The rulings represent the most aggressive judicial endorsement of the unitary executive theory in modern American history, reshaping the separation of powers and throwing the future of independent regulatory agencies and the U.S. asylum system into profound uncertainty.

The shift in the structure of the administrative state came on June 29, 2026, in the case of *Trump v. Slaughter*. In a 6–3 decision, the Court ruled in favor of President Donald Trump. Chief Justice John Roberts delivered the majority opinion, declaring that the statutory "for-cause" removal protections for commissioners of the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) are unconstitutional. In doing so, the Court explicitly overruled *Humphrey’s Executor v. United States* (1935), a 91-year-old precedent that had long insulated independent federal agencies from political interference. The case arose after President Trump, early in his second term in March 2025, fired Democratic FTC Commissioners Rebecca Kelly Slaughter and Alvaro Bedoya. The administration stated that their continued service was "inconsistent with [the] Administration's priorities," bypassing the statutory requirement that commissioners could only be removed for "inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office." While Bedoya later resigned, Slaughter pressed her legal challenge, winning reinstatement in a federal district court before the Supreme Court intervened.

Writing for the majority, Chief Justice Roberts declared that because the FTC exercises executive power, its commissioners must be accountable to—and removable by—the President at will. "Nearly 250 years ago, the Framers decided to vest [t]he executive Power in one person—a President of the United States of America," the majority opinion stated. "Although it is up to the Senate to decide whether to confirm those with whom the President would prefer to work, neither Congress nor the courts may saddle him with those with whom he cannot work. Subordinates who exercise the President's power are subject to removal by him... only then can they remain accountable to the President, and the President to the people." Justice Sonia Sotomayor, joined by Justices Elena Kagan and Ketanji Brown Jackson, issued a stinging dissent, which Sotomayor read aloud from the bench. She warned that the decision grants the president “a power unknown even to the English Crown against which the Founders revolted, elevating him above his once coequal branches by transforming a duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed into a license to act in defiance of those very laws.”

On the same day, however, the Court drew a sharp, unexpected line at the doors of the central bank. In *Trump v. Cook*, the Court issued a 5–4 decision protecting Federal Reserve Board Governor Lisa D. Cook from at-will presidential removal. In August 2025, President Trump attempted to dismiss Cook, alleging that she had made false statements on mortgage agreements prior to her appointment—claims that Cook strongly denied. Cook sued, and the lower courts blocked her removal. In affirming the lower courts, Chief Justice John Roberts, joined by Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Jackson, emphasized the "unique historical status and role" of the Federal Reserve. The Court held that even under the statutory "for-cause" framework, the President must provide basic procedural protections, including notice of the evidence and an opportunity to respond. Because the administration failed to provide this due process, the Court ruled the attempted removal "erroneous and void" at this stage, allowing Cook to remain in her post until her term expires in 2038 while litigation continues in the lower courts.

The administrative battles followed two sweeping immigration rulings handed down on June 25, 2026. In *Mullin v. Al Otro Lado*, the Court ruled 6–3 that non-citizens seeking asylum who are blocked at the southern border while physically on the Mexican side have not legally "arrived in the United States" under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA). The ruling reversed a Ninth Circuit decision and provided explicit legal authorization for the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) to reinstate the controversial "metering" policy, which caps the number of asylum seekers processed at ports of entry. Justice Samuel Alito, writing for the majority, adopted a strict, literal interpretation of the statutory text, writing that "an alien standing in Mexico does not 'arriv[e] in the United States' by attempting, and failing, to set foot in this country." Sotomayor again read her dissent from the bench, declaring that the majority had allowed the executive branch to "slam the door shut" on those fleeing persecution. The reading prompted an unusually tense exchange in the courtroom, as Alito took the rare step of speaking from the bench to verbally counter Sotomayor’s remarks.

Hours later, the Court delivered another major victory to the administration in *Mullin v. Doe* (consolidated with *Trump v. Miot*). In another 6–3 decision, the Court held that the TPS statute (8 U.S.C. § 1254a) bars judicial review of non-constitutional claims regarding the designation, extension, or termination of Temporary Protected Status. The ruling effectively dismantled lower-court injunctions that had blocked the administration from terminating TPS for nationals of Haiti and Syria. This decision clears the way for DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin to proceed with deportations, immediately affecting approximately 350,000 Haitian nationals and 4,000 to 6,000 Syrian nationals. In her dissent, Justice Kagan, joined by Sotomayor and Jackson, accused the majority of insulating the executive branch from mandatory statutory requirements, such as consulting with relevant agencies on country conditions. Kagan also criticized the majority for dismissing "compelling evidence" that racial animus was a motivating factor in terminating Haiti's TPS, noting that the plaintiffs had met their burden of proof by citing the President's own public statements.

The reaction to the Court's blockbuster week was immediate and polarized. Former Vice President Kamala Harris issued a statement warning that the rulings "threaten the core foundations of the rule of law and the independent regulatory protections that Americans rely on every day." Senator Sheldon Whitehouse (D-RI) described the *Slaughter* decision as a "pro-corruption decision" that systematically dismantles the integrity of consumer protection, while Senator Michael Bennet (D-CO) warned it would severely weaken agency independence. In the immigration arena, New York Attorney General Letitia James condemned the TPS ruling as "a betrayal of our values that tears families apart." Immigrant advocacy groups, including the American Immigration Council and the Florence Immigrant & Refugee Rights Project, warned of an impending humanitarian crisis at the southern border as metering policies are fully restored.

By dismantling *Humphrey’s Executor*, the Court has placed independent agencies like the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) under direct presidential control. While the Federal Reserve managed a narrow escape in *Cook* due to procedural deficiencies, the decisions collectively signal a major restructuring of the federal government, centralizing unprecedented authority in the Oval Office.
