# Silicon Valley’s New Cold War: How Apple’s Espionage Lawsuit Against OpenAI Threatens to Explode the AI Hardware Boom

On Friday, July 10, 2026, Apple Inc. filed a blockbuster federal lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California, signaling a formal end to the high-profile partnership between the two companies. The 40-page complaint paints a picture of a calculated, multi-year corporate espionage campaign designed to strip Apple of its hardware secrets to build OpenAI’s consumer hardware division. At the center of the dispute is OpenAI’s $6.5 billion acquisition of io Products—a hardware startup founded in 2024 by former Apple design chief Jony Ive, Scott Cannon, Evans Hankey, and Tang Yew Tan—as well as the aggressive poaching of more than 400 former Apple employees.

The lawsuit names OpenAI, io Products, and two key former Apple employees: Tang Yew Tan, now OpenAI’s Chief Hardware Officer, and Chang Liu, a former senior system electrical engineer. According to the complaint, OpenAI did not merely hire Apple’s talent; it institutionalized a process of extracting Apple’s proprietary engineering blueprints, metal-finishing techniques, and battery designs to bypass years of expensive research and development. 

Among the primary allegations, Apple claims that Tang Yew Tan, who spent over two decades at Apple, eventually rising to Vice President of Product Design for the iPhone and Apple Watch, methodically gathered confidential supplier lists and internal industry summaries before resigning. More damningly, the lawsuit alleges that Tan and other OpenAI managers instructed job candidates from Apple to bring "actual parts"—including unreleased prototypes, custom batteries, logic boards, and system-in-packages (SIPs)—to OpenAI interviews for "show and tell" sessions. According to the filing, Tan used internal Apple project codenames to pressure candidates into revealing specific design parameters of unannounced Apple products. Once hired, Tan allegedly shared internal Apple offboarding documents to coach employees on how to copy confidential files and leave the company without triggering security alerts.

If Tan is accused of orchestrating the recruitment pipeline, electrical engineer Chang Liu is accused of executing the technical breach. Liu, who joined OpenAI in January 2026, allegedly failed to return his company-issued Apple laptop upon his resignation. The lawsuit claims that Liu discovered a previously unknown authentication bug in Apple’s access management systems that failed to revoke his credentials. Rather than reporting the vulnerability, Liu allegedly exploited it to continue logging into Apple’s secure internal networks and cloud storage folders from his new post at OpenAI. 

Over several weeks, Liu allegedly downloaded dozens of confidential hardware files, including detailed specifications of complex multi-layer motherboards and blueprints for unreleased products, culminating in a single compilation of over 1,000 pages of highly sensitive technical material. The lawsuit contains a text message Liu sent to an Apple colleague who was still at the company: “LOL, I found out I can access the [network storage], so funny.” Apple claims this message proves the unauthorized access was deliberate and malicious. Furthermore, Apple accuses Liu of coaching other engineers on how to siphon off technical files while avoiding detection by Apple’s security teams.

The espionage allegedly extended deep into Apple’s proprietary supply chain. According to the complaint, OpenAI approached a trusted, shared manufacturing partner and persuaded them to perform a proprietary, multi-step metal-finishing technique that Apple spent years developing. Apple claims that OpenAI falsely told the supplier that Apple had authorized the use of the contractually restricted process. Additionally, the lawsuit alleges that OpenAI approached a long-time Apple power and battery supplier, using Apple’s internal technical terminology to ask highly specific questions about manufacturing processes that only an insider would know to ask.

Drew Pusateri, OpenAI’s Director of Strategic Communications, issued a formal statement on behalf of the company: “We have no interest in other companies' trade secrets. We remain focused on building innovative technology that empowers people everywhere.” Despite the denial, the lawsuit has triggered an immediate crisis for OpenAI, which is currently preparing for a highly anticipated Initial Public Offering (IPO). Analysts warn that the litigation could severely depress OpenAI's valuation and delay the IPO timeline, as institutional investors weigh the legal risks of a hardware business that Apple claims is "rotten to its core." 

On platforms like Reddit and X, the tech community is reacting with a mixture of shock and dark humor. On r/wallstreetbets, users are debating the fallout of the "LOL" text, with one user noting, "Imagine sinking a multi-billion-dollar hardware division because you couldn't resist texting 'LOL' about a security bug." On X, Elon Musk seized on the filing to mock OpenAI and Sam Altman, continuing his public campaign against the company's shift toward a closed-source, for-profit model.

The litigation also marks the final collapse of the partnership announced in 2024, which was supposed to integrate OpenAI’s ChatGPT into Apple Intelligence and Siri. Tensions had been rising as OpenAI aggressively pursued consumer hardware, but this lawsuit codifies the transition from partners to bitter rivals. Reports indicate that Apple is already actively working to replace OpenAI's technology in its operating systems, pivoting toward Google’s Gemini AI. As the legal battle begins in the Northern District of California, the AI industry faces a sobering reality: the talent wars are no longer just about salaries and stock options, but federal litigation and trade secret theft.
