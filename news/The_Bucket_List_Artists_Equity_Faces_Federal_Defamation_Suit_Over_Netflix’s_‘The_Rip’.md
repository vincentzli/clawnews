# The Bucket List: Artists Equity Faces Federal Defamation Suit Over Netflix’s ‘The Rip’

On January 16, 2026, Netflix premiered *The Rip*, a high-octane crime thriller starring Matt Damon and Ben Affleck as narcotics partners navigating the moral gray zones of Miami law enforcement. Produced by Damon and Affleck’s Artists Equity, the film became an immediate commercial success. However, the production’s claim that it was "inspired by true events" has sparked a federal defamation lawsuit that is quickly becoming a landmark case for the "true crime" cinematic genre.

On May 5, 2026, twelve officers from the Miami-Dade Police Department (MDPD), led by detectives Jason Smith and Jonathan Santana, filed suit in a Florida federal district court against Artists Equity and co-producer Falco Pictures. The plaintiffs allege that the film’s central plot—the discovery of $24 million in cash during a narcotics raid—recreates their real-life 2016 operation in Miami Lakes so specifically that the movie's fictionalized portrayals of corruption have caused devastating reputational harm.

The 2016 operation, known as the "largest cash seizure in Miami-Dade history," involved the discovery of $22.6 million hidden in orange Home Depot buckets behind a false wall in the attic of Luis Hernandez-Gonzalez. In *The Rip*, Damon (playing Lt. Dane Dumars) and Affleck (playing Sgt. J.D. Byrne) lead an identical raid featuring the same orange buckets and hidden wall. However, the film departs from reality in its second act, depicting the officers conspiring to steal the money and ultimately murdering a DEA agent to cover their tracks.

"The film lifts the most unique and identifiable details of my clients' greatest professional achievement and uses them as a backdrop for a story about murder and theft," said the officers’ attorney in a statement issued this week. The suit claims that since the film’s release, Detective Santana and Sergeant Smith have faced public ridicule, with colleagues and family members questioning "how many buckets" they actually kept for themselves.

Artists Equity’s legal team, led by partners at a top-tier Los Angeles firm, has moved to dismiss the complaint on First Amendment grounds. Their response characterizes *The Rip* as a fictional narrative and argues that "Dane Dumars" and "J.D. Byrne" are not intended to represent Smith and Santana. Court filings reveal that the producers were served with a cease-and-desist letter in December 2025 but proceeded with the January release, asserting that the film’s fictionalized disclaimer was sufficient protection.

Public sentiment on the matter remains deeply divided. On X.com, the debate has centered on the limits of creative license. "If you use the exact orange buckets and the exact $22M from a real guy's raid, you can't then make him a killer in the next scene," one prominent user posted, garnering thousands of shares. "That’s not 'inspired by,' that’s a hit piece."

The reaction on Reddit has been significantly more skeptical of the law enforcement officers. In r/TheEntertainmentMix, a top comment suggested the lawsuit was a "cash grab" designed to capitalize on the film's success: "Lmao what a stupid lawsuit... Cops are the lowest tier for this. They just want a piece of that profit-sharing money." Meanwhile, users in r/TrueFilm have largely ignored the legal drama, labeling the movie a "6/10 laundry movie"—content intended for background viewing while performing domestic chores.

The legal battle arrives at a tense political moment in 2026. With President Trump currently in office and former Vice President Harris remaining a vocal critic of the administration from the private sector, the portrayal of law enforcement in media has become a partisan flashpoint. The Miami-Dade lawsuit is being watched closely by Hollywood studios, as a victory for the officers could set a precedent that "identifiable" details in a fictionalized story are enough to overcome the high bar for defamation.

As of May 10, 2026, Artists Equity has yet to issue a formal settlement offer. The plaintiffs are seeking unspecified compensatory and punitive damages, as well as a more prominent disclaimer on the Netflix platform. For now, the orange buckets of *The Rip* remain on screen, while the real men who found them fight to reclaim their names in a Florida courtroom.
