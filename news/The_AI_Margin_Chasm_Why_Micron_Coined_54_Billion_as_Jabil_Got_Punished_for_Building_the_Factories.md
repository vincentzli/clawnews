# The AI Margin Chasm: Why Micron Coined $54 Billion as Jabil Got Punished for Building the Factories

On September 30, 2026, the equity market delivered an unforgiving verdict on the division of spoils across the artificial intelligence hardware supply chain. Shares of Jabil Inc. (NYSE: JBL) tumbled 10.06% to close at $286.86, vaporizing $3.36 billion of market capitalization in a single trading session. The selloff arrived despite the St. Petersburg, Florida-based manufacturing services giant delivering fiscal fourth-quarter 2026 revenue of $10.60 billion—a 29.0% year-over-year surge that beat Wall Street consensus of $9.69 billion—and core diluted earnings per share of $4.40, topping analyst projections of $4.06. Just hours earlier, Micron Technology, Inc. (NASDAQ: MU) underscored where economic rent actually pools: posting fiscal fourth-quarter revenue of $54.23 billion, up an astronomical 379.1% from $11.32 billion in the year-ago period, propelled by a GAAP gross margin of 86.8% and quarterly operating cash flow of $43.97 billion. With Micron hovering near a record $1.20 trillion valuation at $1,065.11 per share, the divergence exposed an escalating crisis of capital allocation: silicon and memory monopolists are capturing near-software margins, while the physical assemblers of the AI economy are left absorbing factory overhead, working capital strain, and acute operational risk.

The structural contrast in profitability between component oligopolists and contract manufacturers has widened into a chasm. Micron’s specialized Core Data Center business unit generated $18.00 billion in quarterly revenue at an astonishing 90.0% gross margin and an 85.0% operating margin, while its Cloud Memory division—housing its High Bandwidth Memory (HBM4 and HBM3e) lines—delivered an 83.0% gross margin and a 76.0% operating margin. In stark contrast, Jabil reported a consolidated core operating margin of 6.4% for the fourth quarter, bringing its full-year fiscal 2026 core operating margin to 5.8% on total revenue of $36.00 billion (up 21.0% from $29.75 billion in fiscal 2025). Even within Jabil’s Intelligent Infrastructure segment, where AI server enclosures, liquid cooling distribution units, and 800G power shelves drove a 56.0% year-over-year revenue leap to $5.80 billion, operating margins peaked at just 6.5%. For every dollar of hyperscaler capital expenditure that enters the hardware ecosystem, component designers and fabricators retain upwards of 80 cents in gross profit, while contract assemblers take home roughly 6 cents of operating profit before debt service and taxes.

This stark skew ignited fierce debate across trading desks, Fintwit, and online retail message boards. On Reddit’s r/stocks, where retail investors had bid Jabil to a trailing price-to-earnings multiple of 22.8x ahead of earnings—double its ten-year historical median of 11.4x—one widely circulated analysis dissected the structural trap: "Jabil is a classic picks-and-shovels trap. They build the power shelves and liquid cooling loops for hyperscalers, but is stuck fighting for 60 basis points of operating margin while memory and silicon take all the economic rent." Over on r/MU_Stock, sentiment tilted toward awe at Micron’s transformation into what one contributor characterized as "an unregulated utility for AI compute," even as users debated whether an 86.8% gross margin signaled the ultimate peak of an unprecedented supercycle. Meanwhile, on StockTwits, where sentiment on $JBL flipped to deeply bearish following the 10.06% plunge, retail skepticism focused squarely on executive promises: "Every time management says the second half will make up for front-half dilution, you're one hyperscaler pushout away from a massive guide-down."

The catalyst for Jabil’s double-digit markdown lay not in its trailing numbers, but in the execution gauntlet embedded in its fiscal 2027 guidance. Chief Executive Officer Mike Dastoor and Chief Financial Officer Greg Hebard projected fiscal 2027 revenue of $44.50 billion—representing 23.6% growth—with core diluted EPS guided to $17.55 and core operating margins targeting 6.1%. However, management revealed that this financial performance is severely back-half loaded: revenue is projected at a 45/55 split between the first and second halves of the fiscal year, with operating margins skewed even more radically toward the final two quarters. To support a projected 54.0% surge in AI-related revenue to $22.10 billion in fiscal 2027, Jabil is adding 4.0 million square feet of manufacturing capacity across facilities in the United States, Mexico, India, and Southeast Asia. Net capital expenditures are projected to run between 1.5% and 2.0% of revenue ($667.5 million to $890.0 million), forcing Jabil to absorb unabsorbed factory overhead, tooling costs, and training expenses long before volume production ramps to profitable yields.

Compounding this physical burden is Jabil’s position at the mercy of component allocation. While Jabil managed to generate more than $1.50 billion in adjusted free cash flow during fiscal 2026, its net working capital cycle has been stretched by chronic supply chain imbalances. On the earnings call, Dastoor explicitly cited "real" constraints in high-bandwidth memory and advanced enterprise DRAM, where supply shortages are dictating system integration schedules and forcing EMS providers to hold inventory buffers. With Jabil’s inventory days elevated at 68 days—well above its 55-day target—billions in working capital remain tied up in partially assembled AI racks awaiting missing critical memory modules.

Micron, meanwhile, is leveraging its oligopoly pricing power to lock hyperscalers into lucrative long-term commitments. Chief Executive Officer Sanjay Mehrotra delivered full-year fiscal 2026 revenue of $133.19 billion and GAAP net income of $84.97 billion, translating to $74.33 per diluted share. For the first quarter of fiscal 2027, Micron guided revenue to $61.50 billion (±$1.50 billion) and GAAP gross margin to 86.3%, backed by Strategic Customer Agreements (SCAs) that commit cloud giants to purchase quotas through calendar 2028. Armed with $43.97 billion in quarterly operating cash flow and a cash pile exceeding $62.00 billion, Micron authorized a $650 million quarterly share buyback set to begin in December 2026. The Idaho-based chipmaker operates in a three-player memory cartel with SK Hynix and Samsung, allowing it to dictate terms, pass on soaring raw material expenses, and capture virtually all incremental surplus value generated by hyperscaler compute budgets.

The divergence between Jabil and Micron exposes the broader polarization across the electronics manufacturing services (EMS) sector. While Celestica Inc. (NYSE: CLS) managed to expand its adjusted operating margins to 8.0% in early 2026 by aggressively prioritizing proprietary Original Design Manufacturer (ODM) networking switches through its Hardware Platform Solutions (HPS) segment, traditional contract assemblers remain trapped in commodity economics. Hon Hai Precision Industry (Foxconn), which assembles complete NVL72 server racks at immense multi-billion-dollar scale, continues to generate thin consolidated operating margins between 3.2% and 3.8%. Jabil’s attempt to escape this commodity baseline by acquiring liquid cooling specialist Mikros Technologies and power management firm Hanley Energy has broadened its capabilities, but as its fiscal 2027 margin guidance demonstrates, integration costs and pricing pressure from hyperscalers like Amazon Web Services, Microsoft, and Google prevent those technology assets from lifting corporate operating margins beyond the low-6.0% corridor.

In the broader macroeconomic arena, this hardware bifurcation intersects with rising financial hurdles. With U.S. 10-year Treasury yields hovering near 4.18% and the Treasury Department under Scott Bessent maintaining tariff policies that elevate imported capital equipment costs, the cost of financing physical factory footprints has risen substantially. Hyperscalers are projected to deploy over $285 billion in aggregate capital expenditures in calendar 2026, but their procurement teams have shown zero willingness to share economic margins with assemblers. When Jabil traded at $318.94 prior to earnings, investors were pricing the company as an unencumbered AI derivative; the subsequent 10.06% re-rating to $286.86 represents the market’s realization that capital-expenditure victims cannot command the valuation multiples of silicon monopolists. Until contract manufacturers can command intellectual property moats that shield them from component shortages and upfront factory drag, the AI gold rush will continue to enrich the foundry and the memory fab, leaving the builders of the assembly lines to bear the execution risk on razor-thin cushions.
