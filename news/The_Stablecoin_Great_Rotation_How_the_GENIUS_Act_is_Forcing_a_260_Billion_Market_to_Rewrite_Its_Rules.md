# The Stablecoin Great Rotation: How the GENIUS Act is Forcing a $260 Billion Market to Rewrite Its Rules

On the public ledger of the Ethereum blockchain, the migration of capital is silent, but its scale is historic. Over the course of the past three months, Tether’s flagship stablecoin, USDT, has seen its market capitalization contract to $184 billion—a sharp $6 billion drop from its peak of $190 billion in May 2026. Simultaneously, Circle’s USD Coin (USDC) has compressed to $74 billion. These are not merely temporary fluctuations in the digital asset markets; they are the opening ripples of a structural realignment triggered by the U.S. federal government. The Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins Act (GENIUS Act), signed into law by President Donald Trump on July 18, 2025, is rapidly redrawing the boundaries of digital finance as its January 18, 2027 enforcement deadline approaches.

At the heart of this market contraction is a legislative mechanism designed to protect the traditional banking sector from deposit flight. Under the GENIUS Act, any entity issuing a stablecoin used for payments within the United States must obtain a charter as a Permitted Payment Stablecoin Issuer (PPSI). While the law grants these issuers a pathway to federal legitimacy, it attaches a critical constraint: a strict, comprehensive prohibition on paying interest or yield, in any form, to stablecoin holders. This rule, cemented by the "Hagerty Amendment" introduced by Senator Bill Hagerty during legislative negotiations in mid-2025, was specifically crafted to prevent stablecoins from acting as high-yield digital deposit accounts that could drain liquidity from regional banking institutions.

With U.S. interest rates remaining elevated, the yield ban has transformed stablecoins from highly attractive capital-parking vehicles into inert transactional rails. Corporate treasurers and institutional investors are refusing to leave billions of dollars in non-yielding instruments. Instead, they are rotating their holdings into tokenized real-world assets (RWAs) that hold a different regulatory classification. Because tokenized money market funds operate under the Investment Company Act of 1940 as securities rather than payment stablecoins, they are legally permitted to distribute yield.

The primary beneficiaries of this regulatory bifurcation are products like the BlackRock USD Institutional Digital Liquidity Fund (BUIDL) and Circle’s partnered USD Yield Coin (USYC). As of late July 2026, BUIDL’s market capitalization has climbed to approximately $2.68 billion, while USYC—originally launched by Hashnote and integrated by Circle in early 2025—has grown to approximately $3 billion. The total market for tokenized U.S. Treasury and money market products has surged to $16 billion, up from $11 billion at the start of the year. This migration is actively draining the liquidity pool of traditional stablecoins, leaving the digital asset ecosystem structurally altered.

As federal agencies including the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC), the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), and the Treasury Department scramble to finalize the operational frameworks before the January 2027 cliff, stablecoin issuers are executing drastic restructurings.

Circle, long positioning itself as the compliant, U.S.-first issuer, is pursuing the PPSI charter under the OCC. To do so, it has formally bifurcated its product suite: USDC remains the non-yielding, transactional stablecoin, while USYC serves as the yield-bearing vehicle for institutional custody.

Tether, operating from its offshore base in the British Virgin Islands, has taken a more complex dual-track approach to survive the U.S. regulatory dragnet. Recognizing that its global USDT product does not comply with the strict reserves and audit standards mandated by the GENIUS Act, the company launched USAT (USA₮) in January 2026. Ring-fenced specifically for the U.S. market, USAT is issued through Anchorage Digital Bank, a federally chartered U.S. institution, with its reserves managed directly by the custodian Cantor Fitzgerald. Tether is actively deploying USAT across networks like Ethereum and Celo to capture U.S. transaction volume, while maintaining USDT for its international, non-U.S. client base.

To shore up confidence ahead of the enforcement deadline, Tether is also undergoing an unprecedented auditing overhaul. In March 2026, the company transitioned from its historical point-in-time reserve attestations, conducted by BDO Italia, and engaged KPMG to perform its first-ever full, independent financial statement audit. PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC) was simultaneously brought in to overhaul Tether’s internal accounting systems to prepare for the rigorous scrutiny of federal regulators.

The regulatory transition has sparked intense debate within the financial sector. On social media platforms and crypto forums, the reaction to the implementation of the GENIUS Act is divided. In discussions on Reddit's r/wallstreetbets, users argue that the interest prohibition is a direct protection mechanism for traditional commercial banks. As one prominent online commentator noted, the banking lobby was unable to halt the adoption of stablecoins, resulting in legislation that simply prohibited them from paying yield to holders. Conversely, banking analysts defend the law, pointing out that unchecked, interest-bearing stablecoins operate as unregulated shadow banks without FDIC insurance, presenting a systemic risk to the broader financial system.

With the federal agencies entering the final stages of rulemaking in late July 2026, the countdown to January 18, 2027, is accelerating. The contraction of USDT and USDC is not a sign of the death of stablecoins, but of their transformation. As the market splits between zero-yield payment tokens and yield-bearing tokenized securities, the era of the unregulated, high-yield digital dollar is coming to a close.
