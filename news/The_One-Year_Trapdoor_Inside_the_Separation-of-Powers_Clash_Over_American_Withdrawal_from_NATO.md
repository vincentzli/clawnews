# The One-Year Trapdoor: Inside the Separation-of-Powers Clash Over American Withdrawal from NATO

Inside the executive suites of the Harry S. Truman Building, legal advisers at the Department of State face a constitutional collision whose central statutory obstacle was authored by their own department head, Secretary of State Marco Rubio.

In July 2023, while representing Florida in the United States Senate, Rubio partnered with Senator Tim Kaine, Democrat of Virginia, to introduce a bipartisan firewall designed to prevent any occupant of the Oval Office from unilaterally dismantling the transatlantic alliance. Enacted as Section 1250A of the Fiscal Year 2024 National Defense Authorization Act and codified at 22 U.S.C. § 1928f, the statute establishes a categorical domestic restriction: the President “shall not suspend, terminate, denounce, or withdraw the United States from the North Atlantic Treaty... except by and with the advice and consent of the Senate, provided that two-thirds of the Senators present concur, or pursuant to an Act of Congress.” To reinforce the restriction, Congress anchored the statute in Article I’s Appropriations Clause, mandating that no authorized or appropriated federal funds may be used to support, directly or indirectly, any decision to execute an exit.

Yet as President Donald Trump sharpens his long-standing criticism of European burden-sharing—pressing allies to elevate defense budgets toward 5 percent of gross domestic product while questioning the automaticity of mutual assistance—the statutory mechanism faces an unprecedented legal challenge. Diplomats, congressional counsel, and constitutional litigators are confronting a high-stakes jurisdictional paradox: the friction between domestic statutory constraints and the self-executing provisions of international treaty law.

The operational fault line sits directly within Article 13 of the North Atlantic Treaty, signed in Washington on April 4, 1949. The provision dictates that any party may cease to be a member precisely one year after depositing a formal “notice of denunciation” with the depositary government. Under the treaty’s unique structural history, the designated depositary government is the United States itself. On the international plane, governed by customary international law and reflected in the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties, an official notification of denunciation delivered by a head of state is immediately recognizable and binding among signatory capitals. Once transmitted, the international clock begins. Even if federal courts in Washington were to issue an injunction declaring the transmission domestic ultra vires conduct, the international community would confront a live, irreversible 365-day countdown toward the legal dissolution of Washington’s treaty obligations.

Executive branch legal doctrine has steadily developed arguments to bypass legislative treaty constraints. In December 2020, the Department of Justice’s Office of Legal Counsel issued a formal memorandum addressing President Trump’s decision to exit the Open Skies Treaty without adhering to a 120-day statutory congressional notification requirement. The OLC concluded that the President possesses exclusive, preclusive authority under Article II of the Constitution to terminate international agreements, asserting that statutory conditions or prohibitions on treaty withdrawal violate the separation of powers. Because that opinion was not revoked during the Biden administration, it remains binding executive precedent for federal agencies.

Congressional leadership and national security scholars argue that 22 U.S.C. § 1928f fundamentally displaces the historical ambiguity surrounding treaty termination. In 1979, when President Jimmy Carter unilaterally abrogated the Sino-American Mutual Defense Treaty with Taiwan, Senator Barry Goldwater and a group of legislators filed suit. In *Goldwater v. Carter*, the Supreme Court vacated lower court judgments and dismissed the suit without reaching the merits. A plurality led by Justice William Rehnquist classified the dispute as a nonjusticiable political question, while Justice Lewis Powell concurred on the grounds that the conflict was not ripe because Congress had never passed a collective statute or resolution directly confronting the President’s assertion of authority.

Section 1250A was drafted specifically to dismantle the *Goldwater* barrier. By passing a binding bicameral statute signed into law, Congress formally registered institutional opposition, fulfilling Justice Powell’s ripeness test. Furthermore, under the canonical separation-of-powers doctrine articulated by Justice Robert Jackson in the 1952 steel seizure case *Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer*, an executive attempt to exit NATO in defiance of § 1928f falls into Category 3—presidential power at its “lowest ebb.” In that posture, the executive can prevail only by establishing that the Constitution grants the President exclusive and preclusive authority over treaty termination, leaving Congress powerless to regulate the matter. Subsequent rulings, such as *Zivotofsky v. Clinton* in 2012, demonstrated that federal courts will adjudicate statutory-executive conflicts rather than dismissing them under the political question doctrine when the boundaries of constitutional power are at stake.

Congress, however, left a critical vulnerability in the statutory framework. In the original Senate bill, Section 1250A contained an explicit provision preauthorizing the Office of Senate Legal Counsel and the House General Counsel to initiate emergency federal litigation in the event of an unauthorized withdrawal. During bicameral conference committee negotiations, House conferees insisted on stripping the litigation preauthorization from the final text. 

That omission leaves any immediate judicial challenge vulnerable to threshold dismissal under the strict legislative standing rules established in *Raines v. Byrd* (1997). In *Raines*, the Supreme Court held that individual lawmakers lack Article III standing to litigate institutional injuries that are abstract and widely dispersed across the legislative branch. Legal scholars offer two competing pathways around this obstacle. Former State Department Legal Adviser Harold Koh argues that because treaty ratification requires a two-thirds Senate supermajority, a coalition of 34 senators—the precise number required to withhold consent—suffers a direct and particularized “vote nullification” injury when a president exits unilaterally. Alternatively, Scott R. Anderson of the Brookings Institution highlights the D.C. Circuit’s framework in *U.S. House of Representatives v. Mnuchin* (2020), which recognized that a legislative chamber possesses independent standing to challenge executive expenditures that violate express statutory funding prohibitions. Because § 1928f explicitly bans the use of appropriated funds to implement an unauthorized exit, a lawsuit centered on the Appropriations Clause provides an established jurisdictional bridge into federal court.

If an administration transmitted an Article 13 notice, the resulting constitutional litigation would trigger widespread operational paralysis across the North Atlantic alliance. NATO’s military command architecture relies on the Supreme Allied Commander Europe (SACEUR), a position dual-hatted with the commander of U.S. European Command (EUCOM). Because § 1928f prohibits the expenditure of federal funds to execute a withdrawal, any administrative action to draw down staff at the Supreme Headquarters Allied Powers Europe (SHAPE) in Mons, Belgium, or alter intelligence sharing arrangements could trigger violations of the Antideficiency Act. Military commanders and Department of Defense comptrollers would operate under immediate threat of fiscal and administrative sanctions.

The legal impasse would extend directly to NATO’s nuclear deterrent. Under long-standing nuclear sharing agreements, approximately 100 U.S. Air Force-owned B61 gravity bombs are stored in hardened subterranean vaults at six air bases across five European nations: Büchel in Germany, Aviano and Ghedi in Italy, Kleine Brogel in Belgium, Volkel in the Netherlands, and Incirlik in Turkey. Custody and maintenance are managed by specialized U.S. Munitions Support Squadrons, while allied dual-capable aircraft train to deliver the weapons under NATO Nuclear Planning Group directives. Emergency litigation over whether the United States remains a treaty partner would destabilize custodial agreements, technical assistance funding, and nuclear command-and-control protocols.

The political tensions surrounding the statute have generated fierce debate across Washington and allied capitals. When the measure cleared Congress, Kaine emphasized that the law "reaffirms U.S. support for this crucial alliance that is foundational for our national security." When Rubio co-sponsored the text, he described NATO as an "essential military alliance that protects shared interests and enhances America's presence in the world." Now, with Rubio managing foreign policy from the executive cabinet, the dual realities have become a focal point of public discourse. On Reddit’s r/politics, debates frequently highlight the institutional ironies, with participants noting the constitutional friction of a sitting cabinet official facing statutory guardrails he personally engineered. On X, legal analysts observe the tension between dual legal systems, pointing out that an executive transmission would immediately begin the international calendar in Brussels regardless of domestic procedural litigation in Washington.

Defense strategists emphasize that an administration would not need to secure a complete judicial victory to neutralize the alliance. By issuing an Article 13 notice and pursuing an administrative drawdown—withholding participation from North Atlantic Council deliberations, leaving command vacancies unfilled, and casting doubt on Article 5 mutual defense guarantees—the executive branch could achieve effective disengagement while litigation works its way through the D.C. Circuit and Supreme Court. Because collective defense deterrence relies entirely on the undisputed credibility of an immediate American military response, a twelve-month window of constitutional paralysis would dismantle the alliance’s deterrent umbrella long before the judiciary delivers a final judgment.
