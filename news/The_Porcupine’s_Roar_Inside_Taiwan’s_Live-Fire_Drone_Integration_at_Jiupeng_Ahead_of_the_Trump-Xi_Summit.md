# The Porcupine’s Roar: Inside Taiwan’s Live-Fire Drone Integration at Jiupeng Ahead of the Trump-Xi Summit

On the wind-swept coastal bluffs of Jiupeng Military Base in southern Pingtung County, the Pacific horizon dissolved into plumes of white exhaust and sonic reverberations on September 18, 2026. Over seventy-two hours of intensive live-fire exercises, Taiwan’s armed forces executed an unprecedented multi-domain operational drill that military strategists have spent years drafting on paper: the combat pairing of indigenous loitering attack drones with high-mobility American precision rocket artillery. 

Standing on the observation perimeter in standard digital camouflage alongside civilian Defense Minister Wellington Koo, President Lai Ching-te observed the sequential launch of indigenous Hsiung Feng III supersonic anti-ship missiles, Tien Kung III surface-to-air interceptors, and newly fielded U.S.-manufactured M142 High Mobility Artillery Rocket Systems (HIMARS). The drills, which concluded Friday, simulated responses to an escalating Chinese People's Liberation Army (PLA) joint blockade, cross-strait saturation airstrikes, and rapid amphibious landings along Taiwan’s southern approaches.

Yet the primary audience for the Jiupeng maneuvers extended far beyond the PLA Eastern Theater Command in Nanjing. The exercises occurred exactly six days before U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping are scheduled to meet on September 24, 2026, for a high-stakes bilateral summit. In Taipei, the live-fire display functioned as an urgent, physical proof-of-concept: demonstrating that Taiwan’s transition toward an asymmetric "porcupine" doctrine is operational, even as political uncertainty shadows the future of American security commitments.

At the technical center of the exercise was the combat integration of uncrewed strike wings with long-range kinetic fires. The National Chung-Shan Institute of Science and Technology (NCSIST) demonstrated coordinated salvos featuring the Chien Hsiang, an indigenous delta-wing anti-radiation loitering munition. Launched from mobile 12-canister truck trailers, the propeller-driven, delta-wing loitering munitions flew simulated Suppression of Enemy Air Defenses (SEAD) missions, loitering over simulated coastal approach lanes to track radiating target radars before plunging onto target coordinates.

Simultaneously, Taiwan’s artillery units deployed frontline M142 HIMARS launchers. Taiwan procured 29 HIMARS launchers and 84 MGM-140 Army Tactical Missile Systems (ATACMS) across two tranches—an initial batch delivered between 2024 and 2025, and an accelerated second batch rolling into frontline service through 2026. Armed with tactical ballistic munitions featuring an operational reach of up to 300 kilometers, the HIMARS crews coordinated with batteries firing the ramjet-powered Hsiung Feng III—which streaks at Mach 2.5 to 3.0—demonstrating an anti-access bubble capable of striking naval staging ports across the 100-mile Taiwan Strait.

The doctrine tested at Jiupeng mirrors the "Hellscape" strategy articulated by Admiral Samuel Paparo, Commander of U.S. Indo-Pacific Command (INDOPACOM). Paparo’s conceptual framework envisions turning the Taiwan Strait into an unnavigable morass by blanketing its airspace and waters with thousands of autonomous aerial drones, unmanned surface vessels (USVs), and subsurface assets the moment Chinese naval assets leave port, buying critical operational time for allied forces to assemble.

Beijing’s response to the drills was immediate and severe. The People's Republic of China Ministry of National Defense condemned the maneuvers as a "futile step toward disaster," reiterating that arms purchases cannot alter Beijing’s claim over the island. Within hours of the initial launches, the PLA deployed naval flotillas including Type 052D guided-missile destroyers and launched dozens of J-16 strike fighter sorties across the median line and deep into Taiwan’s southwestern and southeastern Air Defense Identification Zones (ADIZ), testing Taipei’s early warning radar baselines.

The military friction coincides with intense anxiety in Taipei over the diplomatic maneuvering surrounding the upcoming September 24 Trump-Xi summit. On Capitol Hill and within the Pentagon, persistent debate has raged over the massive U.S. arms delivery backlog to Taiwan. Tracked meticulously by organizations like the Cato Institute, the cumulative backlog of approved but undelivered American weaponry has stubbornly hovered around $21.5 billion. While the accelerated delivery of HIMARS and ATACMS provided a tangible capability boost, critical asymmetric staples—including ground-launched Harpoon coastal defense missile systems, F-16V Block 70/72 fighter airframes, and man-portable Stinger air-defense missiles—have faced repetitive delays linked to U.S. defense industrial base constraints, supply bottlenecks, and shifting prioritization.

The uncertainty is compounded by President Trump’s transactional posture toward Indo-Pacific allies. In interviews, Trump reiterated his view that Taiwan "should pay us for defense," explicitly analogizing the United States to an "insurance company" that receives nothing in return while alleging that Taiwan "took all of our chip business." The rhetoric has fueled anxiety among national security hawks in Congress that Taiwan could be positioned as a bargaining chip during the September 24 talks, where Beijing is expected to demand halts or pauses on major arms packages in exchange for bilateral trade commitments.

In response to pressure from Washington figures such as former National Security Advisor Robert O'Brien—who argued Taiwan must hike its defense spending to at least 5% of GDP—Taipei has aggressively escalated its fiscal commitments. With the passage of a recent NT$145.7 billion supplementary defense budget in late 2026, Taiwan’s overall defense spending surpassed the NT$1 trillion threshold for the first time, bringing defense expenditure to approximately 3.83% of its gross domestic product, moving toward the 5% benchmark advocated by U.S. defense strategists.

On social media and financial forums, the confluence of live-fire missile drills and summit diplomacy has sparked volatile debate. Following reports of the Jiupeng drills, discussions on Reddit’s r/geopolitics focused heavily on whether asymmetric systems alone can break a prolonged maritime blockade. One user pointed out that while loitering munitions and HIMARS deter amphibious hulls, they cannot replenish liquefied natural gas tankers if the PLA enforces a quarantine zone without firing a shot. Meanwhile, on X, defense analysts highlighted the tactical significance of the drone launches: as one prominent defense analyst noted, the ability of NCSIST to mass-produce cheap, containerized loitering munitions fundamentally degrades Beijing's ability to achieve a rapid, low-cost fait accompli.

The findings from CSIS's comprehensive wargame series, titled "Lights Out? Wargaming a Chinese Blockade of Taiwan," underscore this tactical tension. The CSIS simulations concluded that a PLA blockade would quickly deteriorate into a high-stakes, escalatory conflict requiring massive maritime convoy escorts. For Lai Ching-te, the Jiupeng exercises were designed to prove that Taiwan possesses the domestic industrial stamina and military will to hold the line—sending an unambiguous message to Beijing that an assault will be catastrophic, and to Washington that Taiwan is an armed, capable partner rather than a passive protectorate.
