# The 5% Treasury Paradox: How an $8B AWS Deal and Nebius Price Hikes Broke the Discount Rate

On Friday, September 18, 2026, the global capital markets presented a mathematical anomaly that defied three centuries of financial theory. The yield on the benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury note crossed 5.006%, its first close above the 5% threshold since July 2007, after touching an intraday peak of 5.04%. Two days earlier, on September 16, the Federal Open Market Committee delivered a unanimous 12–0 vote to raise the federal funds target range by 25 basis points to 3.75%–4.00%—the Fed's first interest rate hike since 2023—following an August Consumer Price Index report that revealed headline inflation accelerating to 3.4% year-over-year and 0.4% month-over-month, propelled by a 2.1% spike in energy. Standard discounted cash flow models dictate that a surge in the risk-free hurdle rate must crush the terminal value of long-duration growth assets. Instead, the tech-heavy NASDAQ 100 climbed +197.19 points, or +0.67%, to settle at 29,644.17, hovering near its daily high of 29,647.80, while the S&P 500 finished at 7,650.50.

This divergence has compressed the Equity Risk Premium (ERP)—the spread between corporate earnings yields and risk-free government paper—into negative territory, a phenomenon last witnessed during the peak of the dot-com bubble in 2000. With the S&P 500 trading at 24.7 times forward earnings, its implied earnings yield sits at 4.05%, creating a negative risk premium of -96 basis points against the 5.006% 10-year Treasury. For the NASDAQ 100, which trades at approximately 32.5 times forward earnings with an earnings yield of 3.08%, the ERP has collapsed to negative 193 basis points. Institutional asset allocators are effectively paying a 193-basis-point penalty to hold volatile technology equities over guaranteed government debt. The Shiller CAPE ratio, lingering north of 41, signals valuation multiples that have historically preceded sharp equity contractions. Yet equity investors have refused to liquidate.

The catalyst for this defiance lies in a wave of mega-cap artificial intelligence infrastructure deals that bulls argue have rendered Big Tech immune to traditional discount rates. On September 16, Generac Holdings (NYSE: GNRC) filed an 8-K disclosing a multi-year master supply agreement with Amazon Web Services (AWS) carrying a potential ceiling of $8.0 billion through September 2033. The contract guarantees an initial $2.4 billion in high-output backup diesel and gas generator deliveries across 2027 and 2028 to power AWS's expanding hyperscale data centers. To cement the alliance, Generac issued Amazon warrants to purchase 1,693,745 shares of common stock at an exact strike price of $200.9266 per share, with roughly 308,000 warrants vesting immediately upon execution. Generac's stock price rocketed 33% in pre-market trading on September 17 and gained over 40% across two trading sessions, adding billions to its market capitalization following a second-quarter earnings report that posted $1.17 billion in net sales.

Simultaneously, European AI infrastructure pure-play Nebius Group (NASDAQ: NBIS) provided concrete proof of extraordinary pricing power. Just weeks after posting Q2 2026 revenue of $582.3 million—a 454% year-over-year surge that generated $236.2 million in adjusted EBITDA against a $21 million operating loss in Q2 2025—Nebius notified cloud customers of its second major price hike in three months. Effective October 1, 2026, hourly rental rates for Nvidia H100 clusters will rise 16.9% from $3.85 to $4.50 per hour. High-demand Nvidia H200 instances will jump 20.0% from $4.50 to $5.40, while next-generation Blackwell B200 instances will climb 18.9% from $7.15 to $8.50 per hour. Top-tier B300 hardware will command $9.50 per hour, a 21.0% jump from $7.85. Complementary AMD EPYC Genoa compute instances will increase 25% to $0.015 per vCPU-hour, accompanied by a 41% hike in memory pricing to $0.0045 per GiB-hour. Chief Executive Officer Arkady Volozh explicitly told analysts that Nebius can "choose when to sell, to whom we sell, and on what terms," as the firm lifts its contracted capacity target to 5 gigawatts by the end of 2026 backed by an annualized run rate (ARR) of $3.0 billion.

These price increases and capex commitments have ignited a fierce debate across financial message boards and institutional trading desks. On Reddit's r/wallstreetbets, retail sentiment has embraced the narrative that hyper-growth eclipses macro gravity. "Who cares about a 5% discount rate when Nebius is compounding revenue at 454% year-over-year and hyperscalers are writing $8B blank checks for backup power?" argued one widely circulated post. Contrarian retail bears, however, pointed directly to the broader economic fallout of 5.04% yields, noting that 30-year fixed mortgage rates spiked to 7.17% within 48 hours of the Fed decision. Another user cautioned: "Treasuries should be scored like golf—the lower the yield, the better—and right now the bond market is screaming fire while tech trades on pure hopium." On X.com (formerly Twitter), macro strategists highlighted that enterprise buyers are paying 20% more for compute while the cost of funding those data centers has surged by 150 basis points year-to-date.

The macroeconomic tension reached Washington this week, pitting Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent against prominent Wall Street veterans. On September 15, testifying before the House Financial Services Committee, Secretary Bessent defended the Treasury's expanded liquidity-support bond buyback program—dubbed the "Bessent Twist"—which doubled operations from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per session starting September 9. Bessent claimed recent Treasury debt auctions were among the "most successful in 20 years," blaming climbing yields on the $40 trillion national debt and Middle East geopolitical shocks that drove crude prices upward. His defense prompted an immediate rebuke from his former mentor, billionaire investor Stanley Druckenmiller. In a Wall Street Journal commentary, Druckenmiller labeled the buyback strategy "price management" rather than genuine market liquidity, warning that "governments defending prices against fundamentals always lose" and reiterating that the 10-year yield remains the economy's "only remaining fiscal disciplinarian."

The financial stakes of this standoff cannot be overstated. If the S&P 500 equity risk premium were to normalize to its post-2008 historical median of +350 basis points over the 10-year Treasury yield, the implied earnings yield would need to surge from 4.05% to 8.51%. That shift would mandate a contraction in the benchmark's price-to-earnings multiple from 24.7x to 11.75x, representing an equity valuation decline of roughly 52%. Even if mega-cap tech companies maintain 25% annualized earnings growth through 2028, absorbing a persistent 5.006% Treasury yield requires flawless execution. The physical constraints revealed by Generac’s $8 billion generator backlog and Nebius’s 21% GPU price increases show that AI hyperscalers are no longer merely competing for digital market share—they are competing for constrained physical megawatts and capital in an economy where money is no longer free.
