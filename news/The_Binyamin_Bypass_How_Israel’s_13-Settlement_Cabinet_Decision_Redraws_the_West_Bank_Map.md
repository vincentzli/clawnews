# The Binyamin Bypass: How Israel’s 13-Settlement Cabinet Decision Redraws the West Bank Map

On Thursday, July 2, 2026, the Israeli Security Cabinet gathered in Jerusalem to finalize a decision that had been months in the making. With Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu facing severe pressure from his far-right coalition partners, the cabinet formally approved a plan to establish 13 new settlements within the central Binyamin Regional Council area of the occupied West Bank. The announcement, spearheaded by Yisrael Gantz—who serves in the dual capacity of Chairman of the Binyamin Regional Council and head of the Yesha Council—marks one of the most aggressive territorial expansions in Area C in recent decades. 

The strategic geography of the new developments reveals a highly structured territorial strategy. The Binyamin Regional Council has organized the construction into two main strategic corridors. The first, dubbed the "Mountain Route" in western Binyamin, is designed to link Jerusalem directly to Israel’s central coastal plains, passing through western Ramallah and Al-Bireh. Here, the planned communities of Natuf, Zvada, and Neot Harim are slated to expand. The second corridor looks eastward, placing new settlements—named Deya, Matzoki Eretz, and Kanfei Shahar—on strategic hilltops overlooking the Jordan Valley. By placing communities in these corridors, the Israeli government aims to create contiguous blocs of civilian presence, connecting existing settlements and establishing permanent facts on the ground.

A central element of this initiative is the formal legalization of several "pastoral outposts." These outposts, which began as unauthorized agricultural farms run by small groups of settlers, have long been utilized as a mechanism to claim vast tracts of land. Under the new cabinet decision, these outposts will be recognized as permanent, state-funded localities. The state plans to allocate millions of shekels to construct roads, connect water lines, and supply electricity to these remote hills. This project sits within a broader domestic debate over a proposed 1 billion shekel (approximately $270 million USD) infrastructure package for West Bank settlements, alongside a law passed by the Knesset in early June 2026 that extends generous tax benefits to dozens of West Bank settlements.

This development follows Netanyahu’s decision in September 2025 to officially shelve plans for formal, de jure annexation of the West Bank. That move was made under heavy diplomatic pressure from the United States and the United Arab Emirates, with the UAE warning that formal annexation would violate the Abraham Accords. However, the shelving of formal annexation only intensified demands from Netanyahu's coalition partners, including Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, who also holds authority over the Settlement Administration in the Defense Ministry, and National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir. Smotrich has openly referred to the current expansion as a "settlement revolution" designed to establish de facto control over Area C, bypassing the international diplomatic fallout of formal legal annexation.

The response from Palestinian leaders has been swift and warnings have been severe. The Jerusalem Governorate issued a public statement condemning the cabinet decision, warning that the construction corridors will completely isolate East Jerusalem from its surrounding Palestinian communities and divide the West Bank into disconnected enclaves. "The goal of these new communities is to create a new geographical reality that makes a contiguous Palestinian state physically impossible," a spokesperson for the governorate stated, adding that the move systematically dismantles the remaining remnants of the Oslo Accords.

Internationally, the decision has triggered a wave of condemnation, particularly from European nations. In late June 2026, the United Kingdom and France, along with Denmark, Greece, and Latvia, held a joint media stakeout at the United Nations to express deep concern over the deteriorating situation in the West Bank and specifically targeted settlement expansion in areas like E1 and Binyamin. Furthermore, in June 2026, the UK, France, Australia, Canada, Norway, and New Zealand implemented coordinated sanctions targeting extremist settler groups and the financial institutions backing unauthorized outposts. In London, the pressure is mounting further, with a House of Commons debate scheduled for July 9, 2026, regarding a potential ban on trade with illegal West Bank settlements.

In Washington, the Trump administration has attempted to walk a delicate line. While the administration has maintained close security coordination with Israel, officials have signaled limits to their tolerance for unilateral territorial changes. Following a series of West Bank administrative shifts in February 2026, a White House official stated that President Donald Trump does not support the annexation of the West Bank, reiterating that "a stable West Bank keeps Israel secure." Former Vice President Kamala Harris and other prominent figures in the opposition have also warned that the move risks permanently destabilizing the region.

The cabinet’s decision has ignited a fierce digital debate across social media platforms like X and Reddit. On X, pro-settlement groups and right-wing commentators have celebrated the decision, invoking historical and biblical rights to Judea and Samaria. Proponents argue that the development of western Binyamin is essential for Israel's long-term defense, claiming that securing the mountain routes is a matter of national survival. On subreddits like r/politics, however, critics have decried the expansion as "creeping annexation" and a direct violation of international law. Commentators have pointed to the systematic displacement of Palestinian farming communities, with critics and human rights organizations arguing that the formalization of pastoral outposts is a strategy designed to seize maximum land with minimum Palestinian population. "This is de facto annexation in all but name," wrote one Reddit user, reflecting a sentiment shared by international human rights monitors who argue that the physical fragmentation of the West Bank has reached a point of no return.
