# The Dual-Front Siege: Inside the Cyber Breaches and Russian Cognitive Campaigns Rattling France’s 2027 Presidential Race

On the afternoon of August 18, 2026, the French government convened an emergency crisis meeting to address a major cyberattack on the Directorate General of Public Finances (DGFiP) that resulted in the unauthorized extraction of personal data belonging to 678,438 taxpayers. Simultaneously, the Paris Public Prosecutor’s Office announced that its AC2 division, which specializes in press and public liberties, had formally initiated an inquiry into coordinated pro-Russian disinformation campaigns targeting key political figures ahead of the 2027 French presidential election. The simultaneous developments have sparked intense political debate across France, raising serious concerns over both cybersecurity and electoral integrity.

The judicial investigation follows complaints filed by former Prime Minister Gabriel Attal on August 7 and Member of the European Parliament Raphaël Glucksmann on August 12. Both figures, along with former Prime Minister Édouard Philippe, have been the targets of highly structured digital influence operations. With President Emmanuel Macron in the final year of his second term, the upcoming 2027 race is already highly contested. Attal, who serves as the general secretary of the Renaissance party and the leader of its parliamentary group, announced his presidential bid in May 2026. Philippe, the mayor of Le Havre and president of the Horizons party, has been campaigning actively since declaring his candidacy, recently holding a major national rally in Paris on July 5, 2026. Glucksmann, leading the center-left Place Publique party, has remained a prominent voice advocating for robust European military support for Ukraine.

The French digital watchdog VIGINUM has attributed the disinformation campaigns with a high level of confidence to two Russia-linked networks: Storm-1516 and Matriochka. According to VIGINUM’s monitoring, the campaign targeting Édouard Philippe utilized artificial intelligence to generate fabricated news articles and videos. These reports claimed that Philippe suffers from a terminal neurodegenerative disease, specifically targeting his public struggle with vitiligo and alopecia—autoimmune conditions causing skin depigmentation and hair loss—to make the false claims appear credible. 

Against Gabriel Attal, the Matriochka network circulated deepfakes and articles alleging he has Parkinson’s disease and a history of substance abuse. The campaign against Raphaël Glucksmann involved a cloned website mimicking the French investigative media outlet *Blast*. The site published forged documents claiming Glucksmann’s partner, journalist Léa Salamé, attempted to bribe editors at other investigative outlets, including Mediapart co-founder Edwy Plenel, with up to 50,000 euros to secure favorable coverage. Glucksmann filed his complaint after being briefed by the Secretariat General for National Defence and Security (SGDSN) on the foreign origin of the operation.

While the AC2 division investigates the press-related aspects of the election interference, French cyber defense authorities are addressing the DGFiP breach. A threat actor operating under the pseudonym "ZeroBytes" claimed responsibility for the intrusion on a cybercrime forum on August 12, later telling journalists via Telegram that they had already sold parts of the database to third parties.

Technical investigations by the National Cybersecurity Agency of France (ANSSI) revealed that the attackers gained access to internal systems during separate incidents in late June and late July 2026. ZeroBytes bypassed multi-factor authentication (MFA) protocols by using compromised credentials belonging to an authorized employee and a partner organization with VPN access. Although the DGFiP detected and closed the unauthorized access points, the agency did not immediately recognize that data had been stolen. The delay in reporting the breach prompted criticism from the Solidaires Finances Publiques trade union, which questioned the two-month gap between the initial intrusions and the public notification of taxpayers and the data protection authority, CNIL.

The compromised records contain taxpayers' names, reference taxable income (revenu fiscal de référence), family quotients, withholding tax rates, and cadastral land registry details. Security analysts warn that while user passwords for the impots.gouv.fr portal were not stolen, the precision of the leaked data significantly increases the risk of highly targeted spear-phishing campaigns and identity theft.

In a communique issued by its embassy in Paris, the Russian government denied any involvement in the incidents. The Russian Embassy described the accusations as "unfounded and gratuitous," asserting that French authorities are using the narrative of a "Russian threat" to distract from domestic political and economic issues and to justify increases in military spending.

The convergence of the disinformation campaigns and the tax database breach has fueled widespread public anxiety in France. Observers point to the dual threats of cognitive warfare and administrative cyber-sabotage as a major challenge for the democratic process. In the words of one citizen posting on X.com, "If the tax authority cannot secure our most private financial data, how can we trust the state to secure the vote?" The investigations by the Paris Prosecutor’s Office and ANSSI remain ongoing as France prepares for an election cycle increasingly defined by digital conflict.
