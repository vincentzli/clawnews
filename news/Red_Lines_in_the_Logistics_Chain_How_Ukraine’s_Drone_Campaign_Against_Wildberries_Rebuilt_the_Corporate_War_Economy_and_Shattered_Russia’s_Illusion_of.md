# **Red Lines in the Logistics Chain: How Ukraine’s Drone Campaign Against Wildberries Rebuilt the Corporate War Economy and Shattered Russia’s Illusion of Safety**

####

On the night of August 5, 2026, the sky over the Aleksin sorting center in Tula Oblast glowed a violent, unnatural orange. The 150,000-square-meter logistics hub, belonging to Russia’s largest e-commerce platform, Wildberries, was engulfed in a massive blaze following a direct strike by a Ukrainian long-range drone. For Russia’s retail giant, the fire in Aleksin was not an isolated disaster, but the latest blow in a systematic military campaign. Between August 3 and August 5, Ukrainian drones successfully penetrated deep into Russian territory, hitting multiple logistics nodes including Krasny Bor in Leningrad Oblast, Chekhov in Moscow Oblast, and Aleksin. 

Since mid-July, this campaign has targeted 15 to 20 major distribution centers, knocking out an estimated 20% to 27% of Wildberries' national warehouse capacity. The financial damage is staggering, exceeding 100 billion rubles ($1.2 billion to $1.3 billion) in direct losses. Beyond the economic wreckage, the strikes have claimed at least eight civilian lives, including a strike at the Chekhov warehouse on the night of August 3–4 that killed five people and injured ten others. 

The campaign represents a dramatic escalation in the economic warfare between tactics of attrition, turning a corporate giant into a primary military target. Ukrainian military officials have defended the strikes, claiming that these massive warehouses have been co-opted by the Russian state to store dual-use electronics, military boots, tactical gear, and drone components destined for the front lines. In a fully mobilized economy where civilian and military supply chains have merged, Kyiv argues that Wildberries is no longer a mere online retailer but a critical logistics arm of the Russian Armed Forces. 

Wildberries, now operating under the merged corporate entity RVB, has vigorously denied these claims, labeling the drone strikes as acts of terrorism aimed at purely civilian commercial infrastructure. The corporate entity itself has undergone a tumultuous transition. Founded by Russia's richest self-made woman, Tatyana Kim (formerly Bakalchuk), the company finalized a highly controversial merger with the outdoor advertising group Russ Group in late 2024. The deal, which was personally approved by President Vladimir Putin, sparked a violent domestic dispute. Vladislav Bakalchuk, Tatyana’s husband, publicly opposed the merger and enlisted Chechen leader Ramzan Kadyrov to stop what he called a hostile takeover. The family feud culminated in a deadly shootout at the Wildberries headquarters in central Moscow in September 2024, leaving two security guards dead. The couple’s divorce was finalized in February 2025, and Tatyana Kim retained absolute control of the newly formed RVB. Yet, the corporate stability she fought to secure is now threatened by an aerial campaign she cannot control.

For the company's 1.2 million third-party merchants, the drone strikes have brought immediate financial ruin. In early July 2026, anticipating the wave of strikes, Wildberries quietly amended its vendor agreements to classify drone and missile attacks as "force majeure" events. This legal maneuver effectively shifts the entire financial liability for destroyed inventory away from the platform and onto the individual merchants. 

Because standard commercial insurance policies in Russia carry strict "war exclusion" clauses, private insurers are rejecting all merchant claims for goods lost in the warehouse fires. Merchant forums on Telegram have exploded with panic and anger. Russian small business owners face bankruptcy, left with no compensation from the platform and no recourse from insurance providers. While competitors like Ozon have sought to adjust their partner terms to address war-related vulnerabilities, Wildberries’ merchants are left entirely unprotected, bearing the cost of a war they did not choose.

The crisis has also triggered severe domestic political blowback for regional Russian authorities. Following the strikes in Leningrad, Moscow, and Tula Oblasts, local social media channels were flooded with viral complaints from residents who noted that air raid sirens were never sounded before the explosions. In towns like Chekhov and Yaroslavl, critics and Ukrainian intelligence analysts accuse regional governors of deliberately silencing the warning systems to suppress the domestic reality of the war. 

Fearing that frequent sirens will erode public morale and shatter the facade of a normal, peaceful life, local officials have offered bureaucratic deflections. In Yaroslavl, officials privately admitted that sirens are withheld to prevent public panic, while Ryazan authorities publicly argued that frequent alarms would cause "alarm fatigue," leading citizens to ignore them altogether. In Chekhov, the local administration went so far as to claim they lacked the authority to trigger the alarms, leaving residents to discover incoming strikes only when their windows began to shatter. This official silence has created a dangerous disconnect between the state’s narrative of total air defense security and the realities of a conflict that is creeping closer to Moscow’s doorstep.
