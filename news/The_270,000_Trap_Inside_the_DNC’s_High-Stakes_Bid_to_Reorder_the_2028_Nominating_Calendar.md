# The $270,000 Trap: Inside the DNC’s High-Stakes Bid to Reorder the 2028 Nominating Calendar

On July 24, 2026, the Democratic National Committee’s (DNC) Rules and Bylaws Committee (RBC) voted 26 to 19 to approve a tentative 2028 presidential nominating calendar. Under the proposed schedule, South Carolina will lead off the primary season on January 22, 2028, followed by Nevada on February 1, New Hampshire on February 8, New Mexico on February 15, Michigan on February 22, and Virginia on February 29. By elevating racially diverse battleground states, national party leadership hopes to establish a primary window that reflects the modern Democratic coalition.

However, the committee's decision has instantly reignited an intense political standoff with New Hampshire. During the July 24 meeting, New Hampshire Democratic Party Chair Ray Buckley introduced a motion to restore the state's traditional first-in-the-nation primary status. The motion suffered a resounding defeat, securing only four votes out of the 49-member committee. Two of those votes came from New Hampshire’s own committee representatives, Buckley and Joanne Dowdell. 

The vote does not resolve the calendar dispute; instead, it sets up a direct collision course between the national party and state authorities. New Hampshire’s Secretary of State David Scanlan and Republican Governor Kelly Ayotte have pledged to defy the DNC. They cite a 106-year-old tradition dating back to 1920, backed by a 1975 state law (RSA 653:9) mandating that New Hampshire hold its primary first. State officials plan to schedule their contest at least seven days before any "similar election," which would push New Hampshire’s primary to January 15, 2028, or earlier, in direct violation of the DNC's calendar.

To prevent a repeat of the chaotic 2024 cycle, the DNC Rules and Bylaws Committee preemptively approved a series of aggressive rule changes in June 2026. Under the revised Rule 21 of the 2028 Delegate Selection Rules, the penalties for holding an unsanctioned primary have been significantly increased. State parties that violate the DNC's designated calendar will now be stripped of 100 percent of their delegates—up from the 50 percent penalty in 2024. Furthermore, any state party that hosts an unsanctioned early contest will face a direct monetary fine of $270,000, a heavy blow to the state party's operational budget.

The rules also target the presidential candidates themselves. Any candidate who campaigns in an unsanctioned primary, such as an early New Hampshire contest, will be prohibited from earning delegates in any of the early window states. The candidate will also be barred from participating in DNC-sponsored debates. In a new clause, the rules require candidates to publicly denounce any write-in or grassroots campaigns organized on their behalf in non-compliant states. 

This creates an acute strategic dilemma for the prospective 2028 Democratic field. A recent poll conducted by the UNH Survey Center between July 15 and July 20, 2026, among 648 likely Democratic primary voters, shows a highly competitive race in New Hampshire. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez leads the field with 22 percent, with Pete Buttigieg close behind at 21 percent. Other potential candidates trail, including Arizona Senator Mark Kelly at 9 percent, California Governor Gavin Newsom at 8 percent, Senator Bernie Sanders at 6 percent, and former Vice President Kamala Harris at 5 percent. 

For frontrunners like Ocasio-Cortez and Buttigieg, New Hampshire is a critical state for building early momentum. Yet, under the DNC’s June 2026 rules, entering the state risks disqualification from debates and the loss of delegates. In 2024, then-President Joe Biden avoided the New Hampshire ballot entirely to comply with DNC rules, forcing local allies to mount a costly write-in campaign. Biden ultimately won the unsanctioned primary with 63.79 percent of the vote, while challenger Dean Phillips received 19.66 percent. To seat their delegates at the 2024 convention, New Hampshire Democrats were forced to organize a separate, party-sanctioned "firehouse primary" on April 27, 2024. 

Under the 2028 rules, such write-in campaigns are effectively blocked, as candidates must actively denounce them or face sanctions. Candidates must choose between obeying the national party or respecting the local traditions of a key swing state. 

National party leaders defend the calendar as a necessary step forward. RBC Co-chairs Minyon Moore and James Roosevelt Jr. released a joint statement calling the proposal a "rigorous, fair, and efficient" process that represents the most diverse primary calendar in party history. Proponents argue that testing candidates in South Carolina, where Black voters make up a majority of the Democratic electorate, and Nevada, which has a large Latino population, is a better preparation for a general election than starting in New Hampshire, which is over 89 percent white.

New Hampshire Democrats see the move as a strategic blunder. Chair Ray Buckley warned that national party insiders "have learned nothing from 2024," arguing that the DNC is creating unnecessary division that will only benefit Republicans. Governor Kelly Ayotte has seized on the dispute, calling the DNC "completely wrong" and pledging to defend the state's first-in-the-nation status. For Republicans, the DNC's calendar fight provides a potent talking point to accuse Democrats of abandoning the state's voters ahead of the upcoming 2026 midterm elections and the 2028 general election. DNC Chair Ken Martin, currently facing internal party pressure over organization and finance, now must navigate a rebellion that threatens to fracture the party's nominating process.
