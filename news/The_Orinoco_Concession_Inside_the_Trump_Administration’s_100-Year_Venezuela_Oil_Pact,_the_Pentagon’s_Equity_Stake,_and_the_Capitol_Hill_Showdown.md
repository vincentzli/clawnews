# The Orinoco Concession: Inside the Trump Administration’s 100-Year Venezuela Oil Pact, the Pentagon’s Equity Stake, and the Capitol Hill Showdown

Nine months after Delta Force operators and the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment breached the perimeter of the Fuerte Tiuna military complex in Caracas to capture Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores, in the pre-dawn hours of January 3, 2026, the geopolitical aftermath of Operation Absolute Resolve has shifted from tactical battlefields to corporate balance sheets and constitutional confrontation. Rather than establishing a direct military occupation in Venezuela, the White House negotiated an administrative working relationship with Acting President Delcy Rodríguez. In late September 2026, that engagement produced an unprecedented resource agreement that has tested the boundaries of American foreign policy and administrative law: a 100-year concession granting North American Blue Energy Partners (NABEP)—a Barbados-domiciled firm led by Venezuelan businessman Alejandro Betancourt López—operating control over 17 oil fields in the Orinoco Petroleum Belt, an area holding an estimated 65 billion barrels of extra-heavy crude.

The agreement, described by administration officials as an initiative to secure Western Hemisphere energy dominance, has ignited an intense constitutional and statutory confrontation in Washington. Under terms structured during bilateral negotiations, the U.S. Department of Defense's Office of Strategic Capital (OSC) acquired a 35% passive equity stake in NABEP’s corporate parent through zero-strike "penny warrants." In parallel, the transaction guarantees the U.S. State Department preferential rights to acquire 20% of NABEP’s production at direct cost-of-production to refill the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, alongside a right of first refusal for the remaining 80%. Corporate governance covenants further dictate that a majority of NABEP’s board members must be U.S. citizens, with the U.S. government retaining negative veto authority over board selections.

On September 30, senior Senate Democrats opened a formal legislative challenge. In an exhaustive inquiry sent to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Secretary of Defense Pete Hegseth, and Secretary of Energy Chris Wright, Senators Jeanne Shaheen, Jack Reed, Elizabeth Warren, and Martin Heinrich contested the legal architecture underpinning the transaction. The lawmakers argued that Congress authorized the Office of Strategic Capital under Title 10 of the U.S. Code to extend concessional credit, loans, and loan guarantees to commercial entities manufacturing dual-use national security hardware—not to hold equity positions in offshore oil syndicates. Demanding the full unredacted agreements and formal legal opinions from agency general counsels, the senators warned that using defense-appropriated entities to hold commercial mineral assets bypasses Article I appropriations authority.

The prominent role of Betancourt has added political friction to the congressional inquiry. Formerly associated with Derwick Associates, which secured high-value Venezuelan electric power contracts during the presidency of Hugo Chávez, Betancourt has spent over a decade under scrutiny by European and American authorities examining cross-border financial transactions involving state oil entity PDVSA. While he has never been indicted or convicted of a criminal offense in the United States, his role as the commercial custodian of the concession prompted immediate skepticism on Capitol Hill. Public debate mirrored this friction; on Reddit’s r/politics, one widely circulated commentary stated: "The Pentagon just privatized an entire nation's reserves into a Barbados shell company run by a guy federal prosecutors spent ten years investigating."

Beyond the legal debate over administrative authority, the venture faces immediate operational headwinds within Venezuela. Industrial assessments reveal that the national electric grid suffers from an estimated 40% generation deficit, with functional supply hovering around 10,000 megawatts against an installed infrastructure designed for more than 30,000 megawatts.

In the Orinoco Basin, where crude deposits consist of extra-heavy bitumen registering between 8 and 10 degrees API, extraction requires constant electrical power for steam injection, artificial lift, and mechanical blending with light diluents. Without dependable power, bitumen solidifies rapidly within gathering conduits. Technical evaluations estimate that restoring electrical stability to the production fields, overhauling pipeline corridors, and rehabilitating the industrial export terminals at the Jose Antonio Anzoátegui Complex and the Puerto La Cruz refinery will necessitate $14 billion in front-loaded infrastructure repairs before NABEP can approach its target output of 1 million barrels per day.

These operational constraints are compounded by regional instability. In Anzoátegui and Monagas states, armed Chavista factions and irregular syndicates have targeted distribution pipelines and pumping hubs, framing the 100-year concession as an illegal forfeiture of sovereign resources. Because the United States refrained from deploying regular combat forces to secure extraction infrastructure, NABEP executives and Pentagon planners have evaluated contracting armed private security companies to establish defensive cordons around refining and maritime loading nodes at Jose and Puerto La Cruz.

The contemplation of commercial security deployments to protect a venture in which the Department of Defense holds direct equity has raised questions regarding the 1973 War Powers Resolution. Senior committee staff have raised concerns that positioning heavily armed American contractors to defend U.S.-owned mineral holdings against continuous insurgent harassment creates a de facto military commitment that triggers statutory reporting requirements.

The intersection of military strategy, classified planning, and financial opportunism had already come under judicial scrutiny earlier this year. In April 2026, federal prosecutors and the Commodity Futures Trading Commission indicted Master Sergeant Gannon Ken Van Dyke, an active-duty Special Forces operator assigned to the special operations planning element for Operation Absolute Resolve. According to the indictment, Van Dyke misappropriated classified operational timetables to place roughly $34,000 in event contracts on the decentralized prediction exchange Polymarket between December 30, 2025, and January 2, 2026, wagering that Maduro would be deposed. The positions generated more than $400,000 in net gains before the operation became public. Van Dyke has entered a plea of not guilty and is released on a $250,000 bond. On platforms like r/wallstreetbets, traders pointed to the case as evidence of systemic gray-zone commercialization, with one post observing: "A Green Beret literally front-ran an airborne raid on Polymarket, and now the Pentagon is doing equity swaps on the oil fields."

Administration officials continue to defend the arrangement, arguing that commercial concessions backed by defensive equity rights represent an agile foreign policy tool to displace Russian and Chinese influence from the Orinoco Belt while replenishing U.S. reserves at historically low pricing. Yet with Senate committee oversight accelerating, legal challenges mounting under Title 10, and Venezuela’s battered electrical infrastructure threatening field operations, Washington’s 100-year oil concession remains entangled in deep legal and physical uncertainty.
