# The Anatomy of a Collapse: How Wildfire Fallout, a $1.3B Homeless Dilemma, and Budget Austerity Pushed Karen Bass to the Brink

On an unseasonably warm late September afternoon, the parking lot of a commercial corridor along Santa Monica Boulevard in West Los Angeles offered a stark preview of the political reckoning confronting Mayor Karen Bass. Four years ago, this council district delivered 68 percent of its vote to Bass, providing the affluent, moderate-liberal Westside ballast that propelled her into City Hall over developer Rick Caruso. Today, the campaign signs promoting the former congresswoman’s consensus leadership have largely vanished, replaced by the orange-and-teal placards of progressive City Councilmember Nithya Raman.

The shift is no longer merely anecdotal; it is quantified by the most rigorous independent data available. According to a UC Berkeley Institute of Governmental Studies (IGS) poll co-sponsored by the *Los Angeles Times*, surveyed among 2,150 likely voters between September 15 and September 20, Raman leads the incumbent mayor by 11 percentage points, 39 percent to 28 percent, with 33 percent of the electorate undecided heading into the November 3 runoff. Even more precarious for City Hall is Bass’s approval metric: 63 percent of likely voters view her job performance unfavorably, against just 28 percent who approve. In modern Los Angeles history, where incumbent mayors routinely secure reelection by comfortable margins, Bass has found herself fighting an uphill battle against a challenger from her own party’s left.

The erosion of the coalition that delivered Bass’s victory in November 2022—an alliance uniting South Los Angeles African American communities, traditional public-sector labor, and Westside moderates—is directly traceable to administrative friction and infrastructure failures. At the center of this collapse sits the trauma of the January 2025 Palisades Fire.

On January 7, 2025, Santa Ana winds exceeding 70 miles per hour reignited a holdover brush fire, driving a 23,000-acre blaze through the coastal canyons. The disaster destroyed nearly 7,000 structures, claimed 12 lives, and leveled entire neighborhoods in Pacific Palisades. Political controversy ignited immediately when it was revealed that Mayor Bass was out of the country on an official trade and diplomatic mission in Ghana, returning to Los Angeles hours after the outbreak on a military transport.

Beyond the optics of executive absence, municipal infrastructure collapsed under pressure. LAFD officials reported that approximately 20 percent of hydrants in the burn zone lost water pressure or ran dry during peak fire-suppression efforts. Subsequent investigations revealed that the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power (LADWP) had taken the 117-million-gallon Santa Ynez Reservoir offline for long-term bypass and cover repairs, which, combined with power outages that disabled hillside booster pumps, crippled water availability. 

On February 21, 2025, Mayor Bass removed Fire Chief Kristin Crowley, citing leadership deficiencies, a failure to pre-deploy sufficient strike teams, and the dismissal of 1,000 off-duty firefighters on the eve of the windstorm. Crowley responded by filing a comprehensive whistleblower retaliation and personal defamation lawsuit against the city and Bass, alleging the mayor fabricated charges to deflect scrutiny from her overseas absence and the city’s unaddressed infrastructure deficits. For Westside voters who had supported Bass as a seasoned administrative steward, the litigation and the charred ruins of the Palisades created an irreparable breach of trust.

While the wildfire damaged Bass's standing across the city's coastal perimeter, the mounting financial exhaustion surrounding her signature homelessness initiative, Inside Safe, compromised her base among both progressives and fiscal conservatives.

Enacted under an emergency declaration on Bass’s first full day in office, Inside Safe aimed to clear conspicuous encampments by transitioning unhoused Angelenos into rented motels with on-site supportive services. Across four budget cycles, the city dedicated over $1.3 billion toward emergency homelessness responses, including $104 million preserved for Inside Safe in the 2026–27 municipal budget. Yet independent oversight repeatedly highlighted acute structural inefficiencies. Following a prolonged dispute between the Mayor’s office and City Controller Kenneth Mejia over performance auditing authority, U.S. District Judge David O. Carter ordered an external audit conducted by Alvarez & Marsal, costing taxpayers nearly $3 million. 

The findings substantiated critics' warnings: annual expenditures reached up to $85,000 per motel room, client records and service-provider invoices suffered from chronic accounting discrepancies, and permanent housing exits remained rare. More than 40 percent of individuals housed in motels eventually returned to unsheltered conditions. 

Raman, an MIT-trained urban planner representing Council District 4 who won a difficult reelection in 2024, made those balance sheets the core of her platform. "Right now we have Inside Safe having a lot of people who are in there for a year or more," Raman stated during a broadcast mayoral forum. "That’s like having someone in an emergency room for a year." Proposing to redirect capital toward master-leasing apartment buildings, direct rental subsidies, and expanded permanent supportive housing, Raman argued that Bass spent more than a billion dollars on temporary hotel rooms that functioned as an expensive holding pattern rather than a systemic cure.

These program expenditures collided directly with a severe municipal budget crisis. Facing a structural deficit exceeding $400 million—part of a projected fiscal gap that had approached $1 billion—the City Council and the Mayor implemented an austere hiring freeze, eliminating more than 2,000 vacant civil service positions. The resulting personnel cuts degraded day-to-day services across Los Angeles: street repaving schedules fell behind, tree-trimming backlogs climbed past three years, and street-lighting maintenance crews were curtailed.

At the same time, the fiscal fallout of Bass’s 2023 labor agreement with the Los Angeles Police Department intensified budget scrutiny. The contract provided compounded wage increases of approximately 13 percent over four years, substantially expanding the general fund's fixed costs. Despite the pay increases, LAPD sworn staffing continued to drop, falling to roughly 8,800 officers—the lowest department headcount in two decades. Moderates criticized the administration for lengthening 911 response times, while progressive civic groups argued that protecting police budgets while gutting civilian service divisions hollowed out core city investments.

Hovering over this municipal strain is anxiety regarding the 2028 Summer Olympic Games. In August 2024, following the Paris Olympics, Bass proclaimed that Los Angeles would host a "car-free" Games, relying on an auxiliary fleet of 1,750 to 3,000 transit buses borrowed from agencies across the United States. 

With two years remaining, the transit master plan faces an acute capital shortfall. L.A. Metro’s formal request for $2 billion in federal transit subsidies has stalled in Washington, and internal reports show less than 6 percent of designated Olympic transit infrastructure projects are funded. Relations with the federal government are fraught: President Donald Trump established the "Los Angeles Olympic Federal Task Force" by executive order, appointing himself chair and Vice President J.D. Vance vice chair, while publicly warning that federal security clearances and event authorizations could be withheld over the city’s homelessness crisis. Coupled with the California Legislature's refusal to pass supplementary municipal transit funding, and a City Charter stipulation that mandates Los Angeles cover the first $270 million of any Olympic operating deficit, the Games have evolved from a civic milestone into a major fiscal risk.

This matrix of crises has fractured the electoral coalition that carried Bass into office. In the June 2, 2026 primary, Bass secured only 34 percent of the vote against Raman’s 31 percent in a crowded 14-candidate field. As the runoff approaches, Raman has consolidated renters, younger voters under 40, Eastside and Central progressive networks, and a significant bloc of disaffected Westside property owners. While Bass maintains the formal endorsements of organized labor unions such as SEIU Local 721 and solid institutional backing among older Black voters in South Los Angeles, voter participation in those strongholds is dampened by frustration over reduced neighborhood services.

With weeks remaining before the November 3 vote, the Bass campaign has intensified attacks, seeking to frame Raman as an untested policymaker whose proposals could further destabilize city finances and policing. Yet in an election cycle defined by wildfire fallout, high-cost homeless motel programs, and structural municipal deficits, Karen Bass must defend a four-year governance record against an electorate that is increasingly questioning the return on its investment.
