# The Coordinated Cash Flood: Inside the Supreme Court's Reshaping of American Campaign Finance

On June 30, 2026, the United States Supreme Court delivered a landmark ruling in *National Republican Senatorial Committee (NRSC) et al. v. Federal Election Commission (FEC)*, voting 6-3 to strike down federal limits on coordinated campaign spending between political parties and their candidates. The decision dismantles a central pillar of the nation’s campaign finance architecture, permitting national and state party committees to spend unlimited sums in direct, synchronized coordination with their candidates. 

Writing for the majority, Justice Brett Kavanaugh declared that the coordinated-expenditure limits in the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) violate the First Amendment. Kavanaugh’s opinion explicitly overruled the 2001 precedent established in *FEC v. Colorado Republican Federal Campaign Committee* (*Colorado II*), which had restricted how much parties could spend in tandem with candidate campaigns. Kavanaugh argued that base contribution limits and modern digital disclosure requirements are sufficient to prevent *quid pro quo* corruption without restricting a political party’s core freedom of speech. He characterized the additional coordinated spending caps as a "prophylaxis upon prophylaxis," an unnecessary restriction that burdens political communication. Kavanaugh noted that "disclosure has become a much stronger anticircumvention tool over time because of 'modern technology,' especially the internet."

In a dissenting opinion joined by Justices Sonia Sotomayor and Ketanji Brown Jackson, Justice Elena Kagan warned that the majority had opened a massive loophole that would unleash coordinated dark money directly into candidate campaigns. "The law's theory is simple," Kagan wrote. "A candidate may be induced to trade official acts for campaign contributions—and the bigger the contribution, the stronger both the candidate's temptation and the public's suspicion." She argued that by dismantling these coordinated spending caps, "the Court ushers back in the same opportunities for *quid pro quo* corruption that the contribution limits were meant to check," enabling a party "to serve as an alternative checking account for a campaign." Criticizing the majority's reliance on disclosure, she added, "To count on disclosure to prevent corruption is as much as to give up on the goal itself... Which is, sad to say, what this Court does today."

The case originated in late 2022 when the NRSC, the National Republican Congressional Committee (NRCC), and candidates including then-Senate candidate J.D. Vance of Ohio—now the Vice President of the United States—and Representative Steve Chabot sued the FEC. The campaign committees argued that the statutory limits on coordinated party expenditures severely restricted their ability to run cohesive campaigns. Under the old rules, a party could only spend a capped amount—ranging from approximately $65,000 for a House candidate to a few million dollars for a Senate candidate in a large state—in coordination with the campaign on advertising, polling, and staff. Any party spending beyond those caps had to be done via "independent expenditures," which legally barred the party from consulting with the campaign on messaging, timing, or strategy.

The strategic implications of the *NRSC* ruling are poised to reshape the 2028 presidential and congressional elections, reversing a decade-long trend that saw political influence migrate toward independent Super PACs. Since the 2010 *Citizens United* ruling, wealthy donors have poured billions of dollars into Super PACs because these outside groups can accept unlimited contributions. However, Super PACs suffer from two major operational disadvantages: they are legally barred from coordinating with the candidates they support, and they must pay standard commercial market rates for media advertising.

Under the new regime, formal party committees can now leverage their relationship with candidates to spend unlimited sums in direct coordination. Crucially, candidate campaigns are entitled under Federal Communications Commission (FCC) rules to the "lowest unit charge" (LUC) for broadcast ads in the weeks leading up to an election. This regulatory discount allows candidates to buy airtime for a fraction of the cost paid by outside groups—often three to four times cheaper. If party coordinated spending is allowed without limit, campaigns can stretch their advertising dollars farther by routing money through party committees rather than independent Super PACs. RNC Chairman Joe Gruters welcomed the decision, while NRSC Chairman Tim Scott indicated that the ruling would restore operational flexibility by allowing candidate campaigns and parties to speak with one voice. Democratic campaign chairs, including DSCC Chair Kirsten Gillibrand and DCCC Chair Suzan DelBene, must now navigate this deregulated environment as they map out strategies for the upcoming midterms and the 2028 presidential cycle.

The fundraising landscape is also set to shift. The ruling enables political parties to raise massive sums through Joint Fundraising Committees (JFCs). JFCs allow a candidate, the national party, and multiple state party committees to solicit a single, large contribution from a donor, which is then split according to a formula among the participating entities. In the 2024 cycle, committees like the Biden Victory Fund and the Trump 47 Committee collected individual checks exceeding $800,000 by aggregating maximum contribution limits. 

Previously, while a JFC could raise millions, the party could only spend a tiny fraction of those funds in direct coordination with the candidate. The rest had to be spent independently or shifted elsewhere. Now, state parties can transfer their JFC proceeds to the national party committee without any limits, and the national committee can turn around and spend the entire amount directly on coordinated television ads, campaign staff, and polling in tight congressional districts. 

Advocacy groups and campaign finance reform organizations have reacted with alarm. The Campaign Legal Center, which filed an amicus brief supporting the FEC, stated that the ruling damages the integrity of elections. The Brennan Center for Justice warned that the decision effectively allows political parties to act as conduits for wealthy donors to bypass candidate-specific contribution limits by funneling huge sums through party committees with the understanding that the funds will be used to support a specific candidate. 

As the 2028 election cycle begins to take shape under President Donald Trump's administration, campaign finance lawyers are already advising clients on how to establish mega-JFCs. Meanwhile, Democratic-aligned groups have filed lawsuits challenging the applicability of the lowest unit charge to coordinated party advertisements, setting up the next major legal battleground. For now, the Supreme Court has cleared the way for a centralization of financial power back to the formal party machinery, leaving the era of pure Super PAC dominance in question.
