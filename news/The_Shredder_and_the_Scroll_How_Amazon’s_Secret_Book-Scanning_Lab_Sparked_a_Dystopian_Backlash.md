# The Shredder and the Scroll: How Amazon’s Secret Book-Scanning Lab Sparked a Dystopian Backlash

Inside Amazon's LAS8 fulfillment center in North Las Vegas, Nevada, a quiet industrial line operates under the internal designation VGT3. The work here is highly specialized and systematically destructive. Shipping containers full of physical books arrive daily, purchased in bulk from used bookstores and independent dealers across the country. Workers at VGT3 perform a repetitive sequence: they scan each book's ISBN barcode, use industrial machinery to slice off the spine, feed the loose pages into high-speed scanners, and then send the shredded remains of the physical copies to be recycled or discarded. The operation is marked by a logo at the facility’s entrance that features a Tyrannosaurus rex clutching an open book between its teeth, an image that observers have noted is a remarkably literal representation of the project's purpose.

The VGT3 project remained hidden from the public until mid-August 2026, when 404 Media co-founder Emanuel Maiberg partnered with an independent used bookseller to investigate a series of mysterious bulk orders. The bookseller had received a non-negotiated purchase order for approximately 1,000 distinct titles through the online used-book marketplace Biblio. Suspecting the buyer was an AI company looking for training data, they slipped an Apple AirTag into the binding of a rare, out-of-print historical volume. Over the following weeks, the AirTag pinged its way from a warehouse in California, through distribution hubs in Kenosha, Wisconsin, and Colorado, before finally settling at the north end of Amazon’s LAS8 facility.

This high-speed digitization and destruction pipeline is the direct result of a looming technical crisis in the artificial intelligence industry known as "model collapse." Research published by computer scientists at Oxford and Cambridge universities has demonstrated that when large language models are trained on data generated by other AI models—which now saturates the public internet—they suffer recursive degradation. Within a few generations, the outputs become garbled and unusable. To prevent this, developers are engaged in an aggressive search for "clean," human-written, pre-2022 text. The most reliable repository of such data exists in printed books, many of which have never been digitized.

Yet the decision to buy, scan, and destroy physical books rather than licensing digital copies or scraping online databases is driven by a critical legal loophole. In the landmark copyright lawsuit *Bartz v. Anthropic PBC*, unsealed in 2025, Judge William Alsup of the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California ruled that digitizing *lawfully purchased* physical books for internal AI model training constitutes transformative "fair use." In contrast, training models on pirated digital text sourced from shadow libraries like Books3 and Library Genesis was ruled to be copyright infringement. In August 2025, Anthropic agreed to pay a $1.5 billion settlement to resolve the claims regarding pirated data, but Judge Alsup's ruling left the door wide open for physical book acquisition. By purchasing books through commercial channels, tech companies can legally scan their contents without paying licensing fees to authors or publishers, so long as they destroy the physical copies to prevent them from re-entering the market and violating copyright distribution rights.

This commercial scanning loop has triggered a gold rush in the used book market, to the alarm of preservationists and booksellers. Because VGT3 targets out-of-print, historically significant, and rare works that contain unique language patterns, booksellers fear that rare or single-edition cultural works are being permanently erased from existence. Amazon’s official response did not address the physical destruction of the books, stating only: "Amazon purchases books through commercial channels to help develop and improve the products and services our customers use."

The revelation of the VGT3 program has ignited a fierce public backlash. On platforms like Reddit, in communities such as r/technology and r/books, users have compared the operation to a modern corporate equivalent of book burning. "They are literally destroying physical history to feed a corporate statistics engine," one user wrote. In an ironic twist, forum members pointed out that the T-Rex logo displayed at the VGT3 facility is riddled with AI-generated visual artifacts, including distorted letters and extra claws, symbolizing the circular nature of the technology. Preservationists and independent booksellers argue that this practice prioritizes corporate data acquisition at the expense of cultural conservation.

In response to the public outcry, a coalition of civil society organizations led by the Demand Progress Education Fund, the Consumer Federation of America, and the Institute for Local Self-Reliance took formal action. On August 21, 2026, the coalition submitted a petition to the Federal Trade Commission, urging FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson and Commissioner Mark Meador to launch an antitrust and consumer protection investigation. The petition argues that the "hoard-and-destroy" practice violates Section 5 of the FTC Act by engaging in unfair methods of competition. By systematically purchasing and destroying physical copies of rare and out-of-print books, the groups argue that Amazon is creating an insurmountable "systemic moat" that denies smaller rivals and public researchers access to the non-renewable training inputs necessary to build competing AI models.

The online comparisons of the VGT3 facility to historic book burnings underscore the intensity of the public debate surrounding the program. As the FTC reviews the petition, the conflict between corporate AI development and cultural preservation has reached a critical bottleneck. The coming months will determine whether the physical records of human knowledge can be legally bought and shredded to build proprietary digital monopolies, or if regulators will step in to protect the shared cultural heritage of the pre-AI era.
