# **The Ankara Ultimatum: How Trump’s Iran Feud and 'Conventional Retrenchment' are Forcing a 'Europeanized' NATO**

##

When the thirty-sixth NATO summit convened on July 7, 2026, in Ankara, Turkey, the atmosphere inside the Bilkent Congress Center was anything but united. It was the first time the alliance had met in Turkey since the 2004 Istanbul Summit, and the geographic shift to NATO's Southern Flank mirrored a deeper tectonic shift in the alliance’s power balance. Behind the closed-door sessions, the administration of U.S. President Donald Trump issued a stark warning to its European allies: accelerate compliance with the aggressive 5% GDP defense spending target by 2035, or face a systemic withdrawal of American conventional military assets. 

The roots of the current crisis trace back to April 2026, when German Chancellor Friedrich Merz publicly criticized the Trump administration’s military operations against Iran. The U.S.-led campaign, which had escalated since February 2026 with airstrikes on Iranian energy infrastructure and a naval blockade, faced stiff opposition from Berlin. Chancellor Merz remarked during a press briefing that the United States was being "humiliated" by Iran’s leadership in negotiations, suggesting Washington had lost its strategic footing. President Trump, who recently paused a major strike on Iranian energy facilities on August 1, 2026, following diplomatic appeals from Gulf allies, reacted with fury. Within weeks, the Pentagon announced a planned drawdown of 5,000 U.S. troops from bases in Germany, a move widely characterized by European officials as a direct act of retaliation.

For decades, the U.S. military presence in Germany, particularly the Landstuhl Regional Medical Center and Ramstein Air Base, has served as the logistical backbone for U.S. power projection in the Middle East and Europe. Former President Joe Biden had previously sought to reinforce these bases, but the current administration’s decision to execute a drawdown over the next six to twelve months has sent shockwaves through the alliance. Senator Jack Reed, Chairman of the Senate Armed Services Committee, criticized the drawdown as a "reckless" move that compromises U.S. national security and plays directly into Moscow's hands. Conversely, German Defense Minister Boris Pistorius attempted to downplay the impact, calling the drawdown "foreseeable" and noting that he had long warned that the U.S. would pivot its conventional focus to the Indo-Pacific. Pistorius argued that the troop reduction should serve as a wake-up call for Europe to take immediate responsibility for its own conventional defense.

But Trump’s leverage is legally constrained. In December 2023, Congress passed the National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) for Fiscal Year 2024, which included a bipartisan amendment authored by Senators Tim Kaine and Marco Rubio. The law strictly prohibits the President from unilaterally suspending, terminating, or withdrawing U.S. membership in NATO without a two-thirds Senate majority or an Act of Congress. If the President attempts to bypass this, the statute cuts off federal funding for the withdrawal and permits Congress to hire independent legal counsel to block the move in federal court. 

Faced with this congressional wall, the administration has pivoted to what analysts describe as a strategy of "conventional retrenchment." Rather than exiting the alliance, the U.S. is squeezing its allies by restricting allocations of advanced conventional military hardware, including F-35 fighter jets, conventional submarines, and surveillance drones. This policy is governed by the "America First Arms Transfer Strategy," established in February 2026, and reinforced by a July 2026 executive order that severely restricts supply-chain waivers for foreign components. 

To offset the loss of American conventional guarantees, the Trump administration has pressed allies to meet the "Hague Investment Plan," a defense spending target of 5% of GDP by 2035, which was agreed to at the June 2025 summit in The Hague. The mandate is divided into a 3.5% allocation for core military requirements and 1.5% for broader resilience investments, such as cyber defense, infrastructure protection, and industrial capacity. While Spain secured a formal exemption from the 5% target, other allies are struggling to find the necessary capital. Canada has proposed the creation of a Defense, Security and Resilience Bank (DSRB) to provide low-cost financing for defense infrastructure, but the economic strain remains severe. 

At the same time, the administration is shifting its European security commitment from conventional forces to the nuclear umbrella. NATO officials are currently briefing members on plans to expand nuclear-sharing arrangements. This proposal includes stationing U.S. nuclear-capable bombers and tactical weapons further east, targeting Poland and the Baltic states, to reassure allies concerned about the drawdown of conventional U.S. forces. 

This transactional approach has triggered a fierce debate across the continent and on platforms like Reddit's r/europe. Many users argue that the U.S. drawdown is a political maneuver designed to force European procurement of U.S. arms, while others raise alarms over the local economic impact on German base towns. In response to the perceived unreliability of Washington, European strategic autonomy is moving from rhetoric to reality. In March 2026, France announced a significant shift in its nuclear doctrine, increasing the size of its independent nuclear deterrent force and seeking closer coordination with European allies on escalation control. As the Ankara Summit demonstrated, the era of the U.S. serving as Europe's primary conventional shield is drawing to a close, forcing the alliance into a fragmented, "Europeanized" future.
