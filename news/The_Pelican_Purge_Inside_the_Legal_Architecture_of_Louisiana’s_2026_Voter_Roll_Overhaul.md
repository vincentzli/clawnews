# The Pelican Purge: Inside the Legal Architecture of Louisiana’s 2026 Voter Roll Overhaul

BATON ROUGE — In the sterile, high-ceilinged corridors of the Louisiana State Capitol, the mechanics of democracy are being stripped down and rebuilt with clinical precision. As the 2026 midterm elections approach, Louisiana has become the primary laboratory for a nationwide movement to "harden" election infrastructure. But beneath the rhetoric of "election integrity" lies a complex web of legislative mandates, executive decrees, and a widening financial chasm that plaintiffs in federal court argue is designed to disenfranchise the state's minority and elderly populations.

At the center of this transition is Secretary of State Nancy Landry and a duo of legislative acts—SB 155 and SB 226—that have fundamentally altered the state’s voting landscape. SB 155, now codified as Act 380, introduced the state’s first-ever mandatory signature-matching protocol for absentee-by-mail ballots. The law requires registrars to compare signatures on ballot envelopes against historical registration records, a process that civil rights groups warn is prone to subjective error, particularly for voters whose handwriting may shift due to age or illness.

"If the signature is determined not to match, the ballot is challenged," says one parish election official. "The 'cure' process requires the registrar to notify the voter, but with a mail system under strain, the window for a voter to verify their identity before the deadline is extremely narrow."

Complementing this is SB 226 (Act 317), which tightened regulations on ballot handling. The law restricts absentee ballot witnessing to immediate family members or legal guardians, effectively ending the role of civic organizations that historically assisted homebound seniors. Violations are now classified as election offenses, adding a layer of legal risk to traditional community outreach.

The legislative push is reinforced by the executive branch. Governor Jeff Landry, an ally of President Trump’s administration, has utilized executive orders to drive policy. Executive Order JML 24-21 mandated that state agencies display disclaimers stating that only U.S. citizens may register to vote. More critically, in May 2026, the Governor issued EO JML 26-038 to suspend U.S. House primaries following a Supreme Court-ordered redistricting dispute—an "emergency" declaration that triggered an immediate federal lawsuit, *Bernard v. Landry*, from the ACLU and the League of Women Voters.

The financial engine of these changes was restricted in late 2023 when Louisiana voters passed Amendment 1, the "Zuckerbucks" ban. The amendment prohibited local election offices from accepting private grants. While supporters like Ken Cuccinelli of the Election Transparency Initiative hailed the move for preventing "privatized elections," the reality for some parishes is an operational strain. One rural parish was blocked from receiving approximately $114,000 in grant money, leaving officials to rely on state appropriations that have not always filled the void for temporary staffing and equipment maintenance.

"Elections should never be privatized in Louisiana or anywhere else," Cuccinelli stated. However, on social media, the reaction is more polarized. On X.com, conservative accounts have celebrated the ban as a win for "sovereignty" and "preventing billionaire interference." Conversely, voting rights advocates warn the ban is a "coordinated effort to starve local election offices," which could lead to "longer lines and slower results" in the 2026 midterms.

The most aggressive facet of the overhaul involves the use of the federal SAVE (Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements) database. Nancy Landry’s office has moved thousands of voters to "inactive" status in early 2026. The NAACP alleges these purges violate the National Voter Registration Act’s (NVRA) "quiet period," which prohibits systematic removals within 90 days of a federal election. The state has countered with a lawsuit against the U.S. Election Assistance Commission (EAC), asserting that the NVRA is unconstitutional if it prevents states from enforcing citizenship requirements via state-specific registration instructions.

For Secretary Nancy Landry, the mission is defensive. "We are ensuring that every legal vote counts and that the rolls are as clean as possible," she has maintained. But as the 2026 primary season enters its most volatile phase, the tension between administrative rigor and democratic access remains unresolved. The Pelican State is no longer just holding an election; it is litigating the very definition of who is permitted to participate in one.
