# The $370 Billion Conservatorship Gridlock: Inside Scott Fitzgerald’s Legislative Gambit to Privatize Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac

On June 25, 2026, U.S. Representative Scott Fitzgerald (R-WI) introduced H.R. 9460, the Sustainable Homeownership Act, launching a high-stakes legislative bid to end the federal conservatorship of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac. The two government-sponsored enterprises (GSEs) have remained under the control of the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) since their rescue at the height of the 2008 financial crisis. Fitzgerald’s bill is the anchor of a three-bill housing package aimed at returning the GSEs to private ownership. The other two bills, H.R. 9461 (the Working Families Home Construction Act of 2026) and H.R. 9459 (the Home Affordability Through Mortgage Simplification Act), target housing supply shortages and compliance friction in lending, respectively. While industry groups like the U.S. Mortgage Insurers (USMI) support the legislative package for its focus on taxpayer protection and private capital, the bill has sparked an intense backlash from common shareholders on investment forums like InvestorsHub and ADVFN, who argue that the proposed restructuring would result in a massive dilution of their holdings.

H.R. 9460 outlines a statutory blueprint for a post-conservatorship housing finance system backed by private capital, but it imposes significant operational restrictions. The bill proposes to limit the growth of the GSEs' balance sheets, prohibit the use of "piggyback mortgages" (where secondary loans are taken out to cover down payments), and tie conforming loan limits directly to local household income rather than home-price appreciation. This indexing change is designed to suppress home-price inflation by capping conforming loan sizes relative to local median household income rather than tracking broader housing market gains. Simultaneously, H.R. 9461 requires the GSEs to purchase and securitize construction loans to lower financing costs for homebuilders, though it limits this support to properties sold to buyers earning between 90% and 130% of the local median income. These construction projects are capped at $2.4 million per project or $100,000 per dwelling unit, with a requirement that the builder contribute at least 10% of the capital. Meanwhile, H.R. 9459 streamlines the Truth in Lending Act (TILA) and Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act (RESPA) Integrated Disclosure (TRID) framework, shielding lenders from liability for minor, non-material technical disclosure errors that frequently cause delays in mortgage closings.

Despite the legislative detail, the immediate hurdle for a near-term initial public offering (IPO) remains a massive capital deficit. Under the FHFA’s Enterprise Regulatory Capital Framework (ERCF), the enterprises must build and maintain substantial risk-based and leverage capital buffers before they can be released. In the first quarter of 2026, both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac continued to report strong earnings. Fannie Mae’s net worth reached $112.7 billion as of March 31, 2026, supported by $3.7 billion in quarterly net income, while Freddie Mac’s net worth climbed to $73.9 billion following a $3.6 billion Q1 net income. However, a combined net worth of $186.6 billion does not translate into regulatory compliance. Because the U.S. Treasury’s senior preferred stock does not qualify as Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) capital under the ERCF, the enterprises operate with a structural capital deficit. As of March 31, 2026, Fannie Mae alone reported a CET1 capital deficit of approximately $37 billion, and the combined capital shortfall for both GSEs to meet the full ERCF requirements and buffers is estimated to exceed $100 billion. Under current regulations, the GSEs cannot execute a public capital raise while operating under a regulatory capital deficit.

This capital deficit is directly linked to the treatment of the government's financial stake. Under the Senior Preferred Stock Purchase Agreements (SPSPAs) established during the 2008 bailout, the Treasury holds senior preferred stock with a liquidation preference that grows as the GSEs retain earnings. As of March 31, 2026, Fannie Mae’s senior preferred stock liquidation preference stands at $230.5 billion, while Freddie Mac’s liquidation preference was $140.2 billion at the end of 2025, representing a combined liquidation preference of over $370.7 billion. Furthermore, the Treasury holds warrants to purchase 79.9% of the common equity of both enterprises at a nominal price, which are set to expire on September 7, 2028. H.R. 9460 proposes to codify the 2019 Treasury housing reform plan, which includes converting the Treasury's senior preferred stock into common stock and exercising its warrants.

For retail investors on InvestorsHub and the r/FNMA_FMCC_Exit subreddit, investors argue that the proposed conversion and warrant exercise would result in severe dilution, wiping out the value of their holdings. Legacy common shareholders argue that the government has already been compensated. Between the implementation of the "net worth sweep" in 2012 and its modification in 2019, the GSEs paid the Treasury over $300 billion in cash dividends—exceeding the initial $191 billion draw. Retail commentators argue that the liquidation preference is an artificial accounting barrier that should be written down or declared paid-in-full. Critics on ADVFN note that converting the senior preferred stock and exercising the 79.9% warrants would flood the market with billions of new common shares, diluting existing common shareholders' equity down to a fraction of a percent.

Housing finance experts point out that the legislative focus may be secondary to administrative action. While Representative Fitzgerald's Sustainable Homeownership Act seeks to establish a statutory exit path, the executive branch already possesses the administrative authority to restructure the SPSPAs and release the GSEs without congressional approval. Under the second Trump administration, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and FHFA Director Bill Pulte (who was also recently appointed Acting Director of National Intelligence) hold the necessary administrative powers. Bessent and Pulte can administratively write down the liquidation preference, convert the preferred shares under different terms, or restructure the warrants to pave the way for an IPO. Because any housing reform bill faces steep legislative hurdles in a divided Congress, market observers view H.R. 9460 primarily as political signaling. As one user on r/FNMA_FMCC_Exit noted, the bill "forces the conversation," but the ultimate resolution will come from "Bessent's Treasury, not Capitol Hill."

Furthermore, housing finance analysts remain divided on the level of capitalization the GSEs actually require. Proponents of utility-style regulation argue that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac should operate with lower capital requirements because they do not carry the same operational risks as depository banks. They argue that excessive capital requirements, like those under the current ERCF, increase the cost of mortgage lending and ultimately hurt homebuyers. Conversely, proponents of the ERCF, including the USMI, maintain that robust capital buffers are essential to prevent another taxpayer-funded bailout, arguing that credit risk transfers (CRTs) must be expanded to shift mortgage credit risk to private markets. The introduction of H.R. 9460 has exposed these deep structural disagreements, illustrating that the battle over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac is not just about shareholder returns, but the fundamental design of the American housing finance system.
