# The Blanche Confirmation: A Department of Justice in the Shadow of Personal Loyalty

On July 15, 2026, Todd Blanche sat before the Senate Judiciary Committee for a grueling five-hour confirmation hearing that laid bare a historic battle over the independence of the American legal system. Nominated by President Donald Trump to serve as the permanent Attorney General of the United States, Blanche has operated as Acting Attorney General since April 2, 2026, following the sudden departure of Pam Bondi. But his path to permanent confirmation remains highly precarious, shadowed by his previous tenure as Trump’s personal criminal defense attorney and a series of highly irregular decisions that critics argue have compromised the Department of Justice (DOJ).

The hearing, presided over by Senate Judiciary Committee Chairman Charles Grassley, immediately devolved into a referendum on Blanche's loyalty to the Constitution versus his loyalty to the President. Blanche, who was confirmed as Deputy Attorney General in March 2025, has faced intense scrutiny over two major controversies: a collusive IRS settlement that led to a federal judge referring him for disciplinary action, and a series of private, closed-door meetings with convicted sex trafficker Ghislaine Maxwell that preceded her transfer to a minimum-security facility.

The most immediate threat to Blanche's nomination is a scathing 56-page ruling issued on July 13, 2026, by U.S. District Judge Kathleen Williams of the Southern District of Florida. The ruling addressed a lawsuit filed by Donald Trump, his eldest sons, and the Trump Organization against the IRS, alleging government liability for the leak of Trump's tax returns by former contractor Charles Littlejohn. In May 2026, the DOJ, under Blanche's direction, quieted the suit with a highly controversial settlement. The deal established a $1.776 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund" administered by the DOJ to compensate victims of alleged government "lawfare" and granted sweeping tax immunity to the Trump family and their business entities.

Judge Williams rejected the settlement in absolute terms, characterizing the lawsuit as "improper" and an act of "blatant self-dealing." She noted that because the plaintiffs effectively controlled the Department of Justice defending the suit, there was no legitimate "case or controversy." Williams referred Blanche to the State Bar of New York for potential ethical violations and Associate Attorney General Stanley Woodward to the District of Columbia Bar. She also referred Trump's personal attorney, Alejandro Brito, to the Florida Bar and barred the DOJ from citing the settlement in any future proceedings.

During the hearing, Blanche attempted to defuse the issue, declaring that the controversial $1.776 billion fund was "dead" and "moot." He defended his actions by claiming such settlements are routine and denied any collusion. However, his defense did little to assuage the concerns of committee members. Bipartisan backlash remained palpable, with key Republican senators like John Cornyn and Thom Tillis remaining noncommittal on his confirmation.

Equally contentious was the committee’s focus on Blanche’s actions in July 2025, when he was serving as Deputy Attorney General. Over two days, on July 24 and 25, 2025, Blanche traveled to Florida to personally interview Ghislaine Maxwell. Transcripts confirm Blanche led the questioning. Just one week later, in August 2025, the Bureau of Prisons transferred Maxwell from the low-security Federal Correctional Institution (FCI) Tallahassee in Florida to the minimum-security Federal Prison Camp (FPC) Bryan in Texas. Under Bureau of Prisons policy, inmates convicted of sex offenses are generally barred from minimum-security camps, and Maxwell's transfer required a highly unusual policy waiver.

Senator Sheldon Whitehouse has spent nearly a year demanding documents regarding the Maxwell meetings and the transfer, accusing the DOJ of "stonewalling" and providing "zero responses." Whitehouse released a letter ahead of the hearing demanding these files as a condition for confirmation, calling Blanche's behavior "recalcitrant." During the hearing, Senator Cory Booker pressed Blanche on whether the meetings were connected to a potential presidential pardon or sentence commutation by the end of 2026. Blanche denied discussing clemency, asserting the interviews were strictly investigative. However, Maxwell's legal team has signaled her readiness to cooperate and testify regarding the Jeffrey Epstein network in exchange for a sentence reduction, claiming she could vindicate major public figures. While White House Press Secretary Karoline Leavitt stated in February 2026 that clemency was not under consideration, the rapid transfer has fueled persistent speculation and congressional inquiries regarding whether the move constituted preferential treatment.

Blanche was also forced to address the DOJ's recent release of millions of pages of Epstein-related files. The release was marred by significant redaction errors that exposed the names and personal information of victims. Blanche acknowledged that "mistakes were made" on approximately 1% of the documents, apologizing to the survivors. "Any mistake that we made should not have been made," Blanche testified, though he declined to commit to meeting with the survivors personally.

The institutional opposition to Blanche has reached a fever pitch. On July 13, 2026, the New York City Bar Association sent a letter to the Senate Judiciary Committee urging it to reject Blanche's nomination. Signed by City Bar President Matthew Diller and Rule of Law Task Force Chair Susan J. Kohlmann, the letter cited five areas of concern, including politically motivated prosecutions, violations of constitutional rights, and a lack of independence. 

This was bolstered by a letter from the DOJ alumni group Justice Connection, signed by more than 1,200 former DOJ employees spanning 14 administrations. The letter warned of a "dangerous degradation" of the department, noting that under Blanche's senior leadership, approximately 16,000 career employees have departed. Conversely, a group of 77 former DOJ officials submitted a letter of support, calling Blanche the "right man for the right time."

As the hearing concluded, the future of the Department of Justice hung in the balance. The intense debate on social media, particularly on platforms like Reddit's r/politics, centered on Blanche's relationship with Trump. Critics pointed to his past statements, with many sharing the phrase "I'm his lawyer" as proof that Blanche cannot separate his role as the President's personal defender from his duty to lead the nation's premier law enforcement agency.
