# The Mercenary Mirage: How Erik Prince’s Crowdfunded Crusade Collided With Caracas, the CIA, and the Ghosts of Operation Gedeón

On September 16, 2024, when the digital countdown clock on yacasivenezuela.com reached zero, the expected armed confrontation materialized instead as a digital payment portal. Over the preceding fortnight, Erik Prince, the 55-year-old founder of the private security firm Blackwater, had mounted a sustained social media campaign targeting the Venezuelan diaspora. Following the contested July 28 presidential election—in which the National Electoral Council declared Nicolás Maduro the victor without publishing precinct-level returns, while opposition tallies documented a decisive margin for Edmundo González Urrutia—Prince urged Venezuelans to fund alternative measures. "The Venezuelan people have spoken, and now it's time to vote with your money," Prince stated in one widely circulated video, projecting that private initiative would achieve what civic protests had not.

When the timer expired, the website directed visitors to an unvetted fundraising platform accepting contributions via Tether (USDT), Bitcoin, Ethereum, and credit-card merchant gateways. The organizers established an initial benchmark of $10 million, while promotional statements suggested an operational threshold as high as $100 million. Within several days, the campaign announced collections exceeding $1.2 million. However, the pivot to retail crowdfunding immediately ignited widespread skepticism across the diaspora. On the r/vzla subreddit, where users monitored transactional volume across public blockchain ledgers, the initiative drew sharp condemnation. "Venezuelan politics has officially become a pay-to-win mobile game," noted one top-ranked community commenter, echoing sentiments across social media platforms that characterized the opaque campaign as an opportunistic exploitation of exile sentiment.

The initiative also revived scrutiny of Prince’s transactional history in the region. In late November 2019, Prince traveled secretly to Caracas for meetings with Maduro’s Executive Vice President, Delcy Rodríguez. Arranged by former JPMorgan commodities dealmaker Ian Hannam, the discussions covered commercial proposals including private investments in Venezuelan gold extraction and the legal status of six detained Citgo executives. The documented engagement between Prince and Maduro’s key economic deputy underscored an unresolved conflict of interest for opposition figures. Democratic opposition leaders María Corina Machado and Edmundo González Urrutia declined to endorse the initiative, focusing their strategy on international diplomatic recognition of the verified paper voting tallies (*actas*). Exiled former Caracas police commissioner Iván Simonovis, who had initially promoted the platform to his followers, distanced himself from the operation within days, writing on X that he had acted merely as "another spectator" without executive authority over the gathered funds.

In Caracas, the Venezuelan government capitalized on the campaign to justify an extensive domestic and military crackdown. Interior Minister Diosdado Cabello convened an emergency press conference, presenting a display of more than 400 firearms seized by state intelligence. The display included Century Arms WASR-10 rifles, AR-15 variants, and tactical pistols, which Cabello stated had been smuggled into Puerto Cabello from Florida inside freight containers disguised as animal feed and household items.

Cabello announced that security forces had apprehended Wilbert Joseph Castañeda Gómez, a 37-year-old active-duty U.S. Navy petty officer first class. Although Cabello labeled Castañeda an active Navy SEAL coordinating a Central Intelligence Agency plot to assassinate Maduro and Delcy Rodríguez, U.S. Navy records indicated that while Castañeda had served in SEAL units during deployments to Iraq and Afghanistan, he had been administratively stripped of his Trident insignia prior to traveling to South America. Venezuelan authorities also detained two other American citizens, Aaron Barrett Logan and David Estrella, on cyber-espionage charges; two Spanish nationals, Andrés Martínez Adasme and José María Basoa Valdovinos, whom Caracas accused of ties to Spain’s National Intelligence Center (CNI); and a Czech citizen, Jan Darmovzal. "Those who try to mess with Venezuela," Cabello announced during the state broadcast, "we will screw them, regardless of their name."

Diplomatic denials followed immediately. The U.S. State Department under then-President Joe Biden dismissed assertions of an intelligence-directed coup as "categorically false," confirming Castañeda had entered the country on unauthorized personal leave. The Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs similarly rejected claims that its citizens were intelligence operatives, stating they were tourists traveling near the Colombian border. Nevertheless, Venezuelan authorities used the arrests to enforce legal intimidation across the civilian population. Attorney General Tarek William Saab initiated a formal criminal investigation into Ya Casi Venezuela, warning that anyone contributing to the platform was guilty of money laundering and conspiracy to finance terrorism. Saab stressed that prosecutors were securing donor registries and reminded the public that under the Venezuelan Penal Code and the Organic Law Against Organized Crime, these offenses carry maximum sentences of up to 30 years in prison. Cabello reinforced the warning by pledging to deploy "Operación Tun Tun"—the state's practice of direct residential raids against targeted individuals.

Concurrently, Defense Minister Vladimir Padrino López placed the National Bolivarian Armed Forces (FANB) on high alert. Citing the threat of armed incursions, Padrino López deployed naval patrol units, air defense batteries, and coastal infantry regiments across strategic Caribbean coastal corridors to secure littoral perimeters.

The strategic response in Caracas was conditioned by the memory of Operation Gedeón. In May 2020, former U.S. Army Special Forces sergeant Jordan Goudreau launched an ill-fated amphibious raid using two open boats carrying approximately 60 men toward Macuto and Chuao. Venezuelan security forces intercepted the vessels, killing eight combatants and capturing former Green Berets Luke Denman and Airan Berry. Goudreau’s Florida contractor, Silvercorp USA, collapsed into legal wreckage. On July 30, 2024, federal prosecutors in the Middle District of Florida arrested and indicted Goudreau on 14 counts of arms smuggling and unlicensed weapons exports related to the 2020 incursion. The indictment served as an official U.S. judicial repudiation of private regime-change operations at the precise moment Prince’s campaign re-introduced the concept to the digital sphere.

Meanwhile, official U.S. pressure operated through statutory judicial mechanisms rather than covert paramilitaries. On September 2, 2024, federal authorities seized Maduro’s $13 million Dassault Falcon 900EX executive aircraft in Santo Domingo, Dominican Republic, transferring it to Fort Lauderdale on grounds that its purchase violated federal export control and sanctions statutes. Prince responded publicly by urging U.S. officials to bypass traditional diplomacy, proposing on conservative media outlets that Washington utilize frozen Venezuelan assets to raise the Department of State’s $15 million reward for Maduro’s capture to $100 million.

The diplomatic standoff shifted significantly following the transition in Washington. In July 2025, under President Donald Trump’s administration, the United States, Venezuela, and El Salvador concluded a multilateral prisoner exchange. Venezuela released Castañeda and nine other detained American nationals; in return, the U.S. arranged the repatriation to Caracas of more than 250 Venezuelan nationals who had been detained in El Salvador’s CECOT facility following deportation proceedings. Castañeda’s return to the United States concluded his eleven-month detention, though his subsequent appearance at a political conference in Dallas in September 2026 prompted official Defense Department reviews regarding active-duty compliance with military non-partisanship directives.

The trajectory of the Ya Casi Venezuela campaign demonstrated the systemic risks of privatized foreign intervention in the internet era. While marketed as an agile alternative to state diplomacy, the initiative raised more than $1.2 million with minimal operational transparency, fragmented diaspora unity, and exposed domestic sympathizers to severe legal retaliation, ultimately reinforcing the state security apparatus it purported to dismantle.
