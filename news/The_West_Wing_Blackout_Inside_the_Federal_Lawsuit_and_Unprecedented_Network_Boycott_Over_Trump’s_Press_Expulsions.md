# The West Wing Blackout: Inside the Federal Lawsuit and Unprecedented Network Boycott Over Trump’s Press Expulsions

At 7:45 a.m. on Saturday, September 19, 2026, veteran White House correspondents Betsy Klein of CNN, Akayla Gardner of MS NOW (the independent network formerly known as MSNBC following its November 2025 corporate spin-off into Versant), and Cheyenne Haslett of Politico approached the security checkpoint at the White House Northwest Gate. The routine electronic confirmation that typically authenticates a permanent credential did not register. Instead, Secret Service uniform division personnel confiscated their laminated hard passes and turned them away from the complex. The physical deactivations executed an executive directive posted fourteen hours earlier on Truth Social by President Donald Trump, who announced that all three news organizations were barred from the White House complex “effective immediately” for what he termed “constant ‘reporting’ FAKE NEWS” and publishing “fiction and lies.”

By Monday morning, September 21, the dispute escalated into a direct constitutional challenge in federal court. In the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia, attorneys from Gibson, Dunn & Crutcher LLP filed a 38-page emergency complaint and motion for a temporary restraining order in *Cable News Network, Inc. et al. v. Trump et al.* (Case No. 1:26-cv-03287). The lawsuit names President Trump, White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Communications Director Steven Cheung, and Secret Service Director Sean Curran as defendants in their official capacities. The complaint sets up a fundamental confrontation between core First and Fifth Amendment protections and the administration’s assertion of plenary Article II executive discretion over presidential property.

Lead counsel Theodore J. Boutrous Jr., joined by partners Katie Townsend and Connor Sullivan, structured the plaintiffs’ emergency motion around two interlocking constitutional arguments. Under the First Amendment, the plaintiffs contend that summarily evicting news organizations because the President disdains their editorial scrutiny constitutes unlawful viewpoint discrimination and retaliatory state action. The complaint argues that while the White House press briefing room and West Wing workspaces constitute a limited public or nonpublic forum, the government cannot condition access on ideological alignment or journalistic praise. Under the Fifth Amendment, the suit alleges a severe breach of procedural due process, citing the government’s total failure to provide written standards of conduct, formal notice of alleged infractions, or any administrative mechanism to contest the revocation.

To anchor these claims, the plaintiffs rely on binding regional jurisprudence stretching across half a century. Foremost is the D.C. Circuit’s landmark 1977 decision in *Sherrill v. Knight*, which held that the White House cannot arbitrarily deny or revoke Secret Service press credentials without published, explicit criteria and procedural due process. That precedent was reinforced in November 2018 by *CNN v. Trump*, when U.S. District Judge Timothy J. Kelly ordered the immediate restoration of CNN correspondent Jim Acosta’s hard pass on Fifth Amendment procedural grounds, and again in 2020 by *Karem v. Trump*, which shielded reporter Brian Karem from retaliatory credential suspension. 

In a striking judicial assignment, the new complaint was drawn by Judge Kelly himself. On Monday afternoon, Judge Kelly ordered an expedited briefing schedule, directing the Department of Justice to submit its formal opposition and setting an emergency oral hearing for 3:30 p.m. on Wednesday, September 23.

The administration’s public posture, articulated by White House Communications Director Steven Cheung, signaled no intention of yielding. Cheung affirmed that senior advisers stood beside the President when the directive was issued and “all fully support his decision,” maintaining that physical presence on White House grounds remains an executive “privilege—not a right.” Department of Justice attorneys are preparing to argue that Article II of the Constitution vests the Chief Executive with plenary, unreviewable discretion to control access to the executive residence and secure government facilities, contending that federal courts lack the constitutional authority to compel a sitting president to host specific commercial entities inside his working quarters.

Yet the administration’s strategy collided almost immediately with an unprecedented media counteroffensive. Rather than allowing the three outlets to be isolated, the major television networks that form the White House television pool rotation—ABC News, CBS News, CNN, Fox News, and NBC News—mounted an industry-wide collective boycott.

The crisis peaked on Monday as President Trump prepared to travel to New York for bilateral meetings surrounding the 81st Session of the United Nations General Assembly. Under the longstanding pool schedule, CNN had been assigned to serve as the editorial and technical pool lead, responsible for operating cameras, audio feeds, and distribution lines for all competing networks. When the White House prohibited CNN’s crew from boarding Air Force One and entering the presidential motorcade, the pool consortium refused to supply an alternative broadcaster.

Bryan Boughton, the Washington bureau chief for Fox News and the acting chair of the TV pool consortium, issued an internal memo to all member networks that effectively halted joint visual presidential coverage: “Effective today, the TV pool will not be covering events designated as pool coverage of the President. This follows the White House’s position preventing CNN from fulfilling its assigned pool duties. There will be no replacement pool put in place.”

Hours later, the leadership of ABC, CBS, CNN, Fox News, and NBC released a rare unified joint statement: “The public has a vital interest in receiving accurate, independent information about its government. No administration should restrict a news organization because it objects to its reporting.” White House Correspondents’ Association President Jacqui Heinrich, who also serves as Fox News Senior White House Correspondent, publicly condemned the ban, stating that the “revoking of access for journalists violates the First Amendment” and warning that conditioning credentials on White House approval threatens the foundation of democratic oversight.

The operational impact was instantaneous and severe. During a South Lawn ribbon-cutting ceremony on Monday afternoon for a newly completed $6 million granite helipad, standard network pool cameras and specialized broadcast directional microphones were conspicuously absent. Attempting to bypass traditional media, the White House directed audiences to “Trump TV: The Essentials Station,” an official 24/7 administration-run streaming service launched online over the weekend. However, the government feed suffered an immediate technical breakdown: lacking the specialized noise-filtering audio equipment routinely deployed by network pool technicians, the official livestream broadcast only the deafening roar of an idling helicopter engine, rendering President Trump’s ceremonial remarks completely inaudible.

The visual news blackout sparked sharp scrutiny and widespread debate across Washington and digital platforms. On Reddit’s r/politics, community forums quickly filled with analytical threads dissecting Fox News’s participation in the boycott. As one widely circulated comment put it: “When Fox News is the one pulling the plug on pool coverage, the administration has completely lost the room.” On X, legal commentators and press advocacy groups highlighted the administration’s self-inflicted communications void during a major diplomatic week at the United Nations, with the Reporters Committee for Freedom of the Press observing that the visual news blackout demonstrated the indispensability of the independent pool system.

As the parties prepare to face Judge Kelly on Wednesday, the case has moved far beyond the immediate status of three press badges. It now stands as a defining test of constitutional boundaries: whether the Article II authority of the executive branch can override the procedural guarantees established in *Sherrill v. Knight*, or whether the combined resistance of the federal judiciary and a unified press corps will compel the West Wing to reopen its gates.
