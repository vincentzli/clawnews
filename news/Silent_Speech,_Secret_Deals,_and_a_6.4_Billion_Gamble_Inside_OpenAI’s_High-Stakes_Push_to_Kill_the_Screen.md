# Silent Speech, Secret Deals, and a $6.4 Billion Gamble: Inside OpenAI’s High-Stakes Push to Kill the Screen

In May 2025, OpenAI shocked the technology industry with its massive, all-equity acquisition of io Products, Inc.—a secretive hardware startup co-founded by legendary former Apple design chief Sir Jony Ive and former Apple Vice President of Product Design Tang Yew Tan. Valued at approximately $6.4 billion, the transaction represented the most aggressive hardware play by an artificial intelligence firm to date. Yet, as OpenAI prepares for its next chapter, the venture finds itself entangled in a high-stakes legal war in a federal courtroom in San Francisco, exposing the hidden tensions between Silicon Valley's ambition to replace the smartphone and the intellectual property guardrails meant to protect small inventors.

The conflict centers on IYO, Inc., a voice-computing startup founded in 2021 by Jason Rugolo, the former lead of Google X’s audio-focused "Wolverine" project. In June 2025, IYO filed a federal trademark infringement lawsuit, *IYO, Inc. v. IO Products, Inc. et al.*, under Case No. 3:25-cv-04861-TLT in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. IYO alleged that OpenAI’s hardware venture, branded as "io" (pronounced identically as "eye-oh"), would cause irreparable consumer confusion in the nascent screenless computing market. 

According to court filings, the dispute is not merely phonetic. IYO claims that in 2022, Rugolo and his team met with OpenAI CEO Sam Altman to demonstrate their screenless audio computer, the IYO ONE. In March 2026, IYO amended its complaint to include allegations of trade secret misappropriation, claiming that OpenAI, Altman, and Tang Yew Tan utilized confidential product designs and technical concepts shared during those early demonstrations. Despite OpenAI's legal team dismissing the suit as opportunistic, District Judge Trina L. Thompson issued a Temporary Restraining Order in June 2025, which was subsequently affirmed by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit in December 2025. In April 2026, Judge Thompson granted a preliminary injunction, legally barring OpenAI from using the "io" brand.

Faced with a mounting legal blockade, OpenAI scrubbed the "io" name, pivoting to an internal project codename: "Sweetpea." The tech giant is reportedly moving forward under this new moniker, while IYO has secured financial backing from the global litigation-funding giant Omni Bridgeway to sustain the protracted legal battle against the well-resourced defendants.

Despite these legal hurdles, OpenAI’s Chief Global Affairs Officer Chris Lehane confirmed at the World Economic Forum in Davos in January 2026 that the company remains on track to unveil its first consumer hardware device in the second half of 2026. While Lehane remained tight-lipped on the device's form factor, indicating that the launch window remains fluid, supply chain leaks and patent filings describe a radical departure from the modern smartphone. 

"Sweetpea" is designed as a screenless, audio-first wearable worn behind the ear, positioned along the mastoid bone. Under Jony Ive’s independent design firm LoveFrom, which has contracted with OpenAI to lead the design language, the device embodies a philosophy of "calm computing" or "attention liberation." Rather than fighting for the user's visual attention, the wearable acts as an ambient assistant. 

Technically, the device bypasses the traditional constraints of voice user interfaces. Sources close to the supply chain indicate that Sweetpea will utilize electromyography (EMG) sensors placed along the jawline and mastoid bone to detect "subvocalization"—the electrical impulses sent to facial muscles when mouthing words silently. This allows users to whisper commands to their AI companion without producing audible sound, resolving the public awkwardness that doomed early voice assistants. For audio feedback, the device is expected to employ xMEMS solid-state ultrasonic speaker technology, directing high-fidelity audio into the ear canal via ultrasonic waves without blocking environmental noise.

To support these advanced local capabilities, OpenAI is reportedly moving away from the cloud-first architectures that crippled predecessors like the Humane AI Pin and the Rabbit R1. The Humane AI Pin suffered from severe thermal throttling and lag because every query traveled to the cloud and back. To bypass this bottleneck, Sweetpea is rumored to feature a high-end 2nm smartphone-class processor, likely based on Samsung’s Exynos platform, to run distilled versions of OpenAI’s large language models locally on-device. This local processing is designed to ensure sub-millisecond response latency and absolute data privacy. OpenAI is simultaneously hedging its cloud infrastructure; in June 2026, the company partnered with Broadcom to unveil "Jalapeño," an LLM-optimized custom silicon processor designed specifically for data centers, showing a dual commitment to edge-device and cloud-scale compute.

Foxconn has been selected to handle mass production of the device, with OpenAI setting an ambitious timeline. While Chris Lehane signaled a late 2026 unveiling, supply chain whispers suggest that mass shipments will not begin until February 2027. 

Industry analysts remain deeply divided on the viability of the project. Proponents argue that Jony Ive’s legendary design pedigree—responsible for the iMac, iPod, iPhone, and Apple Watch—combined with OpenAI's market-leading models, could create a genuine platform shift. In Washington, the project is being watched closely, as the current U.S. administration under President Donald Trump continues to push for strict domestic hardware supply chains and data privacy frameworks, elements OpenAI hopes to satisfy by manufacturing with Foxconn and relying on local 2nm silicon.

However, the memory of recent hardware failures looms large. On Reddit’s r/technology and r/singularity, users have expressed deep skepticism. "No one wants a device that whispers in their ear all day," one user wrote. Another noted, "This is just another Humane AI Pin waiting to happen. If I have to carry my phone anyway, why would I buy a $600 ear accessory?" 

Whether Ive's aesthetic genius can overcome the public's screen dependency is the multi-billion-dollar question. If Sweetpea succeeds, it may mark the beginning of the post-smartphone era. If it fails, it will serve as the most expensive monument to Silicon Valley's hubris since the dot-com bust.
