On the morning of July 15, 2026, a retail derivatives trader in Grand Rapids, Michigan, logged into the prediction market platform KalshiEX LLC, expecting to find his settled sports event contracts voided. Just weeks prior, Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel had secured a temporary restraining order in state court, ordering Kalshi to cease offering sports-related contracts and directing the platform to void and refund all executed trades in the state. The trader had resigned himself to losing his positions. Instead, he found his account intact and a message from the exchange: the federal government had stepped in.

Twenty-four hours earlier, on July 14, 2026, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) executed an extraordinary intervention. Invoking its emergency powers under Section 8a(9) of the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA), the federal regulator stayed a rule Kalshi had self-filed on July 12 to liquidate Michigan accounts in compliance with the state court order. The CFTC did not just pause the state court's directive; it ordered Kalshi to keep fulfilling and clearing its sports contracts in the ordinary course of business.

This dramatic collision represents the most significant jurisdictional battle in the history of American derivatives regulation. It pits the federal government’s exclusive oversight of commodities markets against the historic police powers of states to regulate gambling and protect consumers. With parallel legal battles raging from New Jersey to Washington state, and Congress scrambling to hold hearings, the standoff over whether prediction markets are legitimate financial hedging instruments or merely high-tech sportsbooks is heading toward a constitutional showdown.

The road to this regulatory impasse began in Lansing. In March 2026, Attorney General Dana Nessel filed a lawsuit against Kalshi in Ingham County Circuit Court, accusing the platform of violating the Michigan Lawful Sports Betting Act. Nessel argued that Kalshi’s "event contracts"—which allow users to trade contracts on the outcomes of professional and collegiate sports games—were unlicensed sports betting. The state took particular issue with consumer protection safeguards, noting that Kalshi allowed users as young as 18 to trade, while Michigan’s legal sports betting age is 21. On June 29, 2026, Ingham County Circuit Court Judge Rosemarie Aquilina granted Nessel's request, issuing a temporary restraining order that prohibited Kalshi from offering or advertising sports-related contracts to Michigan residents.

For Kalshi, a Designated Contract Market (DCM) regulated by the CFTC, the order was an operational nightmare. Unwinding settled, cleared trades is almost unprecedented in financial markets. To avoid violating state law, Kalshi proposed an emergency rule to liquidate and refund Michigan accounts. 

That was when CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig, who took office in December 2025 after confirmation by the Senate, decided to draw a line in the sand. Selig, a former corporate lawyer and SEC chief counsel who has advocated for clear, principles-based regulation, viewed the Michigan order as a threat to market integrity. The CFTC's July 14 emergency order stayed Kalshi’s liquidation rule, declaring that retroactively unwinding settled trades would create a "market emergency" and undermine contract certainty. Selig stated that states and state courts could not "bully" federally registered entities into violating the CEA and CFTC regulations.

Legal observers on Reddit’s r/law and r/politics quickly highlighted the significance of the move, with one user noting that the "CFTC basically told Michigan to kick rocks, setting up a massive federal preemption fight." Critics in the state capital, however, argued that the federal agency was overstepping its bounds, displacing state consumer protection laws to shield a private trading platform.

The CFTC's legal defense rests heavily on a landmark decision issued by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit. On April 6, 2026, in *KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty*, a panel ruled 2–1 in favor of the prediction market. In that case, New Jersey’s Division of Gaming Enforcement, led by then-Interim Director Mary Jo Flaherty, had issued a cease-and-desist letter to Kalshi, arguing its sports contracts violated state gambling laws. 

The Third Circuit majority opinion, authored by Circuit Judge David J. Porter and joined by Chief Judge Michael A. Chagares, held that Kalshi’s sports event contracts qualify as "swaps" under the CEA and therefore fall under the CFTC’s exclusive jurisdiction. The majority concluded that federal law preempts state gaming regulations when applied to DCM-listed contracts, effectively shielding Kalshi from state enforcement. 

However, the ruling was far from unanimous. In a sharp dissent, Circuit Judge Jane R. Roth argued that the majority's reasoning was a "performative sleight." Roth wrote that Kalshi’s sports contracts are "virtually indistinguishable" from sports betting offered by commercial sportsbooks like DraftKings or FanDuel. She argued that the presumption against preemption should apply with "special force" because gambling regulation is a traditional state police power. Congress, Roth contended, never intended to turn the CFTC into a national gaming regulator when it updated the CEA in 2010.

While Kalshi holds the Third Circuit precedent in its favor on the East Coast, the Western front has proved far more hostile. On July 20, 2026, Judge John McHale of the King County Superior Court in Washington issued a preliminary injunction against Kalshi. Washington Attorney General Nick Brown had sued the platform, alleging "brazen violations" of the Washington Gambling Act. 

Unlike the Third Circuit, Judge McHale rejected Kalshi’s preemption defense, ruling that the state is likely to succeed on its claims that the platform's sports contracts constitute illegal gambling under state law. The court did not immediately halt all operations, but instead deferred the final terms of the injunction to August 5, 2026, allowing Kalshi and the Washington Attorney General’s Office to confer on the language and scope of the remedies. In the meantime, Kalshi has been ordered to preserve all marketing, geolocation, and communication logs related to Washington consumers.

The rapid escalation of these court battles has put intense pressure on lawmakers in Washington, D.C. On July 21, 2026, the House Agriculture Subcommittee on Commodity Markets, Digital Assets, and Rural Development convened a hearing titled "Examining Customer Protections and Market Integrity in Sports Event Prediction Markets." 

Chaired by Rep. Dusty Johnson (R-SD), the hearing exposed deep bipartisan anxiety over the rapid growth of these platforms. Lawmakers questioned whether the CFTC—an agency traditionally tasked with policing agricultural futures and complex financial swaps—has the staffing and expertise to oversee retail sports betting. Carl Kennedy, a derivatives attorney at Katten Muchin Rosenman, testified that the CFTC is understaffed to police these highly active consumer-facing markets.

The gaming industry has also launched a coordinated lobbying campaign against prediction markets. Christopher Cylke, Senior Vice President of the American Gaming Association, testified that Kalshi is operating sports betting without paying state taxes, obtaining state gaming licenses, or adhering to responsible gaming and integrity-monitoring standards. David Z. Bean, Chairman of the Indian Gaming Association, warned that federal preemption of sports contracts directly threatens tribal sovereignty and the tribal-state gaming compacts that fund critical reservation services. 

Underneath the legal maneuvering lies a fundamental economic debate: do prediction markets serve a legitimate hedging purpose, or are they simply speculative gambling? 

Supporters argue that sports event contracts are valuable hedging tools. A sports venue owner, for instance, might buy contracts to hedge against the financial loss of a local team failing to make the playoffs, or a sportswear retailer might hedge against sudden shifts in consumer demand tied to team performance. However, critics argue these use cases are vanishingly rare compared to the flood of retail speculators betting on game outcomes. Bipartisan lawmakers expressed concern that prediction markets are exposing minors to gambling, pointing out that Kalshi’s 18-and-up limit bypasses the 21-and-up rule enforced by almost all state sportsbooks.

The CFTC is currently attempting to codify its boundaries. On June 10, 2026, the Commission issued a Notice of Proposed Rulemaking (NPRM) titled "Prediction Markets; Public Interest Determinations" (RIN 3038-AF65). The proposed rule seeks to establish a formal framework for determining when event contracts are contrary to the public interest. 

Under the proposal, contracts based on aggregate sports outcomes—such as final scores, season win-loss records, and division standings using objective data—would generally be permitted. However, contracts based on discrete in-game actions, player injuries, officiating decisions, or physical altercations would be prohibited. The rule also proposes to replace the current "self-certification" regime with a structured 90-day review process, ending the era of rapid contract listings.

The public comment period for the proposed rule is set to close on July 27, 2026. As the deadline approaches, the CFTC has been flooded with thousands of letters from retail traders, gaming associations, and state attorneys general.

Whether the proposed rule will bring order to the chaotic landscape remains to be seen. With Michigan and Washington state aggressively challenging federal supremacy, and a clear circuit split looming on the horizon, the final decision may rest not with the CFTC or Congress, but with the Supreme Court. Until then, exchanges like Kalshi remain caught in a regulatory no-man's-land—ordered by federal regulators to trade, and by state courts to stop.
