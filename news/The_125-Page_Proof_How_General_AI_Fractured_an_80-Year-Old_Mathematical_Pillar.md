# The 125-Page Proof: How General AI Fractured an 80-Year-Old Mathematical Pillar

The atmosphere in the mathematics community on the morning of May 20, 2026, was characterized by a profound, recalibrating shock. For exactly eighty years, the Erdős unit distance conjecture had stood as a monolith in discrete geometry, resisting the combined intellectual force of generations of mathematicians. Then, an internal general-purpose reasoning model at OpenAI dismantled it. The machine did not offer a statistical guess or a heuristic approximation; it delivered a grueling, 125-page chain-of-thought proof that fundamentally remapped the accepted boundaries of geometric combinatorics.

To understand the magnitude of this fracture, one must look back to 1946, when Paul Erdős first posed a deceptively simple question: Given a set of $n$ points in a two-dimensional plane, what is the absolute maximum number of pairs of points that can be separated by exactly one unit of distance? Erdős, drawing on the geometric intuition of his era, hypothesized that the maximum number of such distances would grow at a rate nearly linear to the number of points, specifically bounded by $n^{1+o(1)}$. For decades, the greatest minds in the field assumed that grid-like, square arrangements of points were the optimal configuration. In 1984, Joel Spencer, Endre Szemerédi, and William Trotter established a rigid upper bound of $O(n^{4/3})$, but the gap between Erdős's lower bound and this ceiling remained completely stagnant.

The OpenAI reasoning model bypassed geometric grid intuition entirely. Instead, it reached into the dense, abstract machinery of algebraic number theory. Utilizing Golod-Shafarevich theory and the construction of infinite class field towers—concepts traditionally alien to classical discrete geometry—the model engineered an infinite family of point arrangements that completely violated Erdős’s assumption. It established a new polynomial lower bound, proving that the number of unit distances can grow as $n^{1+\delta}$. When the machine output its proof, the initial exponent $\delta = 6 \times 10^{-38}$ was exceptionally microscopic but mathematically rigorous, supported by an irrefutable algebraic architecture.

It took human expertise to hone the machine’s valid strike. Within days of the announcement, Princeton mathematician Will Sawin published an accelerated refinement on arXiv. Sawin utilized the AI’s exact algebraic architecture to aggressively tighten the exponent, raising $\delta$ to approximately $0.014$, yielding $n^{1.014}$. The raw validation of the AI's 125-page document fell to a coalition of nine elite mathematicians, including University of Manchester’s Thomas Bloom, Noga Alon, Melanie Wood, and Cambridge Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers. The companion paper published by the verification team, titled "Remarks on the disproof of the unit distance conjecture," successfully distilled the AI's reasoning. Gowers publicly stated that the model's proof possessed such structural integrity that he would recommend it for the prestigious *Annals of Mathematics* "without any hesitation."

The cross-disciplinary nature of the proof forced an immediate institutional introspection. Thomas Bloom, who heavily curates the ErdosProblems database, diagnosed the phenomenon as a "sociological blindspot." For eight decades, mathematicians were culturally anchored to the goal of proving Erdős right. Human intuition had become an echo chamber. The AI, unburdened by academic reverence for Erdős or the historical momentum of grid-based geometry, simply sought the mathematical reality, finding a counterexample in a theoretical sector where humans had collectively decided not to look.
