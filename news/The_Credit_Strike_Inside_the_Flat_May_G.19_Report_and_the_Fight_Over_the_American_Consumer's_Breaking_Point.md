# The Credit Strike: Inside the Flat May G.19 Report and the Fight Over the American Consumer's Breaking Point

When the Federal Reserve released its May G.19 consumer credit report on July 8, 2026, economists and bond traders braced for the usual expansion. Instead, they got a flatline. Total outstanding consumer credit rested virtually unchanged at $5,154.5 billion, representing a tiny monthly contraction of $180 million, or a seasonally adjusted annual growth rate of 0.0%. The figure represents a stunning miss against the Bloomberg consensus forecast of a +$16.90 billion expansion, with some Wall Street estimates ranging as high as +$17.5 billion. It was a sharp reversal from the upwardly revised $20.82 billion expansion recorded in April, signaling that the credit-fueled consumer engine that has powered the U.S. economy may finally be stalling out.

The primary driver of the miss was a sudden contraction in revolving credit, which consists primarily of credit card debt. Revolving credit fell at a seasonally adjusted annual rate of 4.7% in May, representing a monthly drop of $5.3 billion to end at $1,344.2 billion. Nonrevolving credit—which includes closed-end auto loans, student loans, and other personal lines of credit—managed a modest annualized expansion of 1.6%, rising by $5.1 billion to reach $3,810.3 trillion. The diverging trends reflect a fundamental tension in household balance sheets: consumers are continuing to service their long-term, nonrevolving commitments, but they are staging a quiet mutiny against the high-interest revolving lines of credit that fund daily transactions.

To understand why revolving credit plummeted, one must look at the borrowing constraints facing households. Under the Federal Reserve's target range for the federal funds rate, which Chairman Kevin Warsh and the Federal Open Market Committee held at 3.50% to 3.75% during their June meeting, credit card interest rates have climbed to historic levels. The G.19 report noted that the average interest rate on commercial bank credit card plans was 20.94% for all accounts, while the rate on accounts assessed interest sat near a multi-decade high of 22.15%. Carrying a credit card balance in this environment has become an expensive luxury. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has highlighted the underlying resilience of consumer demand, but at 21% to 22% interest, consumers are facing a massive interest burden that is consuming a growing portion of their disposable income.

Yet, corporate performance data reveals an intriguing paradox. While consumer credit fell at the macro level, the major credit card issuers reported improving credit quality in their monthly SEC 8-K filings for May. Across the seven largest credit card banks, the average delinquency rate declined to 2.47% in May from 2.55% in April, while the average net charge-off rate eased to 3.63% from 3.70% in the prior month. Capital One Financial Corp. reported a domestic credit card performing delinquency rate of 3.33% ($8.582 billion) and a net charge-off rate of 4.82% ($1.022 billion). American Express Co. reported a U.S. consumer card 30-day delinquency rate of just 1.1% and a net write-off rate of 2.0%. 

This temporary improvement in delinquencies, paired with the contraction in total credit outstanding, points to what economists call the "tax refund paydown" effect. In late spring, households typically use their tax refunds to deleverage, paying off credit card balances to escape compounding interest. Rather than representing a healthy, voluntary retreat from credit usage, the contraction in revolving credit may represent a forced deleveraging. With the personal savings rate hovering at a low 3.0% as of the May Bureau of Economic Analysis report, U.S. households have run out of the cash cushions accumulated during the pandemic. 

This tension is at the heart of the debate on financial social media. In the subreddit r/economics, one user commented: "At 21% interest, anyone carrying a balance is burning cash. It makes sense people are trying to pay them off if they can, or they are just maxed out." On X, the financial blog ZeroHedge analyzed the May report, characterizing the unexpected contraction as a "doozy" following the prior months of outsized growth, and noting that analysts are waiting to see if the paydown in credit card debt will translate into weaker retail sales data in the coming months. Strategist Charlie Bilello pointed out that U.S. household debt stress remains elevated, with New York Fed data showing that the serious delinquency transition rate (90+ days overdue) for credit cards reached 13.1% in the first quarter of 2026, the highest level in 15 years.

This divergence is further supported by the Federal Reserve's May 2026 Senior Loan Officer Opinion Survey (SLOOS). The survey showed that banks kept credit card lending standards "basically unchanged," but reported that demand for credit card loans and auto loans weakened significantly during the first quarter. The contraction is not a credit crunch driven by banks pulling back credit lines; it is a demand-side strike. Consumers are looking at the math and refusing to borrow.

The pressure on households is compounded by a resurgence of inflation. In May, the Consumer Price Index (CPI) rose by 0.5% month-over-month, pushing headline annual inflation back up to 4.2%, even as Core CPI remained relatively anchored at 2.9% year-over-year. The primary culprit was energy costs, which rose 3.9% for the month and 23.5% over the 12-month period, driven by the escalating naval conflict with Iran in the Strait of Hormuz. Because energy is a non-discretionary expense, U.S. consumers are forced to dedicate a larger portion of their paycheck to gasoline and utilities. 

This leaves the Federal Reserve in a precarious position. The U.S. economy grew at a 2.1% annualized rate in the first quarter of 2026, but the Atlanta Fed's GDPNow model is nowcasting just 1.3% growth for the second quarter. If the flat consumer credit number for May is the precursor to a broader pullback in household spending—which represents nearly 70% of U.S. GDP—the Fed's target of a "soft landing" may be out of reach. With headline inflation stuck at 4.2% due to geopolitical energy shocks, Chairman Warsh cannot easily cut rates to stimulate demand without risking further price increases. The flatline in consumer credit is a warning shot: the U.S. consumer is no longer willing, or able, to borrow the economy out of a slowdown.
