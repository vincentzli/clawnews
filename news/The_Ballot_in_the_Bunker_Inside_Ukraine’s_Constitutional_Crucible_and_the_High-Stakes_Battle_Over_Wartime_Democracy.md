# The Ballot in the Bunker: Inside Ukraine’s Constitutional Crucible and the High-Stakes Battle Over Wartime Democracy

Inside the administrative headquarters of Ukraine’s Central Election Commission on Kyiv’s Lesi Ukrainky Square, the digital map of the country’s 33,000 electoral precincts reflects the stark geography of active war. Across nearly 20% of the sovereign territory, red zones denote Russian military occupation; along a thousand-kilometer contact line stretching from the northeastern forests to the Black Sea coast, active artillery corridors make civilian gatherings an acute target; and across Western Europe, more than 6.5 million registered refugees remain dispersed throughout thousands of foreign municipalities.

For Ukraine's constitutional scholars and election administrators, this operational reality defines the most intricate legal and democratic dilemma faced by a European state since 1945: how to maintain constitutional legitimacy and democratic continuity when the basic physical apparatus of voting is under continuous bombardment.

The legal framework governing this suspension rests upon a structured interplay of constitutional provisions and primary wartime statutes. Under standard peacetime conditions, Article 103 of the Constitution of Ukraine sets a five-year mandate for the presidency. However, the constitutional framework is explicitly designed to avoid an executive power vacuum during wartime. While Article 83, Part 4 of the Constitution explicitly mandates the automatic extension of the Verkhovna Rada’s authority until a newly elected parliament holds its first session following the termination of martial law, executive continuity is governed by Article 108. This article mandates that the sitting President of Ukraine continues to exercise full constitutional powers until a newly elected successor assumes office.

This constitutional continuity is reinforced by statutory prohibition. Article 19 of the Law of Ukraine No. 389-VIII, "On the Legal Regime of Martial Law," explicitly bars the holding of presidential, parliamentary, and local elections while martial law is in force. Furthermore, Article 64 of the Constitution permits temporary restrictions on specific civil liberties during states of war, including the political and electoral participation rights guaranteed under Article 38.

"The architecture of the Ukrainian Constitution is built on the imperative of continuous state authority during foreign invasion," explains Ruslan Stefanchuk, Speaker of the Verkhovna Rada. "Article 108 is unambiguous: the state cannot be left without an operational commander-in-chief while defending its territory."

Beyond statutory mechanics, the Central Election Commission (CEC) has documented insurmountable operational obstacles. CEC Chairman Oleh Didenko and election specialists have outlined three core logistical barriers that prevent the execution of a free, fair, and secure democratic ballot under current conditions.

The first barrier is the disenfranchisement of the external electorate. Over 6.5 million Ukrainians reside abroad under temporary protection, primarily in Germany, Poland, the Czech Republic, and the UK. Ukraine’s diplomatic infrastructure contains only around 100 consular and embassy polling precincts worldwide—a network capable of processing fewer than 300,000 physical paper ballots. Facilitating full voter participation abroad would require establishing thousands of new extraterritorial polling stations across European jurisdictions, requiring unprecedented diplomatic agreements, foreign security details, and billions in administrative funding. While internet voting has been suggested by external commentators, the CEC and state cybersecurity agencies have rejected digital ballots, citing the severe risk of large-scale cyber interference and disruption from Russian state-sponsored hacking units.

The second barrier is frontline enfranchisement. More than 800,000 active-duty Ukrainian soldiers are deployed across combat zones in trenches, command bunkers, and mobile artillery units. International standards established by the OSCE and the Council of Europe’s Venice Commission require that democratic elections guarantee secret balloting, open candidate access, and unhindered debate. In an operating environment dominated by reconnaissance drones and guided aerial bombs, establishing physical voting stations near the line of contact would generate vulnerable mass-casualty targets. Furthermore, redeploying or disarming frontline brigades to manage balloting would undermine active defensive operations.

The third barrier concerns the occupied territories. With millions of Ukrainian citizens living under Russian occupation in Crimea, Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson, holding a national election that excludes these regions carries dangerous legal and sovereign implications. Under Ukrainian law, administering an election that cuts off occupied territories risks creating a de facto political division, permanently disenfranchising citizens who cannot cross military lines to cast a ballot.

These operational constraints have produced a unified stance across Ukraine’s domestic political spectrum. In November 2023, during meetings held under the European Parliament’s Jean Monnet Dialogue for Peace and Democracy in Zakarpattia, leaders of every parliamentary faction signed a formal memorandum. Signatories including Davyd Arakhamiia of the governing Servant of the People, former President Petro Poroshenko of European Solidarity, Yuliia Tymoshenko of Batkivshchyna, and Oleksandra Ustinova of Holos formally agreed that all national elections must be deferred until after the conclusion of the war and the formal lifting of martial law, followed by a mandatory preparation period of at least six months.

Civil society organizations have reinforced this consensus. More than 100 civic and electoral monitoring organizations, led by the Civil Network OPORA, released a joint appeal declaring that competitive elections during active hostilities would severely compromise national unity and security. "Democratic elections require genuine political competition, free media, and open debate," noted Olha Aivazovska, Chair of the Board at OPORA. "Introducing polarized partisan campaigning into a society under daily missile strikes would critically weaken domestic resilience."

Sociological data confirms that the public shares this assessment. Independent polling conducted by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS) and the Razumkov Centre shows that between 70% and 80% of Ukrainian citizens oppose holding wartime elections. In KIIS surveys evaluating institutional stability, over 70% of respondents affirmed that President Volodymyr Zelenskyy should remain in office until the conclusion of martial law.

Even with elections formally suspended, prospective political dynamics remain a subject of active analysis in Kyiv. Hypothetical polling modeling tested by research groups highlights significant public trust in military and institutional figures. While President Zelenskyy retains strong baseline support, General Valerii Zaluzhnyi—the former Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces who was appointed Ambassador to the United Kingdom in 2024—consistently records the highest public trust ratings in the country, often exceeding 85% in net confidence metrics. Intelligence Chief Lt. Gen. Kyrylo Budanov of the HUR also records broad positive trust, whereas former President Petro Poroshenko commands a committed partisan base alongside high negative ratings.

International diplomatic positioning has similarly evolved. While initial suggestions in 2023 from figures such as U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham and former PACE President Tiny Kox urged Ukraine to demonstrate wartime democratic processes, Western governments and international organizations subsequently recognized the physical and constitutional realities. Bipartisan consensus in Washington and European capitals has aligned with the view that forcing an election under bombardment would divert vital financial and military resources, create acute security vulnerabilities, and produce results easily contested by adversaries.

Meanwhile, the Kremlin has systematically attempted to exploit the calendar. Following May 20, 2024—the five-year mark of President Zelenskyy’s inauguration—Russian state media and officials launched sustained disinformation campaigns labeling the Ukrainian government "illegitimate." Constitutional scholars and international bodies have rejected these claims, pointing out that Ukrainian governance continuity strictly complies with domestic constitutional provisions and international legal standards, including Article 4 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which permits emergency derogations during existential crises.

Across public discourse, the consensus remains anchored in practical reality. As one prominent commentary on X summarized: "Expecting Ukraine to execute a nationwide election while absorbing daily ballistic strikes and managing millions of displaced citizens is an operational impossibility that misunderstands both the Ukrainian Constitution and the nature of modern war."

Inside the Central Election Commission, preparations for post-war governance continue methodically. The CEC has drafted comprehensive legislative proposals for the Verkhovna Rada, establishing a six-month transitional framework after martial law is lifted to audit and restore the State Voter Register, rebuild damaged precinct infrastructure, and establish enhanced overseas voting capabilities across Europe. Until security conditions permit that transition, Ukraine’s constitutional democracy relies on statutory continuity—preserving the legal order and territorial integrity necessary to ensure that future elections remain truly free, competitive, and sovereign.
