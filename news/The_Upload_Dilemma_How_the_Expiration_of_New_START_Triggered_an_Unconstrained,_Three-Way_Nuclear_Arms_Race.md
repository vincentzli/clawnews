# The Upload Dilemma: How the Expiration of New START Triggered an Unconstrained, Three-Way Nuclear Arms Race

When the clock struck midnight on February 5, 2026, the global nuclear order slipped quietly across an unprecedented threshold. For the first time since Richard Nixon and Leonid Brezhnev signed the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) Interim Agreement in Moscow in May 1972, the strategic nuclear forces of the United States and the Russian Federation ceased to be bound by any legally enforceable numerical limitations. The expiration of the New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START) eliminated the long-standing ceiling of 1,550 deployed strategic warheads distributed across 700 deployed intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and heavy bombers, alongside the cap of 800 deployed and non-deployed launchers.

The final diplomatic off-ramp collapsed in late 2025. Russian President Vladimir Putin had floated an informal proposal to maintain reciprocal adherence to the central 1,550 warhead and 700 launcher caps for an additional twelve months as a "unilateral sign of goodwill." Yet the diplomatic initiative immediately ran aground upon irreconcilable structural demands from both capitals. The administration of President Donald Trump insisted that any ongoing restraint was unviable without the immediate restoration of on-site verification and comprehensive telemetry data exchanges, which Moscow had unilaterally frozen in early 2023. Moscow countered with rigid preconditions: Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergey Ryabkov insisted that strategic stability could no longer be decoupled from European conventional theater security. Specifically, the Kremlin demanded binding limits on NATO conventional strike assets, pointing directly to Washington’s plans to station long-range conventional weapons—including ground-launched Tomahawk cruise missiles, SM-6 interceptors, and Dark Eagle hypersonic weapons—in Germany. Ryabkov declared that there was "no basis" for extending limits while Washington pursued Russia’s "strategic defeat," leading Moscow to formally terminate its self-imposed moratorium on intermediate-range missile deployments.

With the treaty dead, military planners in Washington, Moscow, and Beijing are no longer designing strategic posture around treaty accounting sheets, but around the cold physics of "upload capacity"—the latent technical ability of existing delivery systems to carry reserve warheads stored in defense depots.

Nowhere is that capacity more immediate than in the U.S. Navy’s sea-based leg of the nuclear triad. To comply with New START’s strict limits over the past decade, the Navy mechanically deactivated four of the 24 launch tubes on each of its 14 operational Ohio-class ballistic missile submarines, capping each boat at 20 operational tubes. Furthermore, the military "downloaded" its Trident II D5 missiles, configuring them to carry an average of four to five thermonuclear warheads—a mix of the 90-kiloton W76-1, the 8-kiloton low-yield W76-2, and the 455-kiloton W88—far below their design limit.

According to technical assessments by Hans Kristensen, director of the Nuclear Information Project at the Federation of American Scientists, removing these constraints allows for an immediate surge. By reactivating the deactivated tubes and loading Trident II D5 missiles to their maximum rated payload of eight independently targetable warheads (MIRVs), the U.S. submarine fleet can effectively double its deployed warhead count. Without manufacturing a single new rocket booster or laying a single submarine keel, the Pentagon possesses the technical ability to surge its sea-based strategic firepower from roughly 1,000 deployed warheads to nearly 1,900.

Yet this maritime surge capacity sits in sharp contrast to the financial and technical crisis paralyzing the triad’s land-based leg. The Air Force’s LGM-35A Sentinel ICBM modernization program, contracted to Northrop Grumman to replace 400 1970s-era Minuteman III missiles, suffered a statutory "critical" Nunn-McCurdy breach after acquisition costs surged 81% over baseline projections. A Pentagon review concluded in July 2024 confirmed that the program’s total acquisition cost ballooned from an initial baseline of $77.7 billion to $140.9 billion. The crisis stems not from the missile body itself, but from a massive civil engineering quagmire: unearthing and modernizing 450 launch facilities, 600 support structures, and over 7,500 miles of 70-year-old copper cabling across five western states.

The Sentinel cost explosion has set off a firestorm across defense policy circles and social media platforms. On Reddit’s defense and national security forums, skepticism has reached a boiling point, with users widely scrutinizing the Pentagon's initial engineering assumptions. "Initial estimates were criticized for relying on faulty assumptions, such as the feasibility of reusing 70-year-old infrastructure instead of installing new systems," noted one widely circulated analysis on r/warcollege, as users openly debated whether the Air Force should reconsider the Sentinel program entirely and redirect the roughly $141 billion toward expanding the Columbia-class submarine fleet.

Across the Eurasian landmass, Moscow is accelerating its own counter-deployment strategy. Russia has prioritized systems with heavy throw-weights designed to bypass Western missile defense networks. In Dombarovskiy, the Russian Strategic Missile Forces have continued the serial fielding of the Avangard hypersonic glide vehicle, which travels in the upper atmosphere at speeds exceeding Mach 20. Simultaneously, Moscow has moved toward full operational deployment of the RS-28 Sarmat, a super-heavy, liquid-fueled ICBM capable of carrying up to 10 heavy MIRVs or multiple hypersonic gliders across fractional orbital trajectories over either pole.

To signal its readiness below the strategic ceiling, Russia executed major unannounced non-strategic nuclear field drills in mid-2026 across the Northern and Pacific Fleets, following coordinated tactical delivery exercises with Belarusian Iskander-M units. These maneuvers coincided with Moscow’s formal revision of its nuclear doctrine in late 2024, which lowered the threshold for nuclear release to include conventional strikes that pose a critical threat to state sovereignty.

Compounding this bilateral breakdown is the arrival of the trilateral deterrence dilemma. For half a century, strategic stability was a two-body problem. Today, China’s nuclear breakout has transformed it into a three-body dynamic with no historical precedent. Pentagon assessments project that the People’s Liberation Army Rocket Force, having already surpassed 500 operational warheads, is on track to field more than 1,000 warheads by 2030 and 1,500 by 2035, supported by newly operational silo fields in Yumen, Hami, and Yulin. Beijing continues to reject all invitations to trilateral arms control discussions, pointing out that its stockpile remains dwarfed by the combined inventories of Washington and Moscow.

The bipartisan Congressional Commission on the Strategic Posture of the United States concluded that the nation faces two near-peer nuclear adversaries simultaneously for the first time in history, warning that the U.S. modernization program of record is "necessary but not sufficient." Pranay Vaddi, former Senior Director for Arms Control on the National Security Council, warned that Washington's restraint had structural limits: "Absent a change in the trajectory of adversary arsenals, we may reach a point in the coming years where an increase from current deployed numbers is required."

The human architecture holding back this expansion has completely vanished. UN Secretary-General António Guterres described the expiration of New START as a "grave moment for international peace and security," lamenting that the world has entered uncharted territory without legal guardrails. UN High Representative for Disarmament Affairs Izumi Nakamitsu noted that the treaty's end extinguished the bilateral notification system, which had processed more than 25,000 operational notices since 2011, along with the biannual data exchanges that provided verified counts of deployed warheads and launchers.

Rose Gottemoeller, the chief U.S. negotiator of New START and former NATO Deputy Secretary General, warned that without a verifiable verification framework, both powers are incentivized to engage in worst-case scenario planning. If one side suspects the other is uploading reserve warheads in secret, Gottemoeller noted, the other will "sprint away" in its own upload campaign. As Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, observed, the world has drifted into an "unconstrained, dangerous three-way arms race" with no treaty, no verification, and no dialogue.
