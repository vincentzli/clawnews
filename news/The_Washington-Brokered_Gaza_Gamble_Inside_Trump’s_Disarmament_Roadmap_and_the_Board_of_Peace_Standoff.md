# The Washington-Brokered Gaza Gamble: Inside Trump’s Disarmament Roadmap and the Board of Peace Standoff

Standing before reporters in Washington on July 31, 2026, U.S. President Donald Trump announced what he termed a "historic agreement" for the complete, phased disarmament of Hamas and other militant groups in the Gaza Strip. The draft roadmap, brokered after months of intense backchannel diplomacy, represents the most ambitious attempt to restructure the post-war enclave since the conflict began. Under the proposed framework, Palestinian factions would inventory and surrender their weapons to an International Stabilization Force (ISF) in exchange for a gradual, phased withdrawal of the Israel Defense Forces (IDF). Civilian governance would transition to the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), a 15-member transitional body of apolitical Palestinian technocrats led by Chief Commissioner Dr. Ali Shaath.

Yet beneath the triumphalist rhetoric emanating from Washington, the initiative is already colliding with deep-seated trust deficits and geopolitical friction between the United States, Israel, and regional stakeholders. The immediate point of contention is the sequencing of the agreement. While Hamas agreed to the text of the draft roadmap in principle, its political leadership has made it clear that any actual decommissioning of military infrastructure, tunnels, and weapons is strictly contingent upon a permanent end to Israeli military operations and a total IDF withdrawal. 

In Jerusalem, the response has been characterized by official silence and profound skepticism. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s office has declined to release a formal statement, but senior Israeli diplomatic officials have privately dismissed the timeline. The Israeli defense establishment, led by Israel Katz—who has served as Defense Minister since November 2024—remains resolute that no IDF withdrawal will occur without verified, total demilitarization of the enclave. Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar has similarly signaled that Israel will not compromise on its security oversight, raising doubts about whether the cabinet will ever authorize the final stages of the transition. Critics and political analysts argue that Netanyahu is leveraging this skepticism to manage domestic political pressures and navigate divisions within his governing coalition.

At the center of this diplomatic storm is the Board of Peace (BoP). Formally chartered in January 2026 and headquartered at the U.S. Institute of Peace in Washington, the BoP is an international administrative hybrid. It was established under the authority of UN Security Council Resolution 2803, which was adopted on November 17, 2025, to endorse a comprehensive peace plan for Gaza. Chaired by President Trump, the BoP has been granted international legal personality to coordinate reconstruction funding, manage the civil transition, and oversee the security apparatus in the strip. 

However, the BoP has drawn sharp criticism from international legal experts and diplomats. Critics argue that the Board is an ad-hoc, unilateral structure that effectively bypasses traditional UN peace processes and multilateral oversight. With a mandate extending until December 31, 2027, some human rights organizations warn that the BoP operates with minimal legislative accountability, risking the creation of an indefinite, foreign-led transitional administration that sidelines local political self-determination.

The governance transition is equally fraught. The NCAG, designed to manage day-to-day civil administration, has struggled to establish an operational foothold inside Gaza. Led by Dr. Ali Shaath, a Palestinian civil engineer and former undersecretary in the Palestinian Authority, the committee has spent months operating from Egypt due to Israeli travel restrictions on its members. While Hamas took the symbolic step in July 2026 of dissolving its own Government Emergency Committee to pave the way for the NCAG, hardline Israeli politicians have lobbied against Shaath’s leadership. They point to a leaked 2025 video in which Shaath discussed his participation in anti-occupation protests during his youth as evidence of bias, demanding his replacement.

The security transition faces even more daunting logistical hurdles. On July 26, 2026, the Israeli security cabinet granted initial approval for a pilot deployment of a limited ISF cohort. The plan envisions deploying approximately 200 personnel from friendly countries, specifically Morocco, Albania, and Kosovo, to oversee a humanitarian zone near Rafah. However, the operational reality of this force is highly restricted. The cohort can only deploy with the unanimous sign-off of Netanyahu, Defense Minister Katz, and Foreign Minister Sa'ar, and must operate in strict coordination with the IDF. Military analysts point to the significant operational mismatch of using 200 foreign troops to oversee the disarmament of thousands of entrenched fighters and the decommissioning of a subterranean tunnel network that once stretched for hundreds of miles. 

On social media platforms like X.com and Reddit’s r/politics, the reaction reflects these deep divisions. Skeptical users point out that similar transitional frameworks, such as those attempted in Lebanon under UN Resolution 1701, failed to prevent rearmament. "We've seen this movie before," wrote one widely shared post on r/politics. "An international force with no real mandate or muscle is just going to act as a shield for the next escalation." Meanwhile, proponents of the plan argue that the direct involvement of the U.S. presidency and the mobilization of regional states like Morocco offer a pragmatic, if imperfect, exit ramp from an endless war. 

As the December 2027 expiration of the Board of Peace’s mandate looms, the roadmap represents a high-stakes gamble. If Washington cannot bridge the gulf between Hamas’s demands for an immediate IDF exit and Israel’s insistence on verified demilitarization, this draft agreement will join a long history of failed ceasefires, leaving Gaza’s transitional administration as a government in name only.
