# The In-Memory Rollup: Inside MegaETH’s 100,000 TPS Architecture and the Deepening Fracture Over Ethereum Layer-2 Decentralization

With the operational conclusion of MegaETH’s "Terminal Season 1" ecosystem campaign and the formal reconciliation of its initial token allocations, MegaLabs has initiated the release of 250,000,000 $MEGA tokens—representing 2.5 percent of the network’s fixed 10-billion aggregate supply. The milestone, paired with liquidity backstops in decentralized automated market makers and scheduled exchange integrations, marks the financial and operational maturation of Ethereum’s most controversial scaling experiment. Following weeks of testnet activity that saw incentives distributed via both native token allocations and interim USDm stablecoin reward mechanisms, trading activity has transitioned to secondary venues, establishing an early multi-billion-dollar fully diluted valuation.

Yet the financial launch of $MEGA is secondary to the technical fault line the network has exposed. While dominant Layer-2 rollups such as Arbitrum One, Optimism, and Base process between 50 and 150 transactions per second (TPS) with block times rarely dipping below two seconds, MegaETH claims a sustained execution throughput of 100,000 TPS and sub-10-millisecond state update latencies, pushing the EVM to its physical execution boundary.

MegaLabs—co-founded in 2023 by MIT CSAIL PhD Lei Yang, Stanford computer science PhD Yilong Li, and former ConsenSys business development executive Shuyao Kong—identifies the primary constraint of modern blockchains not as cryptographic consensus, but as hardware input/output (I/O) latency. In traditional EVM implementations such as Geth, executing a transaction requires reading from and writing to a Merkle Patricia Trie stored on solid-state drives (SSDs) or NVMe disks. Even high-throughput enterprise flash storage introduces access latencies measured in microseconds, creating an insurmountable bottleneck when processing tens of thousands of concurrent state reads and writes.

MegaETH circumvents this physical disk bottleneck through an architecture built entirely on in-memory state execution. Utilizing a specialized data structure known as SALT (Small Authentication Large Trie), MegaETH maintains its state authentication structures and global account data directly in dynamic random-access memory (RAM). State transitions occur at nanosecond memory-bus speeds rather than millisecond disk-access cycles. By pairing RAM-resident state with just-in-time (JIT) compilation that dynamically compiles EVM bytecode into native machine instructions, the protocol achieves streaming, continuous execution instead of conventional block-by-block batching.

Achieving this throughput, however, requires a deliberate departure from the hardware parity that has defined Ethereum’s client ecosystem. MegaETH relies on an enterprise-grade, centralized single sequencer. The production sequencer requires a dedicated enterprise server equipped with 100 or more CPU cores, between 1 and 4 terabytes of RAM, high-speed NVMe storage for persistent archival, and networking interfaces scaling from 10 Gbps up to 100 Gbps for high-throughput cluster interconnects.

This severe hardware requirement has ignited a fierce architectural debate among Ethereum researchers, rollup engineers, and independent analytics groups like L2BEAT. For years, the core ethos of Ethereum’s Layer-2 roadmap centered on permissionless verification—the principle that any individual with consumer hardware can validate state transitions and enforce the rules of the chain. In a single-sequencer architecture where the ordering engine requires enterprise data-center infrastructure, critics argue that the network concentrates systemic operational power into a single point of failure.

Researchers at L2BEAT have consistently categorized centralized sequencer configurations as carrying severe trust assumptions. In a centralized execution setup, the sequencer maintains discretionary authority over transaction ordering, creating latency monopolies, risks of selective transaction censorship, and exposure to centralized maximal extractable value (MEV) exploitation. If the single sequencer experiences an outage or a network-level denial-of-service event, real-time transaction processing halts immediately.

MegaLabs leadership and its institutional backers argue that critics misunderstand the core mechanics of rollup security. MegaLabs secured a $20 million seed funding round in June 2024 led by Dragonfly Capital, with angel participation from Ethereum co-founder Vitalik Buterin, ConsenSys founder Joseph Lubin, EigenLayer founder Sreeram Kannan, and investor Jordan Fish (Cobie), whose Echo platform later hosted a $10 million community funding round.

Proponents point directly to Vitalik Buterin’s 2021 research framework, "Endgame." In that analysis, Buterin posited that while block production would inevitably centralize onto high-spec hardware to satisfy demand for extreme scalability, the network retains decentralization and trustlessness provided that verification remains cheap, lightweight, and accessible to ordinary users.

MegaLabs CTO Lei Yang maintains that MegaETH fulfills this criterion through a model of radical node specialization. The system explicitly decouples transaction execution from block verification:
- The Sequencer: A singular, enterprise server responsible exclusively for real-time ordering and in-memory execution.
- Full Nodes: Machines running on 16-core processors and 64 gigabytes of RAM that re-execute state transitions to provide localized trust anchors.
- Replica Nodes: Lightweight validating nodes requiring only 8-core CPUs and 8 gigabytes of RAM. These nodes do not re-execute every transaction from scratch; instead, they consume pre-computed state diffs streamed directly from the sequencer over a peer-to-peer network.
- Provers and Settlement: Cryptographic proofs and transaction commitments are published to EigenDA for high-bandwidth data availability, while final state settlement is anchored directly into Ethereum Layer-1 smart contracts.

Under this paradigm, Yang argues, a centralized sequencer cannot forge unauthorized transactions or steal user funds. If the sequencer attempts an invalid state transition, replica nodes and Ethereum Layer-1 contracts will reject the submission based on cryptographic dispute proofs, preserving Ethereum's ultimate base-layer security guarantee.
