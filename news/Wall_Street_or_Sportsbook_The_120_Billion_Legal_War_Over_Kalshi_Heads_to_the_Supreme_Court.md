# Wall Street or Sportsbook? The $120 Billion Legal War Over Kalshi Heads to the Supreme Court

When the United States Supreme Court dismantled the federal ban on sports wagering in May 2018 in *Murphy v. NCAA*, the legal victory belonged entirely to New Jersey. The state had spent nearly a decade and millions of dollars in litigation asserting its Tenth Amendment right to regulate and tax sports gambling within its borders. That landmark ruling unleashed an unprecedented wave of state legislation: thirty-eight states and the District of Columbia established state-licensed wagering frameworks, creating an industry that handles upwards of $120 billion annually and channels more than $11 billion in tax revenue into state treasuries and civic programs.

Eight years later, New Jersey has returned to One First Street, petitioning the justices to prevent federal commodities law from preempting the very state regulatory system it fought to establish.

On September 2, 2026, New Jersey Solicitor General Jeremy Michael Feigenbaum filed a petition for a writ of certiorari, docketed as No. 26-299, on behalf of Mary Jo Flaherty, Interim Director of the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement (DGE). The petition asks the Supreme Court to overturn an April 6, 2026 ruling by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit in *KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty*. In a 2–1 decision authored by Circuit Judge David J. Porter and joined by Chief Judge Michael A. Chagares, the Third Circuit affirmed a preliminary injunction barring New Jersey regulators from enforcing state gambling statutes against KalshiEX LLC, a designated contract market registered with the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC).

Judge Porter’s majority held that Kalshi’s event contracts—which permit retail participants to trade binary positions on athletic outcomes such as championship winners, margin of victory, and point spreads—satisfy the definition of "swaps" under Title VII of the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. Under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA), codified at 7 U.S.C. § 2(a)(1)(A), Congress vested the CFTC with exclusive jurisdiction over designated swap exchanges. The Third Circuit concluded that this federal oversight preempts state gaming enforcement, reasoning that because the contracts trade on a federally licensed exchange, individual states cannot regulate, license, or tax them as unlawful gambling. In her dissent, Circuit Judge Jane Richards Roth cautioned that gambling regulation has historically fallen under state police powers, arguing that federal commodities law should not be presumed to supersede local enforcement absent unambiguous congressional command.

That decision stood as a major victory for the prediction market sector until August 28, 2026, when the legal landscape fractured. In *KalshiEX LLC v. Assad*, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit unanimously rejected the Third Circuit’s statutory interpretation. Writing for the panel, Circuit Judge Ryan D. Nelson, joined by Judges Kenneth K. Lee and Bridget S. Bade, dissolved a preliminary injunction that had prevented the Nevada Gaming Control Board—whose members include George Assad and Chairman Mike Dreitzer—from enforcing state gaming licensing laws against Kalshi.

The Ninth Circuit focused on 7 U.S.C. § 1a(47)(A)(ii), which defines a swap as an agreement dependent upon an occurrence or contingency "associated with a potential financial, economic, or commercial consequence." While Kalshi contended that sports outcomes carry commercial consequences by affecting team merchandise sales, broadcast revenues, and municipal economies, the Ninth Circuit held that downstream economic ripple effects do not convert athletic contests into commercial events under the statute. The panel noted that Kalshi’s core product functions as a traditional sports wager rather than an instrument designed to hedge preexisting commercial enterprise risk. To reinforce the point, the court cited Kalshi’s own 2024 D.C. Circuit brief, where the company had acknowledged that "the classic example" of gaming "is a contract on the outcome of a sporting event." Following the ruling, Chairman Dreitzer publicly reiterated Nevada’s position: "Placing sports bets even when called by another name is still gambling."

The regulatory rift widened further on September 16, 2026. In *Blue Lake Rancheria v. Kalshi, Inc.*, the Ninth Circuit ruled that sports event contracts traded by users physically located on tribal reservations constitute Class III gaming under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act (IGRA). The court held that a wager legally occurs where the bettor is located, rather than where the trading exchange’s servers reside, and affirmed that the CEA does not displace tribal authority to regulate gambling on sovereign land.

The divergent appellate rulings have created an acute circuit split. Across the Third Circuit’s jurisdiction in New Jersey, Pennsylvania, and Delaware, Kalshi operates under federal preemption protection. Across the Ninth Circuit’s territory—including Nevada, California, and Arizona—offering identical sports event contracts leaves the exchange open to state cease-and-desist mandates, civil penalties, and tribal regulatory challenges.

The financial ramifications of this divide prompted swift collective action from state lawmakers. In an amicus curiae brief filed with the Supreme Court in September, the National Council of Legislators from Gaming States (NCLGS) argued that nationwide federal preemption would destabilize state budgets. The coalition emphasized that the existing state regulatory model protects an estimated $11 billion in state sports wagering tax revenues derived from a $120 billion legal handle. Under state systems, commercial sportsbooks must secure state licenses, pay state privilege taxes that reach up to 51% of gross revenue in states like New York, enforce strict 21-and-over age limits, contribute to compulsive gambling treatment funds, and submit algorithms to independent testing laboratories. In contrast, Kalshi operates under federal CFTC rules: it admits retail participants at age 18, operates on an exchange transaction-fee model, and pays zero state gaming taxes.

Commercial sportsbook operators—including DraftKings, Flutter Entertainment’s FanDuel, and BetMGM—view Kalshi’s fintech classification as an attempt to bypass the high-cost regulatory frameworks that define the legal sports betting industry. The American Gaming Association (AGA), the national trade body led by President and CEO Bill Miller, has mounted an aggressive campaign against sports prediction markets, labeling them "backdoor sports gambling" designed to circumvent state laws. In congressional testimony and policy briefings, Miller pointed to AGA survey data showing that 85% of Americans view contracts on athletic outcomes as sports gambling rather than financial derivative trading, warning that unmonitored platforms threaten consumer protection and game integrity.

The courtroom fight has escalated into a high-stakes proxy contest between Silicon Valley venture capital and traditional gaming establishments. Kalshi, supported by venture funding from firms including Sequoia Capital and Charles Schwab, has retained former Acting U.S. Solicitor General Neal K. Katyal of Milbank LLP to direct its Supreme Court representation. On September 18, 2026, Katyal submitted a formal application for a 30-day extension to file Kalshi’s brief in opposition to New Jersey's petition, extending the response deadline to November 9, 2026.

Public discourse across financial and sports betting communities underscores the underlying contradiction. In forums such as Reddit's r/wallstreetbets, retail traders regularly discuss the battle between traditional bookmakers like DraftKings and prediction markets, with users debating whether event contracts represent an innovative financial frontier or simply a re-labeled betting product attempting to dodge state tax levies. On X, legal scholars and sports betting analysts have noted the historical irony of the dispute: New Jersey, having fought for years to establish state primacy in *Murphy*, must now convince the Supreme Court that the Commodity Exchange Act cannot be stretched to transform athletic wagers into Wall Street swaps.

As the Supreme Court considers Docket No. 26-299, the justices face a definitive constitutional choice. Upholding the Third Circuit would establish a federal preemption standard that circumvents state gambling oversight for any platform operating as a designated contract market under the CFTC. Conversely, siding with New Jersey and the Ninth Circuit would reaffirm state police powers, restrict Dodd-Frank swaps to genuine commercial risk management, and require prediction markets to seek licenses state by state.
