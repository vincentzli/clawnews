# The Amman Conduit: Inside the Secret Talks and Covert Border War Between Israel and Post-Assad Damascus

In late August 2026, inside a heavily guarded government villa on the outskirts of Amman, two men whose governments remain technically in a state of war sat down across an oval conference table. On one side sat Asaad al-Shaibani, the foreign minister of Syria’s post-Assad transitional administration. On the other sat Major General Roman Gofman, the director of Israel’s Mossad and former military secretary to Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. 

Mediated by Jordanian intelligence officials and monitored via secure communications by United States Special Envoy Tom Barrack, the clandestine meeting on August 23, 2026, was not convened to celebrate a diplomatic breakthrough. Instead, it was an urgent exercise in tactical crisis management. Just five days earlier, Israeli combat aircraft had carried out eight precision strikes against the dormant runways and hangars of the Abu al-Duhur military airfield in Syria’s northwestern Idlib province. Jerusalem defended the operation as a preemptive measure to block Turkey from deploying air-defense and radar systems; Damascus condemned it as an unprovoked violation of territorial integrity; and Ankara dismissed Israel's claims as fabricated pretext.

The encounter in Amman underscored the contradictory realities defining post-Assad bilateral interactions: back-channel diplomatic diplomacy overshadowed by kinetic border clashes. Following the fall of Bashar al-Assad on December 8, 2024, the security architecture that had governed the Golan Heights frontier under the 1974 United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF) agreement disintegrated. Within hours of the regime's collapse, the Israel Defense Forces advanced past the armistice line, seizing strategic outposts on the Syrian slopes of Mount Hermon and entering Quneitra province. Over the subsequent twenty months, this presence developed into an entrenched, 12-kilometer-deep military perimeter. Known as the "New East" project, Israeli forces constructed all-weather military corridors—including the "Moshik Axis"—excavated anti-tank ditches, and deployed sensor networks extending to within 12 kilometers of the Damascus-Beirut highway.

Washington and regional intermediaries initially attempted to steer the political opening toward a comprehensive security framework. On January 6, 2026, during multilateral negotiations in Paris, Israeli officials—advised by former Strategic Affairs Minister Ron Dermer—and Syrian representatives led by al-Shaibani signed an agreement establishing a "Joint Fusion Mechanism." Formulated as a trilateral communication and intelligence-sharing cell supervised by the United States, the Paris framework sought to de-escalate military frictions, monitor extremist remnants, and maintain functional hotlines.

However, the diplomatic track has collided with rigid political realities on both sides of the border. Syrian Transitional President Ahmed al-Sharaa remains constrained by a coalition of nationalist and Islamist factions. For Damascus, establishing formal political relations or economic agreements while foreign troops occupy sovereign territory is an existential red line. In Amman, Syrian negotiators reiterated that no comprehensive political settlement can materialize without a complete Israeli withdrawal to the 1974 disengagement lines.

Domestic political calculations in Israel present a matching barrier. In the aftermath of October 7, 2023, Israeli strategic doctrine has prioritized territorial depth. A nationwide poll conducted in June 2026 by the Jerusalem Center for Security and Foreign Affairs showed that 60 percent of Israelis support maintaining a military presence in the newly controlled Syrian security zone. Prime Minister Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz have described the Mount Hermon summits and the Quneitra buffer zone as non-negotiable security assets, with Katz stating that troops will remain deployed there for an indefinite period.

Public opinion in Syria reflects an equally entrenched opposition to compromise. A nationwide study published in Foreign Policy found that only 14 percent of Syrians support diplomatic normalization with Israel, while 92 percent consider Israeli ground operations and airstrikes an acute threat to their security.

This bilateral standoff is further aggravated by direct geopolitical competition between Israel and Turkey on Syrian soil. As Ankara moves to deepen institutional and military relations with al-Sharaa’s government—highlighted by an August 6, 2026 meeting between Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan and al-Shaibani to establish a joint strategic coordination council—Israel has enforced a doctrine of "denial before emplacement." The strike on Abu al-Duhur was calculated to signal that Jerusalem will not tolerate Turkish radar or missile systems capable of tracking Israeli air movements. Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan countered by declaring that Ankara will not allow outside powers to dictate its regional policies. The escalation also provoked friction with Washington, where Special Envoy Barrack publicly characterized the Abu al-Duhur strikes as an "unnecessary escalation," stating that U.S. intelligence found no evidence of an imminent Turkish deployment at the airfield.

The military friction and subsequent diplomatic maneuvers have provoked intense commentary across open-source tracking networks and social media platforms. On online forums such as Reddit's r/syriancivilwar, observers noted that Israel is executing a calculated strategy to "boil the frog," methodically destroying Syria’s dormant military infrastructure before the transitional administration can consolidate national air defenses. At the same time, regional commentators on X highlighted Damascus’s difficult strategic position, arguing that while military restraint avoids an asymmetric war with Israel, it risks allowing the 12-kilometer buffer zone to solidify into a permanent international border.

With the United Nations Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Syria warning in September 2026 that unilateral fortifications and forced civilian exclusions in Quneitra could violate international humanitarian law, any remaining prospects for formal normalization have vanished. Diplomatic contacts between al-Shaibani and Gofman have been stripped of expansive political ambitions, reduced instead to tactical deconfliction intended to prevent miscalculations between Israeli, Turkish, and Syrian forces from sparking a broader conflict.
