# Blackmail, Redactions, and the Gates Deposition: The Bipartisan Battle Over the Epstein Library

The release of 3,500 pages of closed-door depositions by the House Oversight Committee on June 23, 2026, has reopened one of the most contentious investigative chapters in recent American history. Over several weeks in June, lawmakers grilled some of the closest associates of the late sex offender Jeffrey Epstein, seeking to reconstruct both the operations of his sex trafficking network and the extensive web of leverage he held over the global elite. Among the most anticipated documents are the detailed transcripts of Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates and Epstein's longtime administrative assistant, Lesley Groff. The disclosures come amid a broader legal and legislative war over the "Epstein Library," a massive repository of Department of Justice files released under the Epstein Files Transparency Act of November 2025, which critics say has been systematically sanitized.

On June 10, 2026, Bill Gates sat for a six-hour voluntary deposition before the committee. Under questioning from lawmakers, including Chairman James Comer (R-KY) and Oversight Committee Ranking Member Robert Garcia (D-CA), Gates repeated his public stance that his meetings with Epstein—which began in 2011, two years after Epstein’s initial solicitation conviction—were a "grave error in judgment." He maintained that his association, which ended in 2015, was strictly motivated by philanthropic efforts to secure funding for the Gates Foundation's global health initiatives.

However, the transcript contains a far more jarring disclosure: Epstein attempted to use knowledge of Gates's personal life as leverage. According to Gates, Epstein discovered that Gates had had three extramarital affairs, including a relationship with Mila Antonova, a Russian bridge player whom Epstein had met and funded for a software coding school. Gates testified that while Epstein never explicitly issued a formal blackmail demand—noting that the financier "never sent me anything that I would call blackmail"—emails in the government's possession indicate Epstein was actively "brainstorming" and "rehearsing" how to weaponize this information to keep Gates in his orbit after Gates tried to distance himself in 2014. "He tried unsuccessfully to leverage a past relationship to threaten me," Gates told investigators, detailing how a 2017 email from Epstein demanding reimbursement for Antonova’s coding school costs was a veiled threat to expose the affair.

The day before Gates's deposition, on June 9, 2026, Lesley Groff underwent eight hours of intense questioning. Having served as Epstein’s executive assistant for nearly two decades, from 1999 until his arrest in 2019, Groff was the administrative gatekeeper of his daily life. She managed his schedule, booked flights, and coordinated appointments, including the numerous "massages" that victims later identified as the primary venue for Epstein’s sex trafficking.

Groff maintained a defense of complete ignorance. In her opening remarks, she stated, "I believe that my testimony will dispel the false notion that because of my employment with Epstein, I must have knowingly enabled or conspired with him to commit his evil acts." Under questioning from lawmakers, who expressed deep skepticism that someone in her position could remain oblivious for 18 years, Groff portrayed Epstein as a "master manipulator" who kept his "legitimate life" and his "secret life as an abuser" completely separate. When pressed on the sheer volume of "massage" bookings, Groff testified that she did not know the ages of the women she scheduled. "I thought that it was just something that he did, like going to the gym," she said, claiming she never visited Epstein's private island, Little St. James, or his ranches, and never socialized with him. This defense drew sharp skepticism from committee members, who questioned how a long-term administrative gatekeeper could fail to notice the patterns of abuse that formed the basis of federal sex trafficking charges.

The backdrop to these testimonies is the Epstein Files Transparency Act, a bipartisan bill sponsored by Representatives Thomas Massie (R-KY) and Ro Khanna (D-CA) in the House and Senators Jeff Merkley (D-OR) and Ben Ray Luján (D-NM) in the Senate. Signed into law by President Donald Trump in November 2025, the Act mandated that the DOJ release all unclassified records regarding the Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell investigations by December 2025. The Biden administration had previously resisted the full release, citing ongoing national security and law enforcement sensitivities.

On January 30, 2026, the DOJ established the online "Epstein Library," releasing 3.5 million pages of documents. But the release immediately triggered a political storm. Congressional investigators, independent journalists, and victim advocates pointed out massive gaps. The DOJ had collected over 6 million pages of records, meaning nearly half of the archive remained withheld or heavily redacted. Key omissions included deleted email attachments, missing FBI summaries, and the highly sought-after surveillance footage from the Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) in New York, where Epstein died in August 2019. The DOJ claimed the omissions were necessary to protect victim privacy and ongoing investigations, but critics alleged the department was violating the law’s explicit prohibition against redacting for "reputational harm" or "political sensitivity."

This institutional resistance culminated in a major legal clash. Journalist Katie Phang filed a federal lawsuit, *Phang v. Blanche*, against the DOJ and Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, who was nominated by President Trump on June 3, 2026, to permanently lead the department. In a landmark decision on June 25, 2026, U.S. District Judge Emmet Sullivan ruled in Phang's favor. Judge Sullivan ordered the DOJ to either release unredacted versions of specific files—including draft indictments containing the names of potential co-conspirators and emails with redacted sender information—or provide a detailed legal justification for each redaction by July 2, 2026.

The political fallout of these disclosures has reverberated across Washington and social media. On platforms like Reddit's r/politics, the reaction has been polarized. Some users have expressed deep cynicism, arguing that the committee's focus on Bill Gates is a political distraction designed to shield other prominent political figures whose names remain redacted. Many commenters on r/politics debated whether the focus on Gates was a strategic distraction, with some calling the congressional focus a PR stunt while others insisted that all individuals linked to the network must face full scrutiny, regardless of their political status.

For congressional investigators, the next battleground is the July 2 court deadline. If the DOJ refuses to lift the redactions, it could set up a direct constitutional crisis between the judiciary, Congress, and the executive branch. Representative Massie, who pushed the original legislation, has warned that Congress will use its power of the purse to force compliance if the DOJ continues to withhold the remaining 3 million pages. For now, the transcripts of Gates and Groff provide a rare window into the mechanics of Epstein's influence, but the full picture remains locked inside the DOJ's redacted vaults.
