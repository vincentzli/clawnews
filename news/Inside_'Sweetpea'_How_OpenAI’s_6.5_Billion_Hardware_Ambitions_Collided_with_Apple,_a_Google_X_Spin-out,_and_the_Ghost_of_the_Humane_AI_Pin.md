# Inside 'Sweetpea': How OpenAI’s $6.5 Billion Hardware Ambitions Collided with Apple, a Google X Spin-out, and the Ghost of the Humane AI Pin

In late August 2026, OpenAI’s ambitions to build the definitive post-smartphone consumer device remain stalled in a web of federal litigation and engineering delays. Leaked court documents from mid-2026 have confirmed that the shipping timeline for the screenless AI companion, developed in collaboration with Jony Ive’s design firm LoveFrom, has been pushed back from late 2026 to no earlier than late February 2027. Codenamed "Sweetpea" within the company, the device represents OpenAI's high-stakes entry into consumer hardware. Yet, as the venture progresses, it is defined less by design breakthroughs and more by a multi-front legal assault and deep market skepticism.

The project’s troubles began to compound shortly after OpenAI completed a $6.5 billion acquisition of io Products, a hardware startup co-founded by Ive and several former Apple design executives, in May 2025. The acquisition was intended to establish an instant hardware division for OpenAI, pairing Ive’s minimalist design ethos with OpenAI's frontier AI models. The envisioned product was a screenless, donut-shaped speaker with a central opening, priced between $300 and $400. Described internally as an ambient, portable companion, the device was designed to utilize cameras, microphones, and motorized moving parts to interact with its physical environment, providing physical feedback to the user. The design was meant to champion "calm computing"—a philosophy aimed at reducing screen addiction by replacing visual interfaces with natural voice and spatial awareness.

Instead, the "calm computing" initiative has sparked a tempest. The first blow arrived in June 2025 when iyO, Inc., an audio startup spun out of Google’s X lab in 2021, filed a trademark infringement and trade secret lawsuit in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. Founded by Jason Rugolo, who previously ran the X lab's "Project Wolverine" to develop voice-isolating wearables, iyO had spent years developing its own screenless ear-worn audio computer, the "iyO ONE." 

According to court filings, iyO alleged that OpenAI’s "io" brand name was a confusingly similar homophone. More damagingly, iyO claimed that OpenAI and Ive's team had accessed its proprietary product roadmaps and technical specifications during private partnership pitch meetings in 2022. The legal skirmish quickly went against OpenAI. In December 2025, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit affirmed a temporary restraining order against OpenAI. This was followed on April 23, 2026, by a preliminary injunction granted by U.S. District Judge Trina L. Thompson, which barred OpenAI from using the "io" brand. Forced to abandon the name, OpenAI rebranded the project internally to "Sweetpea." While the two companies reached an agreement in principle to settle the litigation in late July 2026, the branding disruption and associated redesigns contributed significantly to the product's timeline slipping into 2027.

OpenAI’s legal worries escalated dramatically on July 10, 2026, when Apple filed a massive trade-secret misappropriation lawsuit against OpenAI, io Products, and two key hardware executives in the Northern District of California. The complaint alleges that OpenAI waged a coordinated campaign to recruit former Apple staff—now numbering over 400 at OpenAI—and pressure them into violating their non-disclosure agreements to steal proprietary hardware secrets.

The named defendants include Tang Yew Tan, OpenAI's Chief Hardware Officer and former Apple Vice President of product design for the iPhone and Apple Watch, and Chang Liu, a former senior systems electrical engineer at Apple who joined OpenAI in 2026. Apple's complaint contains granular allegations: it accuses Tan of using internal Apple project codenames to solicit trade secrets during job interviews, and asking candidates to bring confidential Apple components, such as chips and logic boards, to OpenAI offices for "show and tell" sessions. The lawsuit also claims that Chang Liu exploited an authentication bug to download sensitive engineering documents from Apple's servers after his resignation. Furthermore, Apple alleges that OpenAI's hardware team misled supply-chain partners into using Apple’s proprietary metal-finishing techniques by falsely claiming they had Apple's authorization.

Conspicuously absent from the list of defendants is Jony Ive himself. Analysts suggest that Apple’s decision to spare its former design chief is a calculated move. Ive maintains a close personal relationship with Laurene Powell Jobs, the widow of Steve Jobs. Sparing Ive allows Apple to pursue the poached talent and protect its proprietary data without provoking a high-profile personal feud that could damage delicate institutional relationships.

OpenAI has fired back in court. On August 5, 2026, the company filed a 31-page motion to dismiss, characterizing Apple's lawsuit as "careless, aggressive and oddly personal," and arguing that Apple has failed to identify specific, protectable trade secrets. Apple countered in its opposition brief on August 20, 2026, asserting that OpenAI's defense is built on "distortion, speculation, and improper extrinsic evidence." A critical preliminary injunction hearing is scheduled for October 1, 2026.

While the lawyers trade briefs, the public reaction to the leaked details of "Sweetpea" has been lukewarm. On platforms like X and Reddit, tech enthusiasts have expressed deep skepticism about the utility of a screenless, voice-first device. Critics point to the failures of the Humane AI Pin—which suffered from severe overheating and shipped fewer than 10,000 units before shutting down operations and selling its assets to HP in early 2025—and the Rabbit R1, which was widely panned on Reddit as a "$200 toy" and "an Android app in a plastic housing." 

Commentators in online forums have questioned the value proposition of a screenless, motorized AI companion, arguing that the device offers less utility than a standard smartphone. Others have raised alarm bells over the physical specifications of the device. A portable companion equipped with cameras, microphones, and motorized moving parts designed to track its environment raises significant privacy concerns. Critics argue that a device designed to be "always listening" and "always looking" is a privacy nightmare in search of a market.

For OpenAI, the path to February 2027 is fraught. Even if the company resolves its trademark issues and survives the October preliminary injunction hearing with Apple, it must still convince a skeptical public that a screenless, motorized AI donut is the future of computing, rather than the next addition to the silicon graveyard.
