# Nvidia’s $81.6B Q1: China Revenue Drops to Zero as Hyperscalers Mask Geopolitical Risk

Nvidia just reported a blowout first quarter for Fiscal Year 2027, posting a record $81.6 billion in total revenue—an 85% year-over-year increase. Data Center revenue accounted for the lion's share at $75.2 billion (up 92% year-over-year), driving an adjusted EPS of $1.87 and a GAAP EPS of $2.39. On the surface, CEO Jensen Huang’s execution seems flawless as the company raised its quarterly dividend to $0.25 and added $80 billion to its share repurchase authorization. Yet, beneath the headline numbers, a massive $4.6 billion structural hole has opened up in the company’s revenue base, sparking fierce debate among economists, traders, and analysts.

The tension centers on a stark admission from CFO Colette Kress during the earnings call: Data Center Hopper architecture revenue from China has fallen to exactly zero. Just four quarters ago, Hopper shipments to China generated $4.6 billion. The evaporation of a market that previously accounted for roughly 20% to 25% of Nvidia’s data center business is a direct result of tightening U.S. export controls. Despite rumors late last year of potential workarounds or special licensing under the Trump administration, Kress confirmed the company is assuming zero Data Center compute revenue from China in its forward-looking guidance.

For now, that massive geographical void has been completely masked by insatiable domestic demand. Major U.S. cloud providers and hyperscalers are fully absorbing the supply, driving the rapid ramp of Nvidia’s Blackwell 300 products. Hopper products were explicitly described by management as "sold out in the cloud," as cloud providers utilize existing infrastructure to bridge the gap to the Blackwell transition. Nvidia issued an aggressive Q2 FY27 revenue guidance of $91.0 billion (±2%), indicating absolute confidence that domestic and allied-nation CAPEX will more than compensate for the lost Chinese market.

However, the social media trading ecosystem—where the true market friction is often debated—is highly skeptical of this dependency. On Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets, traders are increasingly questioning the sustainability of this domestic-only hyperscaler boom. One prominent thread perfectly captured the anxiety over market breadth: "Market looks strong but is it basically just NVDA and friends carrying everything? Index keeps grinding up, AI narrative everywhere, but when you actually look under the surface, a few mega caps doing all the heavy lifting, everything else either chops or fades."

The contrarian argument taking hold on Fintwit and r/stocks is that hyperscalers cannot sustain this level of aggressive infrastructure spending indefinitely. If domestic cloud providers decelerate their CAPEX, Nvidia no longer has the Chinese market as a shock absorber. As one highly upvoted comment on r/stocks noted in a debate over AI valuations, "No one read all that. It's about forward PE and forward revenue and growth. If NVDA comes out tomorrow and says sales will drop 20% they will drop faster than anything." Another user pointed out the historical precedents for infrastructure bubbles: "Like in previous technological advances there will a few winners and very many losers. Whenever too much money is invested in one sector the ROI is not great."

The $91 billion question for Q2 is whether the Blackwell transition can sustain the hyper-growth narrative while operating in a fractured global market. Retail sentiment is showing fatigue with the constant beat-and-raise cycles that fail to permanently elevate the stock. "Honestly, over the past few quarters, NVDA has delivered incredible numbers almost every time, yet the stock often ends up falling the next day anyway," a frustrated trader observed. 

With Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent overseeing an administration focused on intense economic statecraft and sanctions enforcement, the regulatory wall blocking Nvidia from China is unlikely to lift. For now, the domestic AI factory buildout is large enough to paper over a $4.6 billion shortfall, but the margin for error has evaporated. If hyperscaler demand slips by even a fraction, Nvidia’s $91 billion guidance will face a brutal reality check.
