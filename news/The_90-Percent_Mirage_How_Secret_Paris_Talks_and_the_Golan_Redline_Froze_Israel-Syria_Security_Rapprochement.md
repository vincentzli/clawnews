# The 90-Percent Mirage: How Secret Paris Talks and the Golan Redline Froze Israel-Syria Security Rapprochement

When Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa addressed an Atlantic Council forum in New York during the 81st United Nations General Assembly in late September 2026, he publicly detailed the progress of the Middle East’s most sensitive backchannel. For nearly a year, al-Sharaa disclosed, Syrian and Israeli delegations had conducted direct negotiations mediated by the United States, advancing toward a bilateral security pact. The two governments, he stated, had reached "nearly 90% agreement" on technical security protocols intended to de-escalate frontier friction following the December 8, 2024 collapse of the Bashar al-Assad administration.

Yet within forty-eight hours, that diplomatic progress encountered a rigid public impasse. Addressing the UN General Assembly on September 23, al-Sharaa declared that the Golan Heights "will remain Syrian territory" and demanded that the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) withdraw entirely to the demarcations established under the 1974 Agreement on Disengagement. Taking the rostrum the following morning, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu rejected Damascus’s territorial demands: "Mr. al-Sharaa, you should know the Jews have been on the Golan Heights since the days of Moses. You can read it in the Bible."

The clash exposed an unbridgeable structural divergence that months of discreet diplomacy in Europe had managed to compartmentalize but not resolve. The bilateral channel had been formalized through the "Joint Fusion Mechanism," a trilateral coordination framework announced by the U.S. State Department on January 6, 2026, following a fifth round of U.S.-brokered talks in Paris. Led on the American side by Special Envoy for Syria Tom Barrack alongside White House advisers Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, the Paris negotiations convened Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad Hassan al-Shaibani, Syrian intelligence chief Hussein Salameh, Israeli Ambassador to the U.S. Yechiel Leiter, National Security Council director Gil Reich, and Military Secretary Roman Gofman.

Operating under U.S. supervision, the Joint Fusion Mechanism was structured as a communication cell tasked with intelligence sharing, military de-escalation, and dispute mitigation along the border. The 90% consensus cited by al-Sharaa covered extensive operational measures: direct emergency hotlines connecting military commanders, standardized warning procedures for troop movements across Quneitra and Daraa, and shared intelligence parameters aimed at neutralizing residual Hezbollah cells and illicit arms transfers.

The negotiations stalled over conflicting demands regarding troop positions and territorial sovereignty. Damascus insisted that any finalized security agreement be contingent upon an unconditional Israeli military pullback to the positions held prior to December 8, 2024. In the days following Assad’s fall, the IDF moved past the 1974 Alpha Line into the United Nations-monitored Area of Separation, occupying strategic peaks on Mount Hermon (Jabal al-Shaykh) and accelerating the construction of the "Sufa 53" military corridor. Spanning approximately 74 kilometers from the foothills of Mount Hermon near Beit Jinn southward to the Yarmouk Basin, the Sufa 53 project consists of an eight-meter-wide military transit road, observation berms measuring up to five meters, and deep anti-tank trenches designed to establish an unbreachable forward operational line.

For the transitional government in Damascus, the ongoing Israeli presence inside the buffer zone and adjacent rural communities represents an unacceptable territorial violation. Al-Sharaa and Foreign Minister al-Shaibani have drawn a strict institutional distinction between tactical military de-confliction—necessary to halt airstrikes and secure international reconstruction aid—and full diplomatic normalization. Syrian officials have repeatedly stated that establishing formal diplomatic relations or accepting Israeli claims over the Golan Heights is excluded from their mandate.

Conversely, Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz have maintained that the IDF must retain forward defense positions and active interdiction capabilities to prevent the emergence of security vacuums in southern Syria. Israeli defense officials argue that withdrawing from the high-ground posts on Mount Hermon or dismantling the Sufa 53 infrastructure would leave northern border communities vulnerable to non-state armed actors and emerging regional factions. Katz reaffirmed that Israel considers the Golan Heights an "integral, eternal part of Israel," ruling out any territorial concessions in exchange for non-aggression pledges from Damascus.

The diplomatic standoff was further highlighted by al-Sharaa’s description of a private conversation with U.S. President Donald Trump. Recounting the meeting in New York, al-Sharaa noted that when Trump raised the subject of U.S. recognition of Israeli sovereignty over the Golan, he jokingly suggested that Trump should "give New Jersey to the Israelis" instead, remarking that Trump had governance over New Jersey—a state "full of Democrats" who did not vote for him—whereas neither Washington nor Damascus had the authority to surrender sovereign Syrian territory.

The remark quickly circulated across digital platforms, becoming a central reference point in public analysis of the negotiations. On X and within specialized forums on Reddit such as r/syriancivilwar, defense analysts debated the sustainability of Damascus's position, observing that al-Sharaa is attempting to balance internal nationalist legitimacy against external pressure to sign a security pact. Concurrently, data on prediction market platforms such as Kalshi and Polymarket reflected the souring outlook: the traded probability of Israel and Syria establishing formal diplomatic normalization before January 1, 2027, fell to approximately 3.1%, down from double-digit projections earlier in the year.

The impasse carries direct strategic consequences for the Levant. Without a formalized security architecture, the Syrian-Lebanese border corridor remains volatile, complicating broader U.S. initiatives to stabilize regional trade corridors and rebuild Syrian administrative institutions. While the Joint Fusion Mechanism continues to operate as an emergency technical de-confliction channel, the broader ambition of converting tactical coordination into a formal regional accord remains constrained by the unresolved status of the Golan Heights and Israel’s forward military perimeter.
