# Steel, Sugar, and Sovereign Pride: Inside the US-Brazil Trade Clash Shaping the 2026 Election

On the eve of July 22, 2026, the offices of Brazil’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Palácio do Planalto in Brasília remained lit late into the night. At 12:01 a.m. Eastern Time, the United States government was scheduled to officially implement a 25% tariff surcharge on a broad range of Brazilian industrial exports under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974. The economic penalties target vital sectors—including ethanol, sugar, machinery, and manufacturing components—while exempting primary commodities like beef, coffee, and oil. The Confederação Nacional da Indústria (CNI) estimates that the tariffs threaten approximately US$ 11 billion in export value, which represents 26.2% of Brazil’s total exports to the United States. 

The implementation of the tariffs comes at a highly sensitive political moment, arriving just two days after the official launch of Brazil’s presidential campaign conventions on July 20. Incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, who is seeking a fourth term in the October 4, 2026 election, has immediately positioned the trade dispute at the center of his campaign. Condemning the tariffs as unilateral U.S. protectionism, Lula's administration has threatened to invoke Brazil's newly regulated Economic Reciprocity Law (Law No. 15.122/2025) to retaliate against U.S. imports.

Conversely, the conservative opposition, led by Senator Flávio Bolsonaro of the Liberal Party (PL), has utilized the trade shock as political ammunition against the government. The opposition argues that Lula’s domestic policies—notably the implementation of new digital taxes under the dual VAT reform, regulatory preferences for the national Pix electronic payment system over U.S. payment firms, and a failure to curb deforestation in the Amazon—directly alienated the administration of President Donald Trump, triggering the economic penalties.

The roots of the trade conflict lie in a shift in U.S. trade policy earlier in the year. On February 20, 2026, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in *Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump* that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) does not authorize the President to impose broad tariffs. Writing for the majority, Chief Justice John Roberts applied the major questions doctrine, holding that such sweeping economic authority requires explicit congressional approval. Following the loss of IEEPA as a tariff tool, the Trump administration shifted its focus to targeted Section 301 investigations. The investigation into Brazil, which began in July 2025, concluded with the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) determining that Brazil's trade and digital practices were "unreasonable" and burdened U.S. commerce.

The USTR's findings highlighted several specific friction points. Washington cited Brazil's domestic digital policies, including court-ordered suspensions of U.S. social media accounts and content moderation rules, as well as the dual VAT regulations established by Decree No. 12.955/2026 in April 2026, which impose tax compliance liabilities on foreign digital platforms. The USTR also targeted Brazil’s 18% tariff on U.S. ethanol, a policy long opposed by U.S. industry groups such as the Renewable Fuels Association (RFA) and Growth Energy. 

While the final USTR rule exempted over 1,600 product categories, including agricultural staples and civil aircraft parts, it left Brazil’s industrial sectors vulnerable. Steel and aluminum products were excluded from the new Section 301 duties to avoid double-taxation on categories already subject to Section 232 tariffs, but they remain constrained by those existing trade barriers.

The industrial response within Brazil has been swift. CNI has called for an emergency "seventh mission" under the government's "Nova Indústria Brasil" (NIB) policy to form a task force and coordinate support for affected manufacturing firms. Meanwhile, the Union of the Sugarcane and Bioenergy Industry (UNICA) defended Brazil's bioenergy policies, stating that the decline in U.S. ethanol imports to Brazil is a natural economic outcome of Brazil's expanding domestic corn-based ethanol production, rather than regulatory discrimination.

The trade dispute has quickly degenerated into personal and diplomatic hostility. U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio criticized the Brazilian administration on social media, accusing it of failing to negotiate in "good faith." Rubio stated that President Lula had "put his own ego ahead of making a deal for the welfare of the Brazilian people," adding that the tariffs were the "price for that." Lula, who has previously described Rubio as an "enemy of Latin America," rejected the statement, calling it an attempt to undermine Brazilian sovereignty and influence the upcoming election.

Behind the scenes, the political stakes are even more complex. Earlier in July, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro traveled to Washington to participate in trade hearings. Reports emerged that the PL standard-bearer quietly urged the Trump administration to delay the implementation of the tariffs until after the October election. Flávio reportedly warned that immediate tariffs would trigger a nationalist backlash that would benefit Lula by allowing him to project himself as a defender of national sovereignty. 

Lula's campaign has seized on these reports. In public addresses, Lula accused the Bolsonaro family of lobbying the U.S. government to harm the Brazilian economy for electoral advantage, stating that the opposition was willing to sacrifice domestic jobs for political gain. 

In response to the U.S. measures, Brazil’s Chamber of Foreign Trade (CAMEX) and the Interministerial Committee for Economic and Trade Negotiations and Countermeasures (CINCEC) have begun evaluating retaliation options under Law No. 15.122/2025. Regulated by Decree No. 12.551/2025, the Economic Reciprocity Law allows Brazil to impose proportional countermeasures against unilateral trade barriers. Brazilian officials have indicated that potential retaliatory actions include the suspension of intellectual property rights on U.S. pharmaceutical and agricultural seed patents, as well as regulatory restrictions on U.S. media and audiovisual companies operating in the country.

As the legal deadline for party nominations approaches on August 5, the trade conflict has polarized the electorate. Voters are faced with a choice between the opposition's argument that Lula's regulatory and environmental policies have isolated Brazil from its second-largest trading partner, and the incumbent administration's appeal to national sovereignty in the face of foreign economic pressure.
