# The 2028 Shadow Primary: Gavin Newsom’s Montana Truce, JD Vance’s Iowa Footprint, and the High-Stakes Midterm Undercurrents

Wading into a Montana river alongside CNN anchor Jake Tapper for a mid-September broadcast of the network's *Out of Office* series, California Governor Gavin Newsom delivered a calculated public declaration that fundamentally altered the prospective field for the 2028 presidential campaign. Asked whether he would seek the Democratic nomination if former Vice President Kamala Harris enters the contest, Newsom was unequivocal. "I wouldn't run if she ran," Newsom said, characterizing a primary battle between two prominent California Democrats as "mutual assured destruction." A direct clash between them, he noted, would be a "waste of everyone's time" and "a gift from God" to the Republican Party.

Newsom’s concession is shaped directly by the financial architecture of California politics. For more than two decades, Newsom and Harris have risen through municipal and statewide offices while drawing from an identical network of major donors. Their fundraising bases overlap across Northern California venture capital firms, San Francisco real estate interests, and Southern California entertainment executives. An open confrontation between the sitting governor and the former vice president would compel high-dollar donors to choose sides, consuming tens of millions of dollars within a single state prior to early nominating contests. By stepping aside conditionally, Newsom preserves his relationships with statewide financiers, prevents a bruising intra-party donor fight, and leaves the immediate political terrain open to Harris.

However, Newsom’s pledge has also created immediate openings for Democratic governors outside Washington and the West Coast. With California's governor committing to stand down should Harris run, swing-state executives are positioning themselves for national scrutiny. In Pennsylvania, Governor Josh Shapiro registers a 58% favorability rating in Quinnipiac University polling while holding an approval rating between 56% and 58% across statewide surveys. As he seeks re-election in November 2026 against Republican State Treasurer Stacy Garrity—maintaining double-digit leads in public polling—Shapiro has built his standing on fiscal management, infrastructure repair, and appeal to moderate suburban voters in the nation's premier battleground state.

In Kentucky, Governor Andy Beshear has similarly broadened his political reach through his federal political action committee, "In This Together," established in January 2024. Throughout 2026, the PAC has deployed advertising and financial support to competitive down-ballot races across multiple states, providing Beshear with national visibility. Having won two gubernatorial terms in a state that voted heavily for Donald Trump, Beshear emphasizes economic delivery and bipartisan tone over ideological confrontation. Beshear has indicated he will assess any prospective 2028 bid with his family following the 2026 midterms, presenting an explicit alternative for Democratic donors seeking candidates proven in competitive territory.

On the Republican side, Vice President JD Vance enters the final stretch of the midterm cycle with a decisive lead in early primary measurements. Tracking data from The Center Square Voters' Voice Poll and related Republican primary surveys conducted in August and September 2026 place Vance at 41% to 42% support among GOP primary voters. Other prospective contenders, such as Secretary of State Marco Rubio, poll in the mid-teens, reflecting Vance's consolidation of populist, rural, and working-class party factions.

On September 18, Vance traveled to the early-caucus state of Iowa, headlining a midterm campaign rally at the Mid-America Center in Council Bluffs. In his speech, Vance campaigned on behalf of Republican candidates facing competitive Senate and congressional races, centering his remarks on the Trump-Vance administration's record on tariffs, tax policy, border enforcement, and prescription drug prices. When questioned by reporters about 2028 ambitions, Vance sought to suppress premature speculation, stating that any personal political decisions would occur only after the November midterms conclude and following discussions with his wife, Usha. Even so, Republican strategists in Iowa noted that Vance's appearance served to reinforce relationships with key county chairs and regional activists ahead of the next cycle.

These developments unfold against an atmosphere sharpened by political theories circulating in Washington. On his *Politics War Room* podcast, veteran Democratic strategist James Carville promoted a scenario suggesting that should Democrats win control of Congress in November 2026, President Donald Trump could face intense oversight and investigative pressure. Carville hypothesized that Trump might choose to resign from office around Easter 2027, transferring the presidency to Vance in exchange for preemptive federal legal protections.

The White House quickly denounced Carville’s comments, with spokespersons dismissing him as a "stone-cold loser" afflicted with "Trump Derangement Syndrome." Seeking to project governing momentum, the administration accompanied its rhetorical rejection with executive actions on trade and energy, alongside a demanding nationwide travel schedule featuring both Trump and Vance in competitive congressional districts.

The converging developments have prompted active debate across digital platforms. In Democratic discussions on Reddit and X, contributors are divided between defending Harris’s status as the party’s titular standard-bearer and warning against repeating the dynamics of the 2024 election, with frequent calls to elevate Midwestern and swing-state governors like Shapiro and Beshear. In Republican channels, commentators dismiss Carville’s hypothesis as midterm partisan spin, turning their attention instead to whether Vance’s 42% polling grip can effectively translate Trump's populist movement into a governing majority in 2028. As the November 2026 vote approaches, the battle for control of Congress remains inseparable from the maneuvering for the next presidential nomination.
