# The 6% Depreciation Trap: How Big Tech’s $320 Billion AI Debt Binge Is Dismantling the Balance Sheet Premium

On September 11, 2026, Amazon.com Inc. finalized a £4.25 billion multi-tranche debt offering in London, pricing 2038 paper at 6.250% and 2045 bonds at 6.650%. The sterling sale capped a blistering summer in which Silicon Valley’s largest hyperscalers dismantled two decades of corporate finance doctrine. In July, Amazon priced a $21.5 billion domestic offering that included 10-year notes at 5.300% and 40-year paper at 6.250%. That came on the heels of Alphabet Inc.’s $25.0 billion bond deal on August 10—featuring $4.5 billion of 10-year debt yielding 5.450% and $2.5 billion of 40-year notes at 6.500%—and Meta Platforms Inc.’s identical $25.0 billion offering on May 1, where Mark Zuckerberg’s company locked in 10-year maturities at 5.250% and 40-year paper at 6.450%. Year-to-date corporate debt issuance across the top hyperscalers has surged past $160 billion, more than doubling the $76 billion issued across all of 2024 and pushing the collective long-term debt burden of Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, and Meta to $202.01 billion.

The capital stampede addresses an existential technological imperative, but it has introduced a violent structural paradox to the credit markets. Hyperscalers are taking down 10-, 30-, and 40-year paper yielding north of 5.2% to 6.6% to purchase computational silicon that carries a recognized accounting useful life of just three to five years. In Microsoft Corp.’s Form 10-K for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2026, capital expenditures reached a record $115.95 billion—up 79.6% from $64.55 billion in fiscal 2025 and nearly triple the $44.48 billion spent in fiscal 2024. Alphabet’s annual capex reached $91.45 billion in 2025, while Meta’s capital outlays surged to $69.69 billion. Together with Nvidia-anchored off-balance-sheet special purpose vehicles, total AI debt issuance in 2026 has crossed $320 billion, according to Michael Cembalest, Chairman of Market and Investment Strategy at JPMorgan Chase & Co. In 10-year equivalent terms, Cembalest calculates the long-duration tech debt pipeline at $303 billion—absorbing roughly 68% of all net new long-duration U.S. Treasury borrowing scheduled for this calendar year.

This unprecedented call on the credit markets has set off fierce debate across institutional trading desks and retail investor forums alike. On Reddit’s r/investing, where a viral thread titled "The $141 Billion Debt Trap: Why Big Tech's AI Buildout is the New Credit Risk in Your Portfolio" sparked intense debate, retail allocators are actively questioning whether Big Tech’s legendary "balance sheet premium" has vanished. "The specific problem with Big Tech's debt is that it's a new credit risk on historically pristine balance sheets," wrote one widely upvoted commenter on r/investing. "The shift to debt financing for AI CapEx now aligns tech risk with the median investment-grade non-financial company. Historically pristine Big Tech balance sheets are now 'approaching normalcy' in terms of liquidity. If the RoIC on these huge AI CapEx projects fails, it poses a direct 'tech debt spiral' risk."

The core controversy boils down to a stark mathematical mismatch between asset duration and liability duration. In previous tech expansion cycles, software code and server chassis were funded out of cash balances that yielded near 0%, while depreciable server assets enjoyed life extensions from three years to six years. Today, high-bandwidth memory (HBM3e/HBM4) and accelerator architectures like Nvidia’s Blackwell and Rubin platforms face physical degradation and technological obsolescence within 36 to 48 months. Over on r/stocks, an exchange on the looming depreciation tsunami laid bare the operational hazard: "When deprecation hits, GAAP income goes down, but if the company is prudent with CapEx, the revenue efficiencies realized by the CapEx should be greater and maintain margin," argued one participant. Yet an opposing allocator replied: "Cutting CapEx to pay off the debt is the solution to solvency, but it creates a competitive death sentence. The risk is not bankruptcy; it is the loss of the AI arms race. If they cut the CapEx because the RoIC fails, they immediately fall behind competitors who are still scaling."

The hurdle rates demanded by this borrowing spree are rising in lockstep with the broader rates market. With the benchmark 10-year U.S. Treasury yield (^TNX) closing at 4.975% on September 11, 2026—testing the psychologically pivotal 5.00% threshold—the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for the hyperscaler complex has expanded from 6.8% in early 2022 to approximately 9.2% today. Historically, Big Tech commanded a return on invested capital (ROIC) comfortably between 22% and 34%, insulated by asset-light software margins and monopoly ad models. To justify borrowing at 5.5% to 6.5% on incremental capital expenditures that exceed $350 billion across the sector, analysts estimate that hyperscalers must generate at least 13.5% to 15.0% in post-tax cash ROIC on their AI hardware portfolios. If server depreciation consumes $18 billion to $25 billion annually per company—Meta’s annual depreciation and amortization charges have already climbed from $11.18 billion in 2023 to $18.62 billion, while Amazon's D&A jumped to $65.76 billion—net operating profit after tax (NOPAT) margins will compress sharply unless AI software monetization accelerates by 40% year-over-year.

The market tension is most acute where free cash flow coverage has inverted. While Microsoft and Alphabet generated over $130 billion in combined operating cash flow over the trailing four quarters, Oracle Corp. presents the credit market’s immediate fault line. Oracle’s fiscal 2026 capex hit $55.7 billion against operating cash flow of roughly $32 billion, leaving the enterprise software giant projected by Wall Street analysts to run negative free cash flow through 2029. In January 2026, a regional pension fund sued Oracle over debt disclosures tied to its multi-gigawatt data center expansion, and its equity has slid 17.91% year-to-date. "Talking about debt without talking about their cash, income, and/or free cash flow is pretty dumb," argued an r/investing defender of the mega-caps, pointing out that Alphabet and Microsoft could technically pay down their annual debt obligations by pausing capital investments for a single fiscal quarter. But as Goldman Sachs credit trader Jeffrey Papai cautioned in a client scenario warning of $340 billion in hyperscaler and chip debt supply in 2027, there is virtually no natural capital recycling from debt maturities in the tech sector, meaning every new cluster requires fresh institutional allocations.

Meanwhile, Washington’s macroeconomic landscape offers little shelter. In the executive branch, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has struggled to break what the Financial Times described on September 11 as a persistent "fever" in the long-term bond market, as federal deficit financing collides directly with corporate supply. At the Federal Reserve, where Kevin Warsh assumed the chairmanship earlier this year, sticky inflation prints above 3.0% have foreclosed any near-term return to monetary easing. With investment-grade corporate bond spreads tightening to historically narrow bands of 85 to 105 basis points over Treasuries, bondholders are accepting minimal risk compensation to finance hardware that will be scrap metal by the time the first principal payments come due.
