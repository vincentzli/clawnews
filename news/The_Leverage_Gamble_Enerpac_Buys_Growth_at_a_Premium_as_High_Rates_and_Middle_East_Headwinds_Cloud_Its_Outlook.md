# The Leverage Gamble: Enerpac Buys Growth at a Premium as High Rates and Middle East Headwinds Cloud Its Outlook

On the morning of July 8, 2026, Paul Sternlieb, President and Chief Executive Officer of Enerpac Tool Group Corp. (NYSE: EPAC), faced analysts on the company’s fiscal third-quarter conference call with what should have been a triumphant hand. The Menomonee Falls, Wisconsin-based industrial toolmaker had just posted a clean double-beat for the quarter ending May 31, 2026. Adjusted diluted earnings per share came in at $0.60, handily beating the Wall Street consensus of $0.50. Net sales for the quarter reached $167.6 million, surpassing the expected $165.6 million (and reported as a rounded $168 million in major financial outlets), posting a 6% increase year-over-year. Yet, the market’s response was swift and unsympathetic: shares of Enerpac fell 2.6% following the announcement on July 7, closing at $34.31, and slid further to close at $34.03 on the day of the call.

The immediate sell-off exposes a deepening rift between management’s long-term consolidator ambitions and a market increasingly hostile to leveraged expansion in a high-interest-rate regime. Under the hawkish leadership of Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, who has held the federal funds rate at 3.50% to 3.75% while keeping further rate hikes on the table, investors are placing a premium on pristine balance sheets and cash returns. Concurrently with its earnings print, Enerpac announced a definitive agreement to acquire Specialized Fabrication Equipment Group LLC (SFE Group) from Gladstone Investment Corporation (NASDAQ: GAIN) for approximately $472 million in cash. The transaction, which represents Gladstone’s 31st realized exit, marks a massive capital structure pivot for Enerpac—one that has left the market weighing the promise of long-term synergies against the immediate weight of debt.

Prior to the SFE announcement, Enerpac’s balance sheet was remarkably clean. The company closed the third quarter with $115.7 million in cash and cash equivalents against $184.8 million in total debt, leaving net debt at just $69.1 million. This represented a conservative net leverage ratio of approximately 0.5x adjusted EBITDA. To finance the $472 million cash acquisition, Enerpac amended its senior revolving credit facility, expanding its borrowing capacity from $400 million to $625 million. Upon closing the transaction, which is scheduled for the first quarter of fiscal 2027, Enerpac’s net debt-to-adjusted EBITDA ratio is projected to jump to 2.8x. 

This leverage spike pushes Enerpac well above its long-term target leverage range of 1.5x to 2.5x. CFO Darren Kozik assured investors that the company plans to deleverage back to approximately 2.2x within 12 months, supported by strong cash flow. Enerpac maintained its full-year free cash flow guidance of $100 million to $110 million. However, the math of carrying this debt in a high SOFR environment has triggered alarm bells. With the effective federal funds rate at 3.63% and credit facility spreads typically adding 1.5% to 2.0%, Enerpac's interest rate on new borrowings will likely hover around 5.5%. Assuming the company borrows roughly $370 million under the facility after utilizing cash on hand, its annual interest expense is poised to rise by approximately $20 million. 

This added debt service directly threatens Enerpac’s capital allocation priorities, specifically its $200 million share repurchase program authorized in October 2025. If all available free cash flow is directed toward aggressive deleveraging over the next 12 months, buybacks will inevitably be put on ice. This capital allocation shift was a major theme on financial forums like Stocktwits and Reddit. Under the ticker symbol $EPAC, retail traders debated whether the deal was a value-destroying shift. Some argued that management was choosing to buy growth at a 10.6x EBITDA multiple rather than buying back its own cheaper shares.

The strategic rationale for SFE Group, led by its CEO Vinay Varma who will transition to President of SFE under Enerpac, is clear on paper. SFE is a high-margin business specializing in portable machining, welding, and fabrication tools, operating brands like Climax, B&B Sumner, and Axxair. It generated $170 million in sales and $44 million in adjusted EBITDA over the trailing twelve months, reflecting a robust 25.9% EBITDA margin. The purchase price of $472 million represents a multiple of 10.6x trailing EBITDA, which drops to 9.5x when factoring in management’s projected synergies of approximately $5.7 million. 

Sternlieb argued that SFE expands Enerpac’s total addressable market by $1 billion, providing crucial exposure to high-barrier sectors like nuclear power, defense, and semiconductor fabrication. Yet, analysts are questioning the timing of this massive outlay. Enerpac's core business is already showing signs of operational friction. While product sales in its Industrial Tool & Service (IT&S) segment grew by 5% organically, service revenue collapsed by 8% in the quarter. Management blamed near-term service pressures and geopolitical disruptions, particularly the conflict in the Middle East.

This operational soft spot forced Enerpac to narrow and effectively lower its full-year fiscal 2026 guidance. Net sales guidance was trimmed to $635 million–$645 million from the previous $635 million–$650 million. Organic growth was narrowed to 1%–2% from 1%–3%. Most critically, adjusted EBITDA guidance was cut to $151 million–$156 million from the prior $158 million–$163 million, and adjusted diluted EPS was lowered to $1.84–$1.89 from $1.85–$1.92. 

This guidance cut has cast a shadow over the SFE acquisition. Ross Sparenblek, analyst at William Blair, who maintains a "Market Perform" rating on the stock, has historically voiced caution regarding Enerpac’s growth trajectory in a cooling industrial market. Conversely, bulls like Daniel Moore of CJS Securities, who holds an "Outperform" rating and a $53 price target, view SFE as a transformative portfolio addition that will drive double-digit accretion in fiscal 2027. Tom Hayes of Roth Capital Partners, with a "Buy" rating and a $51 price target, similarly supports the consolidation strategy.

The tension lies in whether Enerpac can successfully digest SFE and deleverage before a broader macroeconomic slowdown takes hold. With Scott Bessent at the helm of the Treasury and the Trump-Vance administration pushing tariffs that could complicate global supply chains, the industrial sector faces heightened operational risks. By shifting from a net-cash position to a leveraged debt profile of 2.8x EBITDA, Enerpac has exhausted its financial safety net. If Middle East disruptions persist and service revenues continue to slide, the company may find itself trapped with high-cost debt and a halted buyback program. The Q3 double-beat proved that Enerpac's tools remain in demand, but the market's negative reaction shows that in 2026, cash flow preservation is valued far more than debt-fueled expansion.
