# The Pentagon’s Orinoco Gamble: Inside the Secret 100-Year Concession That Turned the Defense Department into an Oil Baron

Nine months after United States Delta Force operators breached a Miraflores compound during Operation Absolute Resolve and placed Nicolás Maduro in federal custody, Washington’s intervention in Venezuela has pivoted from tactical warfare to financial engineering. Rather than dismantling the ruling infrastructure in Caracas, the administration of President Donald Trump opted for a transactional accommodation, negotiating directly with Acting President Delcy Rodríguez and Interior Minister Diosdado Cabello. The cornerstone of this detente is the North American Blue Energy Partners (NABEP) accord—a sweeping energy pact that grants a private consortium exclusive 100-year development rights across 17 strategic oil blocks in the Orinoco Belt, an expanse holding an estimated 65 billion barrels of extra-heavy crude, representing roughly 20 percent of Venezuela’s proven reserves.

The geopolitical arrangement blew open on Capitol Hill on October 1, 2026, when congressional committees uncovered that the Department of Defense’s Office of Strategic Capital (OSC) holds an unprecedented 35 percent equity stake in NABEP’s corporate parent. Under the covenants of the agreement, the U.S. State Department secured a preferential right to purchase 20 percent of total production at production cost—earmarked to replenish the depleted U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve—while retaining a right of first refusal over the remaining 80 percent. The pact further grants Washington veto authority over executive board appointments, mandating that a majority of directors be U.S. citizens.

The disclosure triggered an immediate constitutional confrontation. Four ranking Senate Democrats—Jeanne Shaheen of New Hampshire, Jack Reed of Rhode Island, Martin Heinrich of New Mexico, and Elizabeth Warren of Massachusetts—dispatched an urgent inquiry to Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright, demanding the immediate suspension of the equity transfer and full disclosure of the contract. In their formal letter, the senators underscored that the statute governing the OSC, codified under 10 U.S.C. § 149 in the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2024, explicitly restricts the office to debt instruments—loans, loan guarantees, and technical assistance—for critical technology sectors. It provides no statutory authority permitting the Pentagon to acquire equity stakes in private commercial corporations, let alone foreign hydrocarbon ventures.

"The proposed agreement is unlikely to reduce high energy costs for Americans, and it risks undermining the Venezuelan people's transition away from a dictatorship," the senators wrote, warning that utilizing military intervention to capture natural resources bypasses the War Powers Resolution and establishes an unauthorized precedent of defense-led commercial asset acquisition.

At the center of this state-backed partnership stands Alejandro Betancourt López, the Venezuelan executive directing NABEP. Betancourt first gained commercial prominence during the administration of Hugo Chávez as co-founder of Derwick Associates, which secured more than a dozen state contracts during Venezuela’s 2009–2010 electrical crisis. While Betancourt has been the subject of international investigations in Switzerland, Spain, and the Southern District of Florida concerning allegations of money laundering and embezzlement connected to Petróleos de Venezuela, S.A. (PDVSA), he has never been indicted or formally charged in the United States and has repeatedly denied any financial impropriety.

The selection of NABEP has provoked intense friction across the international energy sector. Established operators, including Chevron—which has operated under Treasury Department authorizations such as General License 41—alongside ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips, were excluded from any competitive bidding process. Concurrently, the administration applied sustained regulatory pressure on independent Florida oil executive Harry Sargeant III to divest competing Venezuelan joint-venture interests, clearing the landscape for NABEP's dominant footprint across the Junín and Carabobo quadrants.

Simultaneously, the security architecture surrounding the Orinoco deposits has catalyzed fierce controversy. Reports indicated that private military contractors, including networks associated with former Blackwater founder Erik Prince, had submitted detailed proposals to establish armed security perimeters around eastern production clusters and coastal refining facilities. Prince, who conducted backchannel discussions with Delcy Rodríguez as early as 2019, has long advocated for contractor-led protective details in unstable extraction regions. However, the prospect of private American security firms guarding Venezuelan state infrastructure triggered diplomatic unease. In late September, Energy Secretary Chris Wright moved to quell the backlash, stating publicly that the administration has "no plans to directly provide security to oil producers in Venezuela" and will not deploy U.S. forces or private mercenaries, asserting that commercial operators must bear their own site security while Washington exercises leverage through the control of international financial flows.

Across digital policy forums and financial discussion boards, the agreement has unleashed widespread skepticism. On r/politics, community reactions characterized the Pentagon’s commercial equity arrangement as a "resource grab" engineered to enrich a circle of "cronies" rather than the American or Venezuelan public, comparing the Pentagon's role to a sovereign investment fund supported by military leverage. Concurrently, on r/wallstreetbets, market participants analyzed the $100 billion capital expenditure pledge announced by the White House, dismissing targets of ramping production past 1 million barrels per day as an unviable "scam" or a political maneuver rather than a sound economic investment, citing the physical collapse of the Venezuelan basin.

That engineering reality represents the most acute structural obstacle to Washington's objectives. The Orinoco Belt’s extra-heavy bitumen averages an API gravity between 8 and 10 degrees—a dense, tar-like consistency requiring heavy dilution with light naphtha or industrial processing into synthetic crude before it can traverse export pipelines to the Jose terminal in Anzoátegui. Decades of deferred maintenance have left three of the nation's key upgraders—Petromonagas, Petrosinovensa, and Petropiar—operating below 40 percent capacity, hindered by damaged hydrotreater units and depleted coker drums. Furthermore, the national electrical grid remains unstable; cascading outages from the Guri Hydroelectric Dam frequently disrupt electric heat tracing along the 140-kilometer main export pipelines, causing viscous crude to congeal within the transmission network.

Compounding these mechanical vulnerabilities is a direct statutory conflict within Venezuelan domestic law. While the White House announced the transaction as a 100-year concession, Article 24 of Venezuela’s Organic Hydrocarbons Law limits joint exploration and production licenses to a maximum term of 25 years, allowing an extension of up to 15 years solely upon formal approval by the National Assembly. By entering into agreements with Rodríguez and Cabello without legislative ratification, the executive branch has committed public resources and defense prestige to a contractual structure that remains vulnerable to judicial invalidation by any future Venezuelan governing authority.
