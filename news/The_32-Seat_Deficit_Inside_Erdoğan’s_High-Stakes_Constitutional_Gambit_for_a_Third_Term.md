# The 32-Seat Deficit: Inside Erdoğan’s High-Stakes Constitutional Gambit for a Third Term

In Ankara’s marble-clad presidential complex, political survival has been reduced to an unyielding mathematical formula: 360 votes out of 600. Under Article 101 of the Turkish Constitution, President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan is barred from seeking another term when his current five-year mandate expires in May 2028. Yet in mid-September 2026, the presidential palace outlined its legal strategy to overcome that limit. Mehmet Uçum, chief presidential legal adviser and deputy chairman of the Presidential Legal Policies Board, publicly floated a specific date for Turkey’s next general and presidential elections: April 16, 2028.

The date is laden with symbolism—the 11th anniversary of the April 16, 2017 referendum that transitioned Turkey from a parliamentary system to an executive presidency—but the legal mechanism underpinning it is meticulously calculated. By pulling the election forward by barely four weeks, the ruling Justice and Development Party (AKP) seeks to activate the exception codified in Article 116, Paragraph 3 of the Turkish Constitution. The clause provides that if the Grand National Assembly of Turkey (TBMM) votes to renew elections during an incumbent president's second term, the president is permitted to run for office one final time.

The constitutional structure, however, creates an unavoidable bottleneck. Under Article 116, Paragraph 2, if Erdoğan were to dissolve parliament unilaterally by presidential decree, he would be legally ineligible to run; the decision to renew elections must originate from the legislature. Under Paragraph 1, that motion requires a three-fifths supermajority of 360 deputies. The governing People's Alliance currently controls 328 seats—anchored by the AKP’s 267 seats, the far-right Nationalist Movement Party’s (MHP) 50 seats, and 11 seats held by micro-allies including the Free Cause Party (HÜDA PAR) and the Democratic Left Party (DSP). That leaves the administration with a 32-vote shortfall. Without peeling off at least 32 opposition lawmakers or securing a formal agreement with an adversarial bloc, Erdoğan cannot legally appear on the ballot.

This arithmetic explains the geopolitical and domestic realignments that have dominated Ankara over the past two years. In October 2024, MHP leader Devlet Bahçeli startled the political establishment by proposing that Abdullah Öcalan, the imprisoned leader of the outlawed Kurdistan Workers’ Party (PKK), address the pro-Kurdish DEM Party’s parliamentary faction to declare the armed group’s complete dissolution in exchange for the "right to hope"—a legal pathway granting potential parole for convicts serving aggravated life sentences. Erdoğan endorsed Bahçeli’s proposal, characterizing it as a "historic window of opportunity."

Behind the rhetoric of reconciliation sat the balance of power in the TBMM. The DEM Party holds 57 seats. If the pro-Kurdish faction votes in favor of renewing elections, the People’s Alliance’s tally would rise to 385 votes, comfortably clearing the 360-seat supermajority without requiring support from other parties. Yet this outreach has coincided with aggressive administrative pressure. Even as dialogue channels flickered, the Interior Ministry continued its practice of appointing state trustees (*kayyum*) to replace elected DEM Party mayors across southeastern municipalities including Mardin, Batman, and Halfeti, alongside CHP Mayor Ahmet Özer in Istanbul’s Esenyurt district, stripping them of office over alleged terrorism links.

The main opposition Republican People’s Party (CHP), which secured a landmark victory in the March 2024 local elections by winning 37.8% of the national vote and defending the municipal leadership of both Istanbul and Ankara, has rejected the early election formula. Speaking to journalists in Bursa following Uçum’s September 2026 statements, CHP Chairman Özgür Özel dismissed the proposed April 2028 timetable. "They cannot deceive the nation by saying, 'We brought elections forward, this is called an early election' one month before the actual date," Özel said. "We told Erdoğan: if you want to run, let's hold an early election at the 2.5-year mark, right in the middle of the term. They ran away. Now they shouldn't pursue other formulas or make calculations. They cannot escape from this election, from this ballot box."

The debate has ignited fierce commentary across Turkish media and social platforms. On X, legal scholars and political scientists, including Berk Esen, observed that moving the vote up by thirty days represents an explicit exploitation of constitutional text to circumvent term limits. Prominent Turkish commentators on X noted that scheduling an election in April instead of May does not constitute a genuine snap election, but rather an administrative tactic designed to re-qualify an incumbent who has reached his constitutional limit. In opposition circles, the legislative mathematics has been branded "the 32-seat auction," amid concerns that the government will use judicial pressure and state patronage to lure individual deputies from smaller conservative parties like DEVA, the Future Party (Gelecek), Felicity (Saadet), and the fractured nationalist İYİ Party.

At the same time, judicial pressure continues to loom over Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, widely viewed as the opposition's strongest presidential contender. İmamoğlu faces an ongoing appeal against a politically disqualifying prison sentence stemming from a 2019 public insult case, as well as separate administrative investigations. Polling data shows İmamoğlu and Ankara Mayor Mansur Yavaş retaining competitive leads over Erdoğan, driven by voter discontent over stubborn double-digit inflation and the austerity measures managed by Treasury and Finance Minister Mehmet Şimşek, which have eroded household purchasing power.

For Erdoğan, the 32-vote gap represents the most formidable legislative barrier of his two-decade tenure. If opposition parties hold their ranks and the DEM Party refuses to trade its 57 votes amid ongoing trustee crackdowns, the palace will have exhausted its constitutional options. Ankara remains locked in a high-stakes standoff: either the ruling coalition must secure 32 votes from across the aisle, or Turkey's long-serving president will face an exit dictated by the constitutional rules established during his own rule.
