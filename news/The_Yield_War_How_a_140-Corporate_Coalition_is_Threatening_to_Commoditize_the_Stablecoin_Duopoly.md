# The Yield War: How a 140-Corporate Coalition is Threatening to Commoditize the Stablecoin Duopoly

On June 30, 2026, as the European Union’s Markets in Crypto-Assets (MiCA) transitional window closed, a financial shift began to reshape the digital asset ecosystem. The Open Standard consortium, an alliance comprising over 140 corporate and financial entities, unveiled Open USD (OUSD). Led by Zach Abrams—the Bridge co-founder whose company was acquired by Stripe in 2025 for $1.1 billion—Open Standard’s premise is built on shared economics: a dollar-pegged stablecoin that distributes its net reserve yields directly back to its distribution partners.

The announcement impacted public markets. Shares of Circle Internet Group (NYSE: CRCL), which completed its initial public offering in June 2025, dropped 17% in the days immediately following the release. The market reacted to the structural challenge OUSD poses to the corporate stablecoin model, which has historically generated significant interest income for single issuers.

Currently, the stablecoin market is dominated by Tether (USDT) at $184 billion in market capitalization and Circle (USDC) at $73 billion. While Tether leads in circulating supply, USDC has captured a significant portion of the institutional settlement market, accounting for roughly 67% of the adjusted settlement volume—a metric that filters out non-economic transactions and automated exchange volume. It is this high-velocity institutional tier that OUSD is designed to target.

Under the traditional model, stablecoin issuers invest their reserves—primarily short-term U.S. Treasuries—and retain the interest yield, which has hovered around 5% over the past two years. In contrast, OUSD operates on a pass-through economic model. After deducting a management fee to cover operational costs, Open Standard plans to distribute the interest generated by its Treasury reserves back to the corporations, exchanges, and payment processors that distribute the stablecoin to end-users.

This model directly pressures the relationship between Circle and its most critical distributor, Coinbase. In August 2023, Coinbase and Circle signed a three-year revenue-sharing agreement under which Coinbase receives 100% of the interest income generated from USDC held on its platform and 50% of the interest from USDC held elsewhere. This deal has been a major revenue driver for Coinbase, which received nearly $908 million in revenue-sharing fees from Circle in 2024, and approximately $1.4 billion in 2025.

With that agreement set to reach a critical milestone on August 18, 2026, Coinbase’s decision to join the Open Standard consortium as a founding partner has introduced a new dynamic to the upcoming renegotiations. Analysts suggest Coinbase is using its alliance with OUSD as leverage to demand a higher yield split from Circle. If Circle refuses to adjust the terms, Coinbase could theoretically transition its user base toward OUSD, a move that would impact Circle’s market share and pressure its valuation. Circle's stock has experienced a long-term correction from its post-IPO peak of $299 in late June 2025, trading in a range of $60 to $66 in mid-July 2026 due to stalled USDC circulation growth and broader competitive pressures.

Circle’s chief executive, Jeremy Allaire, has defended his company's position. In public statements, Allaire argued that stablecoins are network-effect businesses built over years of integration, developer adoption, and regulatory licensing, rather than "corporate logo portfolios." He warned that Open Standard's free mint-and-burn model and yield pass-through structure risks "starving the infrastructure" of the capital needed to maintain robust security, compliance, and liquidity. Furthermore, Allaire pointed out that consortium-led decision-making faces inherent latency, making it poorly suited to navigate the fast-moving digital asset landscape.

This governance model faced immediate scrutiny during the OUSD launch. Open Standard's initial announcement listed several South Korean financial and technology giants as consortium partners, including Samsung Electronics, Shinhan Financial Group, and Dunamu, the operator of the Upbit cryptocurrency exchange. Within days, these companies publicly distanced themselves from the project. Samsung Electronics stated that no formal consultations had taken place and they were unaware of their inclusion. Dunamu and Shinhan Financial Group clarified that while they had been approached to gauge their interest, they had only agreed to review the proposal and had never formally signed on. The controversy exposed coordination gaps in the consortium's onboarding process and triggered a brief credibility crisis.

The battle is playing out against a shifting regulatory backdrop. In the EU, MiCA’s full enforcement has forced stablecoin issuers to meet strict reserve requirements and bankruptcy-remote standards. In the United States, the Guiding and Establishing National Innovation for U.S. Stablecoins (GENIUS) Act was signed into law by President Donald Trump on July 18, 2025. The GENIUS Act established the first federal regulatory framework for payment stablecoins, directing agencies like the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency (OCC) to finalize implementing rules within a year. However, federal regulators missed the July 18, 2026, deadline to finalize these rules, leaving the industry in a state of regulatory uncertainty as they prepare for the law's effective date in early 2027.

Despite the regulatory and operational hurdles, global payment giants are moving ahead with native integration. On July 16, 2026, Visa announced the launch of the Visa Stablecoin Platform (VSP), an enterprise-grade solution that allows financial institutions to mint, burn, and custody stablecoins. In its initial beta phase, VSP exclusively supports OUSD. Visa’s move is a clear indication that traditional payment processors are seeking to capture transaction volume directly on public blockchains, bypassing the traditional merchant fee rails that have historically defined their business models.

As the August 18 deadline for the Coinbase-Circle contract renewal approaches, the stablecoin industry is entering a defining transition. The outcome of these negotiations will determine whether the stablecoin market remains a consolidated, high-margin duopoly, or transforms into a low-margin, commoditized utility dominated by a consortium of global corporate giants.
