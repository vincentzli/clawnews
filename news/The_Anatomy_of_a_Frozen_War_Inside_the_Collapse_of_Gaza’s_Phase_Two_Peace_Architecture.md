# The Anatomy of a Frozen War: Inside the Collapse of Gaza’s Phase Two Peace Architecture

JERUSALEM and WASHINGTON — One year after the October 9, 2025 ceasefire agreement paused full-scale combat in the Gaza Strip, the diplomatic architecture heralded as the blueprint for long-term stability has degenerated into an entrenched, low-intensity war of attrition. The milestone, coinciding with the third anniversary of the October 7, 2023 attacks, has laid bare a profound structural failure: while the initial U.S.-brokered pact successfully facilitated the release of the remaining living Israeli hostages in exchange for Palestinian prisoners in late 2025, efforts to transition into Phase Two have collapsed into an unyielding geopolitical stalemate.

On paper, the governance and recovery of the enclave was delegated to the Board of Peace, an international body chaired by U.S. President Donald Trump and endorsed under UN Security Council Resolution 2803. In practice, the transition framework unveiled in January 2026 by U.S. Special Envoy to the Middle East Steve Witkoff and Board Director-General Nickolay Mladenov—designed to transfer civil administration to the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG) under Dr. Ali Sha'ath, deploy an International Stabilization Force (ISF), and begin a $2.45 billion reconstruction effort—remains paralyzed. On the ground, the nominal truce has offered little reprieve to the 1.9 million displaced Gazans sheltering in squalid tent encampments across Al-Mawasi and central Gaza. Over the past twelve months, more than 1,400 Palestinians have been killed in localized Israeli airstrikes, precision artillery strikes, and buffer-zone clashes, exposing the friction beneath the nominal truce.

At the epicenter of the deadlock is an irreconcilable dispute over sequencing. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s administration has insisted on a rigid precondition: the verified, total disarmament of Hamas, including the relinquishment of all light and heavy weaponry and the documented demolition of its subterranean tunnel infrastructure, before a single Israeli Defense Forces (IDF) soldier withdraws.

Netanyahu’s maneuvering is heavily constrained by internal coalition arithmetic ahead of the October 27, 2026 parliamentary elections. The Israeli premier is tightly hemmed in by far-right coalition partners whose votes sustain his parliamentary majority. National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich have repeatedly threatened to bring down the ruling coalition if the military surrenders its operational hold over Gaza. Smotrich and Ben-Gvir have publicly branded any phased disarmament model as an unacceptable compromise, conditioning their ongoing political backing on an offensive military doctrine and the preservation of Israeli military corridors.

The military reality on the ground reflects this domestic pressure. The IDF continues to fortify two strategic axes: the Philadelphi Corridor along the Egypt-Gaza border and the Netzarim Corridor dividing northern Gaza from the south. Military engineers have broadened security perimeters along Gaza’s perimeter to a depth of up to one kilometer, effectively placing roughly 17 percent of the territory inside permanent Israeli military control. Israeli defense officials maintain that holding the Philadelphi Corridor is vital to prevent weapons smuggling from the Sinai, while the Netzarim axis prevents armed factions from re-establishing administrative dominance in Gaza City.

For Hamas, relinquishing arms under these conditions is viewed as organizational suicide. Operating from subterranean redoubts and coordinated through its political bureau abroad, the group has categorically refused to decommission its arsenal without an immediate, unconditional IDF withdrawal to the pre-October 2023 lines, full Palestinian sovereign oversight of the Rafah crossing, and legally binding international guarantees for reconstruction. In closed-door negotiations in Cairo and Doha, Hamas interlocutors have argued that surrendering weapons while Israeli forces bisect the Strip leaves its remaining personnel exposed to targeted assassinations.

This diplomatic deadlock has throttled Gaza's physical reconstruction. Although the Board of Peace’s Founding Executive Board—which includes Secretary of State Marco Rubio, special envoy Steve Witkoff, Jared Kushner, and former British Prime Minister Tony Blair—unveiled a multi-billion-dollar revitalization plan in September 2026, virtually no raw building materials have crossed into the enclave. Citing the danger of dual-use diversion, Israel’s Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories (COGAT) continues to enforce an embargo on Portland cement, structural steel rebar, and heavy excavation machinery. An estimated 42 million metric tons of rubble remain strewn across municipal centers, contaminated by unexploded ordnance and human remains.

The resulting humanitarian deterioration prompted a sharp rebuke from United Nations Secretary-General António Guterres on October 6, 2026. Marking the solemn three-year anniversary of the conflict's outbreak, Guterres emphasized that the status quo is untenable, declaring that "it is time for the ceasefire to become permanent" and reiterating that humanitarian relief "is non-negotiable." Guterres warned that without advancing to Phase Two under UNSC Resolution 2803, the enclave faces systemic institutional collapse.

The diplomatic impasse has ignited fierce debate across social media and policy forums. On X, security analysts and regional monitors have increasingly characterized the 2025 agreement as a containment device rather than a peace treaty. As one prominent foreign policy analyst posted on X, "Phase Two was never designed to resolve the core contradiction; it simply outsourced an indefinite military occupation under the branding of an international board." On Reddit’s r/politics, discussions analyzing the anniversary highlighted the humanitarian cost of the political standoff, with one widely upvoted comment noting: "A ceasefire where 1,400 people die in a year and two million live in rubble isn't a peace deal—it's a frozen siege."

As Israel prepares to go to the polls on October 27 and regional mediators struggle to convene high-level delegations, the diplomatic architecture of October 2025 faces its definitive reckoning. With neither Jerusalem nor Hamas willing to yield on the fundamental question of arms and sovereignty, the Board of Peace's Phase Two blueprint risks being remembered not as a bridge to self-determination, but as the administrative veneer of an unending conflict.
