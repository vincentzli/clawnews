# The Post-New START Era: Expiration of Nuclear Caps Unleashes Unconstrained Three-Power Arms Race

On February 5, 2026, the global nuclear nonproliferation architecture crossed an unprecedented historical threshold. With the formal expiration of the New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START), the final legally binding agreement limiting the strategic arsenals of the United States and the Russian Federation ceased to operate. For the first time in nearly 54 years—dating back to the signing of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) Interim Agreement on May 26, 1972—the world’s two largest nuclear powers are functioning without verified numerical limits, mutual data notifications, or mandatory on-site inspections.

The lapse follows a protracted diplomatic breakdown that culminated in late 2025. In September 2025, Russian President Vladimir Putin proposed an informal, one-year political pledge to adhere to New START’s core ceilings: 1,550 deployed strategic warheads, 700 deployed intercontinental delivery systems, and 800 deployed and non-deployed launchers. However, the Russian initiative explicitly refused to reinstate on-site verification activities, which Moscow suspended in February 2023. The White House, under President Donald Trump, declined the proposal, stating that Washington would not accept unmonitored limits while Russia expands theater nuclear forces and China accelerates its strategic buildup. Administration officials affirmed that any future pact must incorporate rigorous verification, encompass all nuclear warhead classes, and include Beijing as a full negotiating party.

The termination of New START eliminates the treaty's verification regime, which had conducted 328 on-site inspections and exchanged more than 25,000 notifications over its 15-year lifespan. Hans Kristensen, director of the Nuclear Information Project at the Federation of American Scientists (FAS), noted the structural shift: "The era of negotiated bilateral arms reductions has ended. We are transitioning into a multi-adversary nuclear environment where absence of transparency forces defense planners to base force structures on worst-case capacity projections."

In Washington, strategic debate has immediately centered on "upload capacity"—the speed with which existing platforms can be reloaded with stored reserve warheads. Under New START compliance measures, the U.S. Navy deactivated four launch tubes on each of its fourteen Ohio-class ballistic missile submarines (SSBNs), reducing capacity from 24 to 20 tubes per boat, and downloaded its Trident II D5 missiles to carry an average of four to five W76 or W88 warheads. According to FAS technical analyses, the Navy could re-upload each Trident II D5 missile to its maximum capacity of eight warheads within months. 

Simultaneously, pressure is mounting to re-arm the land-based ICBM force with Multiple Independently targetable Reentry Vehicles (MIRVs). The U.S. Air Force operates 400 active Minuteman III missiles across silos in Wyoming, North Dakota, and Montana, each currently de-MIRVed to carry a single W78 or W87 warhead. Reconfiguring these missiles to carry three warheads each, alongside maximizing submarine loads, would enable the U.S. to surge its deployed strategic arsenal from the treaty limit of 1,550 warheads to roughly 3,500 warheads without manufacturing a single new delivery vehicle. The Congressional Commission on the Strategic Posture of the United States formally recommended establishing the technical infrastructure to execute this upload, citing the requirement to deter two peer nuclear adversaries simultaneously.

Yet the American nuclear modernization timeline faces severe industrial hurdles. The Air Force’s next-generation LGM-35A Sentinel ICBM, under development by prime contractor Northrop Grumman to replace the Minuteman III, triggered a critical Nunn-McCurdy statutory cost breach when projected acquisition costs jumped from $77.7 billion in 2020 to $141 billion—an 81 percent increase. The program was restructured and recertified under then-Under Secretary of Defense William LaPlante, while then-Air Force Secretary Frank Kendall recused himself from decisions due to prior consulting work for Northrop Grumman. Because Sentinel’s initial operational capability has slipped into the 2030s, the Air Force must sustain the 50-year-old Minuteman III fleet through life-extension programs that could extend its operational service toward 2050.

Russia’s strategic modernization is similarly characterized by advanced technological deployment alongside industrial bottlenecks. The Russian Strategic Rocket Forces have deployed the Avangard hypersonic glide vehicle on modified UR-100N UTTKh boosters at Yasny and continue fielding road-mobile and silo-based RS-24 Yars ICBMs. However, the RS-28 Sarmat heavy liquid-fueled ICBM—designed to carry 10 heavy warheads or hypersonic glide vehicles to replace the SS-18 Satan—has experienced catastrophic testing failures and silo detonations at the Plesetsk test site, slowing the conversion of operational missile regiments at Uzhur and Dombarovsky.

The primary structural driver behind Washington’s pivot away from bilateral arms control remains the rapid expansion of China’s nuclear forces. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimated China’s warhead inventory at 620 weapons as of January 2026, on a trajectory toward the U.S. Department of Defense projection of 1,000 operational warheads by 2030 and 1,500 by 2035. Commercial satellite surveillance has identified approximately 775 land-based missile silos across expansive desert installations at Yumen, Hami, and Ordos, housing DF-41 and DF-31AG ICBMs, while the People's Liberation Army Navy deploys JL-3 SLBMs aboard Type 094 SSBNs. 

Beijing has systematically rejected trilateral arms control proposals. Officials at China’s Ministry of Foreign Affairs reiterate that states possessing the largest arsenals bear the primary responsibility for disarmament, noting that U.S. and Russian stockpiles each remain more than five times larger than China’s total force.

The demise of bilateral constraints has simultaneously accelerated nuclear posture adjustments among European allies. In the United Kingdom, the government lifted its warhead ceiling from 180 to 260 and is investing over £31 billion in four Dreadnought-class SSBNs to guarantee its Continuous At-Sea Deterrent into the 2060s. In France, the 2024–2030 Military Programming Law earmarks €54 billion of its €413 billion defense budget directly for nuclear deterrence, achieving full operational capability for the upgraded ASMPA-R air-launched cruise missile on Rafale fighters and starting hull fabrication for the third-generation SNLE 3G ballistic missile submarine class.

Daryl Kimball, executive director of the Arms Control Association, underscored the global risks inherent in the post-New START landscape: "More nuclear weapons will not make anyone safer. Without legally binding numerical limits and verified on-site monitoring, strategic planning defaults to worst-case assumptions, accelerating defense outlays and significantly increasing the risks of miscalculation during crises."

Across defense strategy circles, analysts have reached a sober consensus. As one defense analyst noted on X: "The end of New START isn't just the death of a treaty; it is the death of the predictability that prevented a three-front nuclear arms race for two generations." With no successor negotiations scheduled and satellite reconnaissance substituting for reciprocal inspections, strategic stability now hinges on an unconstrained tri-polar balance of deterrence.
