# The Caracas Garrison Dilemma: Inside Marco Rubio’s High-Stakes Military Negotiations with Delcy Rodríguez

Ten months after 150 American aircraft breached Venezuelan airspace on January 3, 2026, disabling radar networks and extracting Nicolás Maduro during Operation Absolute Resolve, the United States is orchestrating a structured, long-term military presence on Venezuelan soil. 

Diplomatic dispatches and senior administration officials confirm that the State Department, under Secretary of State Marco Rubio, is negotiating a bilateral Status of Forces Agreement (SOFA) with the interim government of Venezuelan President Delcy Rodríguez. The draft framework, revealed in reports by The New York Times, establishes the legal protections, operational jurisdictions, and logistical infrastructure required for regular U.S. military activities across Venezuela. The proposed agreement governs U.S. Navy vessel port visits, rotational training deployments for American advisers, humanitarian staging access, and joint operations targeting transnational narcotics cartels and insurgent sanctuaries along Venezuela's borders.

The push for a binding military pact faces an immediate constitutional hurdle in Caracas. Article 13 of the 1999 Constitution of the Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela explicitly defines the national territory as a "zone of peace" and states that "no foreign military bases or installations having in any way military purposes may be established by any power or coalition of powers." To navigate this legal prohibition without requiring an immediate constitutional referendum, American and Venezuelan negotiators have deliberately avoided designating permanent American bases. Instead, the legal text is being framed around temporary rotational deployments, maritime interdiction protocols, and joint technical assistance facilities. Senior State Department officials have stressed that formal discussions regarding permanent U.S. military bases are "way, way down the road."

The operational necessity for a formal SOFA has been accelerated by direct military action on the ground. On October 30, a coordinated drone and helicopter strike targeted a fortified base camp of the National Liberation Army (ELN) in the border state of Apure, near Puerto Páez and Buena Vista del Meta. Local human rights documentation from Fundaredes confirmed between 19 and 23 fatalities, including guerrilla logistics commanders, alongside the destruction of a massive cocaine-processing complex. U.S. officials acknowledged that the Apure raid was executed with direct American technical assistance, intelligence feeds, and drone capabilities. Coming four months after a June precision strike eliminated Tren de Aragua leader Héctor "Niño" Guerrero in Bolívar state, the Apure engagement demonstrated that combined U.S.-Venezuelan kinetic operations are actively occurring in the absence of a ratified legal framework shielding American personnel.

The security negotiations run parallel to a massive economic restructuring driven by Washington. On September 2, U.S. Energy Secretary Chris Wright joined interim President Rodríguez at the Miraflores Palace to finalize landmark energy agreements tapping into Venezuela's estimated 65 billion barrels of accessible crude reserves. Under the framework, Chevron pledged $7 billion in capital expenditures over five years to double its joint-venture output, while European firms including Italy’s Eni expanded production participation. Crude shipments are now moving regularly to refineries along the Texas Gulf Coast, with revenues flowing through U.S.-monitored accounts designed to service more than $200 billion in defaulted sovereign debt while preventing capital diversion to Beijing, Moscow, or Havana.

This bilateral convergence between Washington and Maduro’s former vice president has generated friction within Venezuela’s fractured political landscape. Opposition leader María Corina Machado, who returned to Venezuela on October 25 following months in hiding and exile, has sharply criticized the opaque nature of the security and commercial accords. Reappearing before crowds in Barquisimeto before traveling to Caracas, the Nobel Peace Prize laureate warned that entering into multi-year security and oil commitments with interim Chavista figures risks entrenching the existing ruling apparatus at the expense of an authentic democratic transition.

Machado and her allies within the Unitary Platform have demanded accelerated timelines for internationally certified presidential balloting, the immediate release of political prisoners, and full institutional transparency. However, Washington has signaled that elections will not be rushed. In an interview at the White House with The New York Times, Secretary Rubio stated that presidential elections could "ideally" take place in 2027, but declined to guarantee a fixed date before the end of President Donald Trump’s current term. Rubio underscored that competitive elections remain contingent on structural benchmarks: overhauling Venezuela’s Supreme Court of Justice (TSJ) by mid-December, replacing the National Electoral Council (CNE), executing security sector reforms, and achieving tangible progress on debt restructuring.

The open-ended timeline has prompted intense debate across policy networks and digital platforms. On Reddit subreddits including r/geopolitics and r/politics, foreign policy analysts and members of the Venezuelan diaspora have intensely debated Washington's expanding role, with several commentators describing Rubio’s day-to-day policy oversight as that of a "de facto viceroy." Discussions on X have focused on whether the security pact represents necessary operational stabilization against armed groups like the ELN or a pragmatic accommodation that trades democratic guarantees for uninterrupted oil flows and military basing rights.

For Delcy Rodríguez, the SOFA negotiations represent a pragmatic survival calculation. By granting the Pentagon legal certainty, joint counter-narcotics access, and strategic alignment, her interim administration mitigates the risk of unilateral American military moves while attempting to rebuild a collapsed economy. Yet by facilitating an institutionalized American military presence, Rodríguez must manage latent dissent among ideological Chavistas and military commanders who view foreign military cooperation as an affront to national sovereignty. As diplomatic drafting continues between Washington and Caracas, Venezuela remains balanced between fragile stabilization and an indefinite deferral of full democratic rule.
