# Hertz’s Structured Descent: How a $450 Million Debt-and-Equity Lifeline Triggered a 40.71% Market Capitulation

On June 24, 2026, the equity valuation of Hertz Global Holdings, Inc. ($HTZ) collapsed in a single trading session, with the stock plunging 40.71% to close at $3.00 per share. The sell-off was accompanied by an extraordinary surge in trading volume, which reached 92,001,287 shares—many times its historical daily average. The descent continued into June 25, 2026, with the stock sliding another 13.67% to close at $2.59. This violent repricing was triggered by a dual-financing package consisting of a $350 million exchangeable senior secured note offering and a concurrent $100 million common stock offering. To a market already sensitized to Hertz’s highly leveraged capital structure, the mechanics of this capital raise looked less like a standard liquidity bridge and more like a structured engine for short-selling and dilution. 

The transaction highlights three core questions that now dominate discussions among institutional credit desks and retail trading forums alike. First, how does the $100 million share-lending agreement with J.P. Morgan Securities LLC function as a technical short-selling conduit, and why did it prompt immediate equity capitulation? Second, how severe is the operational strain on Hertz’s balance sheet as it navigates the ongoing depreciation of the used-vehicle market? And third, are the online debates regarding an impending second bankruptcy for the rental giant a rational assessment of its $18.2 billion debt load or an overreaction to a painful but necessary recapitalization?

To understand the equity collapse, one must dissect the mechanics of the concurrent offerings. Hertz's subsidiary, The Hertz Corporation, priced $350 million in aggregate principal amount of 6.75% Exchangeable Senior First-Lien Secured Notes due 2030. The note offering was upsized from an initial $300 million, indicating some institutional demand, but the terms reflect a borrower with limited options. The coupon is structured as a Payment-in-Kind (PIK) split: 3.375% is payable in cash and 3.375% is payable in additional notes. This PIK feature allows Hertz to conserve cash in the near term but signals to credit markets that the company’s operating cash flow is insufficient to service traditional debt. S&P Global Ratings assigned the notes a 'B-' issue-level rating with a recovery rating of '3', reflecting expectations of a 50% to 70% recovery in a default scenario, while Fitch Ratings assigned an expected rating of 'B+(EXP)' with a Recovery Rating of 'RR2'. Both agencies maintain a 'B-' corporate credit rating on Hertz with a negative outlook, following S&P's downgrade in March 2026.

The true source of the equity panic, however, lies in the companion $100 million common stock offering. Rather than conducting a traditional public equity raise to shore up its balance sheet, Hertz entered into a share-lending agreement with J.P. Morgan Securities LLC. Under this arrangement, Hertz loaned 37,037,037 shares of its common stock to the bank, which priced them at $2.70 per share. J.P. Morgan then sold these borrowed shares directly into the open market. Crucially, Hertz does not receive any of the proceeds from these sales, receiving only a nominal lending fee, and expects the shares to be returned upon the settlement of the exchangeable notes. 

The purpose of this facility is purely structural: it provides the physical shares necessary for the institutional buyers of the exchangeable notes to establish short positions to hedge their investment. Because exchangeable notes carry embedded equity options, institutional arbitrageurs and credit investors must short the underlying stock to run a delta-neutral book. In a highly shorted stock like Hertz, where the borrow is tight and expensive, the share-lending facility acts as a government-sanctioned credit facility for hedgers, flooding the market with 37 million shares of immediate, non-discretionary selling pressure. The market recognized that this arrangement effectively subsidized short-sellers, sparking a run to the exits.

This technical pressure collided with a grim fundamental update. Concurrently with the financing, Hertz released preliminary second-quarter 2026 guidance, warning that its Adjusted Corporate EBITDA would trend toward the bottom of its previous guidance range, landing between $50 million and $80 million. The culprit remains a familiar ghost: used-vehicle depreciation. Hertz disclosed "unexpected softness in the used car market" in May 2026, which wiped out gains recorded in April. Consequently, Hertz expects its net depreciation per unit per month (Net DPU) to hover around $300 for the second quarter. 

While a $300 monthly Net DPU is a slight improvement from the $312 Net DPU reported in the first quarter of 2026, the mathematical impact on Hertz’s balance sheet remains staggering. With an average fleet size of approximately 514,000 vehicles in Q1 2026, a $300 per-unit depreciation rate translates to roughly $154.2 million in depreciation charges every month, or more than $462 million per quarter. In Q1 2026, this depreciation expense drove a net loss of $333 million on $2.0 billion in revenue, resulting in an EPS of -$0.72. CEO Gil West, who took the helm on April 1, 2024, has been forced to manage the costly wind-down of the company's disastrous post-pandemic bet on electric vehicles. Under West, Hertz has aggressively sold off its EV fleet, but the residual values of its internal combustion engine (ICE) vehicles are now also falling, caught in a broader correction. The Manheim Used Vehicle Value Index stood at 212.6 in May 2026, down significantly from its 2022 peak of over 250, illustrating the persistent deflation in wholesale vehicle values that continues to erode Hertz’s asset base.

This fundamental deterioration has reignited intense debate on social media, where the ghost of Hertz's 2020 Chapter 11 filing looms large. On r/wallstreetbets, where Hertz was once celebrated as a meme-stock darling that beat the odds, the tone has turned sharply cynical. On June 24, 2026, a thread titled "Let's save Hertz.. again!" gained traction. The author, a user named `harambeface`, wrote sarcastically that the company's "outdated business model," "significant debt," and "lack of cash" represented a "38% opportunity" for a "ten bagger." 

The community’s response was swift and dismissive, reflecting a retail market that has grown weary of catching falling knives. One prominent comment read, "Hell no this company sucks," while another user remarked, "I'm not catching this falling knife." Others pointed directly to the capital structure, stating they refuse to "act as bagholders for the stock." Commenters also detailed personal frustrations with Hertz's retail operations, citing instances where reserved vehicles were unavailable at airport counters, suggesting that the operational rot matches the financial distress. 

The core debate is whether these bankruptcy fears are mathematically justified. Hertz’s balance sheet is highly leveraged, carrying approximately $18.2 billion in total debt against $1.219 billion in cash and restricted cash at the end of the first quarter of 2026. However, the majority of Hertz’s debt is vehicle-backed, structured through asset-backed security (ABS) trusts. These ABS structures insulate the parent company to some degree, but they require Hertz to maintain strict collateral values. When the wholesale value of the fleet declines, Hertz must post cash to the ABS trusts to maintain the required loan-to-value ratios. This "ABS margin call" risk is the true existential threat to the company. The net proceeds of $339.5 million from the new $350 million PIK notes will be used to pay down the company’s corporate revolving credit facility, restoring some liquidity. Yet, if the Manheim Index resumes its downward trajectory, the cash requirements of the ABS trusts could easily overwhelm the company’s remaining liquidity. Gil West’s structured refinancing buys the company time, but it has come at a devastating cost to equity holders, who are being diluted and shorted out of their positions to keep the debt engine running.
