# The SpaceX Gravity Test: Inside the $1.5 Trillion Tension Between Retail Hype and the Starship Capital Burn

When SpaceX made its highly anticipated public debut on June 12, 2026, under the Nasdaq ticker SPCX, it was hailed as a watershed moment for capital markets. Pricing at $135 per share, the offering raised $75 billion—which expanded to $85.7 billion after underwriters exercised the overallotment option—valuing the company at a staggering $1.75 trillion and securing its place as the largest initial public offering in history. Four days later, on June 16, the stock peaked at a high of $225.64, pushing its market capitalization past $2.93 trillion. Since that mid-summer peak, however, the laws of financial gravity have asserted themselves. SpaceX shares have steadily corrected, closing at $115.07 on July 24, 2026, after touching a record low of $110.85 on July 23, representing a 15% slide below its initial offering price. This retreat occurs against a challenging macroeconomic backdrop, as Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh maintains a restrictive policy stance and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent monitors a volatile bond market, leaving investors hypersensitive to high-valuation tech narratives.

The core controversy animating the public market pricing of SPCX lies in the tension between retail enthusiasm and institutional skepticism. On the Reddit forum r/stocks, a thread titled "Am I The Only One That's Genuinely Bullish on SPCX?" highlights this divide, with one commenter warning that "SPCX as constituted is 60% AI stock 40% space at best." This skepticism centers on Elon Musk’s decision to execute an all-stock acquisition of xAI on February 2, 2026, which valued the artificial intelligence startup at $250 billion and created a combined entity with a pre-IPO valuation of $1.25 trillion. By combining the launch capabilities of SpaceX, the satellite internet constellation of Starlink, and the machine learning infrastructure of xAI, Musk created a corporate behemoth that defies traditional classification. Yet, retail investors are increasingly questioning whether the public market is being used to absorb the massive capital requirements of these combined projects, expressing fears that they are merely providing "exit liquidity" for early backers.

This structural anxiety is amplified by the company’s capital structure. SpaceX has approximately 13 billion shares outstanding, yet the current public float is exceedingly thin, representing only about 4.5% of the total equity, or roughly 585 million shares. Elon Musk retains absolute control of the enterprise, commanding 85% of the voting power through super-voting Class B shares. This low-float dynamic has historically magnified price swings in both directions. While retail traders bid the stock up during its June debut, institutional desks have been far more cautious, pointing to the structural hurdles facing the company's core operations. As one r/stocks participant cautioned, "SPCX - Beware, institutional money is NOT buying this trash on the open market." This sentiment reflects a growing view that the stock's public pricing is artificially decoupled from the underlying economics of its distinct operating segments.

An analysis of the company's S-1 filing reveals that SpaceX’s financial engine is running on two very different speeds. In the fiscal year 2025, the company reported total revenue of $18.67 billion, a 33% increase from the prior year, alongside an EBITDA of $6.58 billion. However, the company posted a net loss of $4.9 billion. The Connectivity segment, driven by Starlink, is the sole engine of profitability, generating $11.4 billion in revenue—representing 61.1% of the company's total top line—and $4.4 billion in operating income, supported by a subscriber base that reached 12 million by mid-2026. In contrast, the Launch segment generated $4.1 billion in revenue, showing a modest 8% year-over-year growth. This sluggishness is a direct result of launch capacity constraints, as SpaceX increasingly dedicates its Falcon 9 fleet to deploying its own Starlink satellites rather than servicing external, commercial launch clients.

Meanwhile, the developmental cash burn of the Starship program continues to drain resources. Capital expenditures in fiscal year 2025 reached $20.7 billion, driven by the furious pace of Starship testing and infrastructure construction at Starbase in Boca Chica, Texas. To date, SpaceX has poured over $15 billion into the development of the Starship program. The financial friction lies in the fact that while Starlink is highly cash-generative, its capital expenditure requirements remain high, and Starship has yet to achieve operational commercial status. This creates an inter-segment dependency where Starlink’s cash flows are entirely consumed by the developmental costs of the next-generation launch platform, leaving no free cash flow for the parent company.

Compounding this launch-and-connectivity burn is the capital-intensive nature of the newly integrated AI segment. The xAI division relies on the "Colossus" supercomputer cluster in Memphis, Tennessee, which requires massive infrastructure investments. A key support for this segment was revealed in July 2026, when Alphabet Inc. disclosed a $94.1 billion stake in SpaceX in its quarterly regulatory filing, reflecting its role as a key early investor since 2015 and representing approximately 5% to 6% of the company's total equity. Google also entered into a commercial agreement to pay SpaceX $920 million per month to access xAI’s computational capacity to support its Gemini Enterprise AI platform. While this monthly compute payment provides a critical revenue stream, it is offset by the enormous energy costs associated with running massive GPU clusters, highlighting the structural reality that xAI remains a heavy net drain on SpaceX’s balance sheet.

The immediate challenge for SPCX is the upcoming lock-up expiration on August 6, 2026, which occurs just two trading days after the company reports its first quarterly earnings on August 4. Approximately 911.5 million shares, representing 20% of restricted insider and employee holdings, are scheduled to become eligible for sale on the open market. A performance-based clause in the S-1 could have unlocked an additional 455.8 million shares if the stock had traded at least 30% above its $135 IPO price (at least $175.50) for five of the ten trading days preceding the earnings release. However, with the stock trading near $115, this performance trigger will not be met. Retail investors on Reddit are actively debating whether this influx of shares will create a "supply shock" that drives the stock lower, with some characterizing the market setup as a "zero-sum game" where early institutional investors are looking to lock in gains at the expense of retail buyers.

This debate highlights a fundamental disagreement over how to value the company. Late-stage private funding rounds in late 2025 valued SpaceX at $210 billion before the xAI merger. The public market's current valuation of $1.5 trillion represents a massive premium over those private valuations, prompting analysts to debate whether the AI narrative justifies such a multiple. Morgan Stanley analyst Adam Jonas has defended the company, maintaining an Overweight rating and a $300.00 price target. Jonas argues that the post-IPO sell-off is "overdone" and that the current market price assigns "zero or even negative value" to SpaceX's AI operations, which he believes represent more than 50% of the company's long-term worth.

As SpaceX approaches its August 4 earnings release, the company faces a crucial test. It must convince public markets that the recurring revenue of Starlink can successfully fund the dual capital drains of the Starship program and the xAI supercomputing infrastructure. With 911.5 million shares set to unlock on August 6, the thin float that helped drive the stock's initial surge may soon give way to a much larger supply of tradable equity, forcing a final resolution between retail narrative hype and public financial reality.
