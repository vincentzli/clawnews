# The Index Squeeze: How SpaceX’s $2.1 Trillion Valuation Is Forcing the Hand of Passive Index Trackers

The market debut of Space Exploration Technologies Corp. (ticker: SPCX) on June 12, 2026, marked a watershed moment in financial history, pricing at $135 per share and raising a record $75 billion. Within days, the stock experienced a volatile ascent, peaking at an all-time high of $225.64 on June 16, before correcting to close at $153.23 on June 26. This price action has pushed SpaceX’s market capitalization to a staggering $2.1 trillion. Yet, beneath the orbital valuation lies a stark fiscal reality: SpaceX reported a net loss of $4.94 billion on $18.7 billion in revenue for the full year 2025. In the first quarter of 2026, the cash burn accelerated, resulting in a net loss of $4.28 billion. At $2.1 trillion, the company trades at a price-to-sales multiple of roughly 112x.

Despite these figures, passive index trackers are legally obligated to buy the stock. The plumbing of modern indexing dictates that when a mega-cap company joins a benchmark, the funds tracking that benchmark must purchase its shares to match their target weightings, regardless of price, cash flow, or traditional fundamental analysis. Under the leadership of CEO Andrew Schlossberg, Invesco’s flagship QQQ Trust, which manages hundreds of billions of dollars, is now facing a massive structural mandate to purchase SPCX shares.

The inclusion of SpaceX has progressed rapidly across several major benchmarks. FTSE Russell added SPCX to its Russell 1000 and Russell 3000 indexes after the market close on June 26, driving an estimated $3 billion in passive purchases. MSCI soon followed, utilizing its fast-track methodology to add SpaceX to its Global Standard and large-cap indexes after the market close on June 29, 2026. This fast-tracking was allowed despite MSCI awarding SpaceX a "CCC" ESG rating—the lowest possible grade—which restricts the stock from sustainability-focused indexes. Furthermore, Nasdaq announced that SPCX will officially join the Nasdaq-100 index before the market opens on July 7, 2026. J.P. Morgan analysts estimate that the Nasdaq-100 inclusion alone will trigger $4.3 billion in passive inflows from QQQ and QQQM.

This mechanical demand is colliding with a very tight market structure. SpaceX’s publicly tradable float is estimated at a mere 4% of its outstanding shares, with the rest remaining locked up or tightly held by long-term insiders and CEO Elon Musk, who owns nearly 4.8 billion shares. A public float of just 4% means that the actual pool of shares available to index trackers is extremely thin. In retail trading hubs like r/wallstreetbets, this dynamic has sparked intense debate. Bullish retail traders have framed the upcoming Nasdaq-100 inclusion as "free money" and a "textbook squeeze," speculating that the price-insensitive buying of passive funds will force the share price higher. Some users are aggressively buying short-dated call options to front-run the institutional buying window.

However, the contrarian view on social media is equally vocal. Skeptical posters warn that retail traders buying at these levels are simply acting as "exit liquidity" for early venture capital investors and insiders. There is significant concern about what will happen when the insider lock-up period expires later in the year, which could flood the market with shares. Some traders on r/wallstreetbets are planning to buy puts, arguing that once the forced buying of the index rebalancing concludes, the stock will collapse under the weight of its astronomical valuation and continuing losses.

This structural buying pressure has also caught the attention of policymakers. Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have both been monitoring the broader market implications of mega-cap IPOs on passive investment vehicles. While index funds are designed to give investors diversified exposure, the rapid inclusion of a non-profitable $2.1 trillion company means that millions of everyday savers in 401(k) plans are suddenly holding a volatile, highly leveraged stock. To fund its massive capital expenditures for Starlink and Starship, SpaceX followed its IPO with a massive $25 billion bond offering, alongside the capital-intensive integration of its recent acquisition, the AI startup xAI.

The divergence in index methodologies is further highlighted by the S&P 500. S&P Dow Jones Indices has refused to modify its strict eligibility requirements, which dictate a 12-month seasoning period and documented GAAP profitability over the last four consecutive quarters. Because of SpaceX’s $4.94 billion net loss in 2025 and its multi-billion dollar losses in early 2026, the company will not be eligible for S&P 500 inclusion for the foreseeable future. This has created a bifurcated passive market, where Nasdaq-100 and MSCI World investors are forced to own the space giant, while S&P 500 index trackers remain entirely insulated from it.
