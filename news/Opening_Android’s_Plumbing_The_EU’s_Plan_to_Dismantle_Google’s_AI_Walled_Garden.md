# Opening Android’s Plumbing: The EU’s Plan to Dismantle Google’s AI Walled Garden

For years, the European Union has pursued the dream of a digital single market where homegrown startups and foreign challengers can compete on a level playing field with Silicon Valley’s giants. But the struggle has entered a new and far more intimate arena: the internal wiring of the modern smartphone. On July 16, 2026, the European Commission, led by First Executive Vice-President Teresa Ribera, escalated this campaign by issuing two sets of binding specification measures against Google under the Digital Markets Act (DMA). The landmark ruling represents a critical shift in how mobile operating systems and artificial intelligence are regulated, fundamentally targeting the default-use advantage of Google's Gemini AI. By requiring Google to open 11 system-level features of Android to third-party AI assistants, the EU aims to dismantle Google's self-preferencing defaults and allow rival systems to run with the exact same OS privileges as its first-party services.

The technical scale of the Commission's interoperability mandate is unprecedented. Under the new rules, Google must grant rival AI assistants, such as Microsoft Copilot or custom developer tools, equal access to highly sensitive Android APIs that were previously restricted. The 11 mandated features span four key functional areas. In the Invocation category, Google must expose wake-word registration, allowing competitors to configure custom background wake-word triggers (such as a voice command equivalent to "Hey Google") even when the device’s display is inactive or in battery-saver mode. In the Context category, the mandates cover real-time screen content analysis, enabling rival models to read on-screen text and pixels to anticipate user needs. For Actions, the measures require Google to open its notifications API, ensure deep-links to apps are "Gemini-neutral," and allow third-party assistants to execute cross-app background commands, such as booking a ride, drafting an email, or adjusting system settings. Finally, the Access to Resources category requires Google to share hardware and software resources, including access to on-device AI models.

To ensure compliance, the Commission has set a strict timeline. Google is required to implement the first wave of interoperability features in its next major operating system release, Android 18, which is scheduled for launch by August 1, 2027. More complex features, specifically concurrent hotword detection—which allows multiple virtual assistants to listen for voice triggers simultaneously—must be fully integrated into Android 19 by August 1, 2028. For five of the most sensitive capabilities, such as screen automation and background execution, Google is permitted to maintain a certification process to verify that third-party services meet necessary data security requirements before gaining access.

Alongside the Android mandates, the Commission issued a second decision under Article 6(11) of the DMA, requiring Google to share anonymized search query, click, and ranking data with eligible rival search engines and AI chatbots starting in January 2027. This data must be offered on fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory (FRAND) licensing terms, with a finalized pricing and distribution model expected to be published by January 2027. By forcing Google to open its search query logs, the EU hopes to lower the barriers to entry for smaller search engines like DuckDuckGo, Ecosia, and Qwant, which have long struggled to match the quality of Google's search algorithms due to a lack of search volume data. These specification decisions are legally binding "build orders" rather than financial penalties, though failure to comply by the designated deadlines could expose Google to subsequent non-compliance proceedings and fines of up to 10% of its annual worldwide revenue.

Google has strongly opposed the measures, arguing they compromise user privacy and device security by opening critical threat vectors. Kent Walker, Google’s President of Global Affairs, warned that the decisions "risk undermining vital privacy and security guardrails for millions of Europeans." Walker argued that sharing query logs could expose private user queries to "unfamiliar companies" without consent, and that opening deep Android APIs would "weaken citizens' privacy, risk business trade secrets, and endanger national security." Security experts note that granting deep system access—like background screen capture and real-time microphone monitoring—to third-party apps creates potential attack surfaces for spyware or data exfiltration. The European Commission, however, maintains that these security risks can be mitigated through Google's approved certification frameworks and that security concerns must not be leveraged as a pretext to lock out competitive AI alternatives.

In contrast, Apple has taken a far more combative path. Rather than risk similar regulatory conflicts under the DMA, Apple has chosen to withhold its rebuilt Siri AI (Apple Intelligence) features from the European market on iOS 27 and iPadOS 27. Because macOS 27 and visionOS 27 are not currently designated as gatekeeper platforms under the DMA, Apple will allow its new AI features to launch on those operating systems within the EU. Apple argues that the DMA’s interoperability mandates make it impossible to guarantee the security and privacy of its users on mobile devices if it must open its system to third-party AI models. The EU, meanwhile, maintains that nothing in its laws prevents Apple from launching its products, arguing that the gatekeeper is simply choosing to deprive European consumers of new technology to avoid compliance.

As the industry prepares for the January 2027 search data sharing deadline and the August 2027 release of Android 18, the global AI assistant market is fracturing along geographic lines. European users face either delayed features from companies like Apple or a more fragmented, multi-assistant operating system on Google's platform. Meanwhile, developers are watching closely to see if the FRAND terms Google proposes in early 2027 will truly lower the barrier to entry or if the compliance costs and technical hurdles of building system-level AI assistants will keep the market firmly in the hands of the tech giants.
