# The Protectors of the Oil: Inside the U.S. Military Footprint in Post-Maduro Venezuela

From the flight deck of the USS *Fort Lauderdale* (LPD 28), anchored just off the devastated port of La Guaira, the geopolitical reshaping of South America is audible. The roar of U.S. Army CH-47 Chinook helicopters and MV-22B Osprey tiltrotor aircraft from the Littoral Combat Force 24 (LCF-24) fills the humid Caribbean air. On the docks below, members of a Marine Corps Combat Logistics Company oversee the offloading of medium-sized transport trucks, mobile off-road vehicles, and military ambulances. Officially, these forces are part of a humanitarian deployment of approximately 2,000 U.S. military personnel sent to assist Venezuela in the wake of the catastrophic twin earthquakes that struck northwestern and central Venezuela on June 24, 2026. 

But as the rescue operations wind down and infrastructure repair begins, the presence of these troops has become the focal point of an intense global debate over the nature of U.S. power in the Western Hemisphere. The humanitarian mission is deeply nested within a highly militarized framework: the logistics and command structure established under "Operation Southern Spear," a regional maritime campaign targeting transnational drug cartels, and the political reality created by "Operation Absolute Resolve," the dramatic U.S. special operations raid on January 3, 2026, that captured former Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores. 

The physical deployment of U.S. troops in Venezuela is the most visible element of a conditional political transition that began when Vice President Delcy Rodríguez was sworn in as acting president on January 5, 2026. While the Venezuelan Supreme Tribunal of Justice formalized her temporary status, her continued hold on power has been heavily structured by Washington. Shortly after Maduro’s capture, U.S. President Donald Trump made it clear that Rodríguez’s tenure is conditional on her compliance with American directives. Yet, in public, Rodríguez has navigated this arrangement by playing a complex double game, frequently denouncing the United States as an "illegal invader" and demanding Maduro’s release to appease domestic loyalists, even as her cabinet implements sweeping hydrocarbons and mining reforms that facilitate U.S. economic control.

The economic levers of this transition were solidified on January 9, 2026, when President Trump signed Executive Order 14373. The order effectively stripped Caracas of direct control over its oil revenues, directing proceeds from Venezuelan crude exports into U.S.-supervised escrow accounts. The U.S. Treasury’s Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) subsequently issued General Licenses, including GL 46 and GL 52, which authorized established U.S. entities to lift, export, and refine Venezuelan-origin oil under strict compliance frameworks. For critics, this financial mechanism, paired with U.S. troops monitoring critical energy hubs under the guise of earthquake relief, constitutes a *de facto* military and economic occupation designed to secure long-term control over the world’s largest proven oil reserves.

The geopolitical stakes escalated further on August 12, 2026. At a security summit in Panama, U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth announced that Colombia had joined the Americas Counter Cartel Coalition (A3C)—the operational arm of the U.S.-led "Shield of the Americas" initiative—as its 19th member. Backed by a newly announced $1 billion U.S. security assistance package, Colombia’s newly inaugurated conservative President, Abelardo de la Espriella, authorized joint military operations with U.S. forces. This represents a complete reversal of the non-interventionist policies of his predecessor, Gustavo Petro. Secretary Hegseth framed the expansion as part of a modern "Donroe Doctrine"—a play on the Monroe Doctrine emphasizing U.S. primacy and the use of "lethal military force" to dismantle narco-terrorist networks, which he compared to ISIS and al-Qaeda.

Yet, even as the coalition expands, the legal and ethical foundation of the U.S. footprint is facing severe domestic and international scrutiny. Democratic lawmakers in Washington have accused the administration of using anti-cartel operations as a smoke screen for forced regime change. Senator Chris Murphy (D-CT) publicly accused Secretary Hegseth and Secretary of State Marco Rubio of having "literally lied to our face" during closed-door congressional briefings regarding their plans for Venezuela, calling the administration's long-term regional strategy "insane."

Concurrently, Senator Tim Kaine (D-VA), a member of the Senate Foreign Relations and Armed Services Committees, mounted a sustained campaign against the conduct of Operation Southern Spear. Kaine revealed that the campaign’s kinetic strikes in international waters had resulted in over 200 deaths, arguing that many targets had no documented links to drug trafficking. After reviewing classified intelligence, Kaine described these casualties as "murder victims" and accused the Pentagon of refusing to declassify details of the operations because they were "ashamed" of the legal justifications. Under intense pressure following an August 3, 2026 letter from Kaine to President Trump, the Pentagon announced it would terminate Operation Southern Spear by the end of August.

Despite the wind-down of the maritime interdiction campaign, the 2,000-troop humanitarian deployment in Venezuela remains. Supporters of the footprint, including administration officials and regional allies, argue that the forces are a necessary stabilizing presence in a fragile post-conflict state. They point to the logistics of the earthquake relief: the 621st Contingency Response Wing ("Devil Raiders") successfully reopened Simón Bolívar International Airport, enabling the delivery of over $300 million in humanitarian aid and facilitating search-and-rescue teams from Fairfax County, Virginia, and Los Angeles County. Without U.S. heavy-lift capabilities and engineering units, they argue, the country’s damaged infrastructure would have collapsed entirely, triggering a massive regional refugee crisis.

The debate remains polarized. While supporters see a successful civil-military partnership stabilizing a broken nation, critics see a permanent military foothold established under the pretext of disaster relief. With the termination of Operation Southern Spear, the U.S. footprint in Venezuela is transitioning from an active maritime blockade to a complex, long-term stabilization mission, highlighting the unresolved friction between the administration's stated stabilizing goals and regional concerns over national sovereignty.
