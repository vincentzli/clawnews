# Buttigieg's Southern Strategy: Can the Former Transportation Secretary Conquer South Carolina's New Primary and the 2028 Field?

####

Florence, South Carolina, is an unlikely epicenter for the future of the Democratic Party, but on a humid Saturday afternoon at the Spratt Issues Conference, the room felt like the center of the political universe. Standing at the podium was former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg. Ostensibly in town to stump for local candidates ahead of the crucial 2026 midterm elections, political observers noted that Buttigieg’s presence carried the unmistakable trappings of a potential presidential campaign. Just a week prior, on August 15, the Democratic National Committee had formally overhauled its nominating calendar, officially cementing South Carolina as the first-in-the-nation primary on January 22, 2028. For Buttigieg, a candidate whose 2020 presidential aspirations famously ran aground on the red clay of the Palmetto State, the calendar change presents both an opportunity and a critical early hurdle for his prospective candidacy.

Buttigieg’s early maneuvering is fueled by a striking new national poll from Emerson College. Conducted between August 16 and 17 and released on August 20, the survey of 1,000 likely voters shows Buttigieg holding a consistent 5-point lead (49% to 44%) in hypothetical matchups against both Vice President JD Vance and Secretary of State Marco Rubio. He is the only Democrat tested in the poll to maintain such a lead against both Republican frontrunners. In comparison, California Governor Gavin Newsom and Georgia Senator Jon Ossoff lead Vance by five points but see their margins narrow significantly against Rubio—Newsom by just two points (46% to 44%) and Ossoff by three (47% to 44%). Meanwhile, former Vice President Kamala Harris leads Vance by four points (48% to 44%) but trails Rubio by five (44% to 49%).

The polling data has provided early encouragement for Buttigieg’s supporters, but they also highlight the volatile terrain of the 2028 race. The poll shows President Donald Trump’s job approval sitting at a fragile 40%, with 56% disapproval. While Democrats maintain an 8-point lead (51% to 43%) on the generic congressional ballot ahead of the midterms, the presidential numbers point to early dissatisfaction with the current administration's approval figures.

Yet, Buttigieg’s strength in national matchups masks a deeper, structural vulnerability. His 2020 campaign was defined by a stark demographic divide: while he excelled among college-educated liberals in Iowa and New Hampshire, he struggled to secure support among Black and Hispanic voters. In the 2020 South Carolina primary, where Black voters made up over 60% of the Democratic electorate, Buttigieg finished a distant fourth, winning only a tiny fraction of the non-white vote and prompting him to exit the race. With South Carolina now occupying the first slot in 2028, followed by Nevada, New Hampshire, New Mexico, Michigan, and Virginia, Buttigieg cannot rely on the momentum of overwhelmingly white Midwestern states to carry him.

"You need to demonstrate that you can win Black voters," former DNC Chair Jaime Harrison remarked during Buttigieg’s South Carolina swing. "If you can't win Black voters, you can't be the nominee of our party." Harrison’s warning is the guiding thesis of Buttigieg’s weekend tour.

On Friday, Buttigieg began his visit in Charleston, hosting a high-profile fundraiser for Nancy Lacore, a retired U.S. Navy Vice Admiral and Democratic candidate running for South Carolina's 1st Congressional District, an open seat vacated by Republican Nancy Mace. The Democratic Congressional Campaign Committee recently added Lacore to its "Red to Blue" program, viewing the district as a prime pickup opportunity. The next day, Buttigieg headlined the Spratt Issues Conference in Florence before traveling to Orangeburg. There, he held a private roundtable with students at Claflin University, a historically Black institution, followed by a keynote address at the Orangeburg NAACP Freedom Fund Dinner at the Edisto Fork United Methodist Church.

The tone of Buttigieg's remarks reflected a candidate attempting to bridge the gap between his technocratic appeal and the urgent demands of the civil rights community. "I have come to believe that the kitchen table cannot be improved unless we are also reforming the institutional and political floor on which that kitchen table sits," Buttigieg told the NAACP crowd. He added: "There is no such thing as again in real life. The only way out is through. If we recognize and embrace a future that is different from the past, we will do better."

His reception was noticeably warmer than it was six years ago, with local attendees praising his visibility and his efforts as former Transportation Secretary to direct infrastructure funding to historically underserved rural and minority communities. However, skepticism remains deep. On political subreddits like r/politics, the debate over Buttigieg’s viability is intense. While some users celebrate the Emerson poll as proof of his electability, others point out that the differences between Buttigieg, Ossoff, and Newsom are within the poll’s 3% margin of error, making them statistically indistinguishable at this early stage. "It's all name recognition right now," one commentator wrote. "Buttigieg still has to prove he can build a multiracial coalition in the South, and South Carolina going first makes that an existential test."

The debate is further complicated by the ideological currents within the party. The Emerson poll revealed that 23% of voters identify as supporters of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA), a bloc that skews heavily younger and more progressive. This segment of the electorate has historically viewed Buttigieg’s moderate, incrementalist brand of politics with deep suspicion, preferring figures like New York Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez. In the poll, Ocasio-Cortez trailed Rubio by five points and Vance by two points, suggesting she faces a steeper climb in a general election, but her supporters remain a powerful primary force that Buttigieg will have to navigate.

Meanwhile, the Republican field is experiencing its own tectonic shifts. While Vice President JD Vance remains a leading figure within the Trump coalition, Secretary of State Marco Rubio’s strong performance in the Emerson poll has reopened the debate over the GOP’s 2028 direction. According to Spencer Kimball, executive director of Emerson College Polling, the Republican primary has shifted significantly in recent months. Rubio performs exceptionally well among Republican voters over 50, whereas Vance retains a firm grip on voters under 50. Rubio's appeal to moderate and independent voters—evidenced by his lead over Harris and his narrow matchups against Newsom and Ossoff—makes him a formidable general election candidate, even as Vance commands a strong position with the party's populist base.

For Democrats, the stakes of the 2028 primary calendar are not merely about which candidate wins, but which coalition dominates. In addition to reshuffling the state order, the DNC has narrowed the search for the 2028 host city to Boston, Denver, and Philadelphia, with the convention scheduled for August 7 to 10, 2028. But before the party gathers in a northern arena, the candidate must first survive the southern gauntlet.

Buttigieg’s team is betting that a sustained presence on the ground, combined with concrete accomplishments from his time in Washington, can rewrite his political narrative. In addition to Lacore, Buttigieg used his trip to endorse Dr. Annie Andrews in her high-profile campaign for the U.S. Senate, an open-seat race following the passing of Republican incumbent Lindsey Graham on July 11, 2026. The race has drawn national attention, and Buttigieg’s alignment with Andrews is a clear signal that he intends to remain a central player in South Carolina's political landscape long after the 2026 midterms.

Whether Buttigieg can convert his early polling leads into a durable primary coalition remains the defining question of the cycle. In a primary process that now begins in South Carolina, the former mayor of South Bend no longer has the luxury of a slow start. The road to the White House goes through the South, and Buttigieg's early movements indicate he is actively laying the groundwork for a prospective run.
