# The Geneva Alliance: Can the UN’s New AI Commission Bridge the Global Digital Divide, or is it Big Tech's Ultimate Regulatory Shield?

Under the Swiss skies of Geneva, as diplomats and tech executives gathered for the UN’s annual AI for Good Global Summit from July 7 to July 10, 2026, a new chapter in global technology governance was officially opened. On July 2, 2026, the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) announced the creation of the AI for Good Global Commission, a high-level body designed to address the fractured landscape of artificial intelligence policy. When the Commission held its inaugural meeting on July 8, the concentration of political and economic figures in the room was notable. Yet, behind the presentations and promises of a shared digital future, a debate is brewing over whether this new body represents a genuine path toward global equity or an exercise in corporate diplomacy.

Co-chaired by Rwanda’s President Paul Kagame and Salesforce Chairman and CEO Marc Benioff, with ITU Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin serving as Vice-Chair, the Commission brings together a coalition of more than 40 founding members. The roster includes prominent leaders of the global tech economy: NVIDIA’s Jensen Huang, Amazon’s Andy Jassy, Microsoft’s Brad Smith, Qualcomm’s Cristiano Amon, Accenture’s Julie Sweet, Grab’s Anthony Tan, and Anthropic’s Jack Clark. They sit alongside heads of state from Estonia, Singapore, Iceland, and Rwanda, as well as directors of various United Nations agencies. 

The Commission’s stated mandate is practical rather than regulatory. Its core mission is to establish collaborative pathways to build global trust in artificial intelligence, expand access to advanced technologies, and bridge the digital divide. The urgency of this mission was underscored by the ITU’s own statistics showing that approximately 2.2 billion people worldwide remain completely offline. As generative AI models require increasingly massive computational power, high-speed connectivity, and specialized data centers, the gap between the technological haves and have-nots is widening. Members of the Commission warned during their inaugural session that without intervention, developing nations risk being permanently marginalized in the AI era.

To address this, the new body is modeled on the ITU/UNESCO Broadband Commission for Sustainable Development, which was launched in May 2010 to expand global internet access. That body relied on public-private partnerships to mobilize infrastructure investments, a strategy that President Kagame has supported. Speaking in Geneva, Kagame emphasized that the Commission's success would depend on cooperation between governments and corporations, focusing on digital infrastructure, skills training, and accountable governance. 

However, the voluntary, non-regulatory nature of the Commission has drawn criticism from digital rights advocates, academics, and civil society organizations. For many observers, the inclusion of the world’s most powerful tech CEOs as full voting members raises concerns about regulatory capture. Critics argue that the Commission could serve as a public relations buffer for tech giants, allowing them to showcase voluntary philanthropic initiatives while lobbying against binding international rules. 

These anxieties culminated in an open letter addressed to ITU Secretary-General Doreen Bogdan-Martin, signed by over 45 civil society organizations and academic experts, including the European Center for Not-for-Profit Law (ECNL) and Access Now. The signatories pointed out the absence of meaningful civil society and academic representation within the Commission’s leadership. "AI governance cannot be shaped solely by state and corporate perspectives," the letter warned, arguing that excluding public-interest voices risks making the body's recommendations less responsive, less trusted, less accountable, and ultimately less effective.

The discontent has translated into active counter-mobilization. Access Now announced the organization of its own counter-summit, titled "Not AI for Good," scheduled to take place on July 15, 2026. This parallel forum is designed to challenge what organizers call the tech-solutionist bias of the UN summits. Instead of celebrating the potential of AI, the counter-summit will focus on the immediate, tangible harms of the technology—specifically the growing role of major tech companies as defense contractors, the deployment of AI in active warfare, and the human rights abuses associated with automated surveillance systems. 

Geopolitical dynamics also affect the new Commission. While American and Chinese corporate interests are represented—with executives from Microsoft, Amazon, and NVIDIA on one side, and Chinese telecom giant ZTE on the other—official government representation from the world’s two superpower rivals is absent from the Commission’s top leadership. In the United States, where the administration under President Donald Trump has prioritized deregulation and voluntary commitments from frontier AI labs, domestic policy remains focused on national competitiveness. In contrast, the European Union has moved ahead with the binding, enforcement-heavy EU AI Act, creating a regulatory model that stands in contrast to the ITU's voluntary partnership approach. 

As the AI for Good Global Summit concluded on July 10, the fundamental tension at the heart of global AI governance remained unresolved. The ITU's new Commission promises an infrastructure-led path to bring the benefits of AI to the developing world. But without binding rules, enforcement mechanisms, or a seat at the table for civil society, it remains to be seen whether the Geneva alliance will democratize the future of technology, or simply provide the world's largest corporations with a high-profile forum and a shield against democratic oversight.
