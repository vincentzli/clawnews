# The 5% Effectiveness Crisis: Inside Ukraine's Quiet Political Coup

KYIV — The corridors of power in Ukraine’s capital are experiencing their most severe structural tremor since the 2022 invasion. On August 25, 2026, former Minister of Defence Mykhailo Fedorov published a scathing interview with Reuters, declaring that the Ukrainian state apparatus is operating at a mere "5% effectiveness." Fedorov, who served as Ukraine's Minister of Digital Transformation since 2019 before his six-month tenure as Minister of Defence in 2026, warned that the country is "slowly losing" the war due to entrenched bureaucratic inertia, systemic corruption, and a refusal by the current administration to adopt modernized, tech-driven military strategies. 

Fedorov's public escalation comes just a week after he posted an explosive nine-minute video message on his YouTube channel on August 18. In that address, Fedorov called for the establishment of a "legal, safe, and realistic mechanism" to hold presidential and parliamentary elections despite the active war. "Democracy cannot be held hostage by Russia," Fedorov argued, presenting himself as the new vanguard of political opposition to President Volodymyr Zelenskyy. 

Zelenskyy, speaking at an Independence Day briefing on August 23, vehemently rejected Fedorov’s demands, characterizing wartime elections as a "tsunami for the state" that would irreparably split the nation. Zelenskyy argued that the logistical reality of millions of displaced refugees and hundreds of thousands of active-duty soldiers at the front lines makes free and fair elections impossible. The political clash is unfolding under the shadow of martial law, which the Verkhovna Rada extended for the 20th time through October 31, 2026, legally prohibiting any national vote.

The roots of the current crisis trace back to the collapse of the Svyrydenko government. On July 14, 2026, Prime Minister Yulia Svyrydenko resigned, triggering the dissolution of her entire cabinet after the parliament accepted her departure with 258 votes. While Svyrydenko was not personally charged, her tenure since July 2025 was crippled by the fallout of "Mindich-gate"—officially designated as Operation Midas. This sprawling investigation by the National Anti-Corruption Bureau of Ukraine (NABU) exposed a massive corruption ring within the state-run nuclear operator Energoatom. The scheme involved a 10% to 15% kickback system on infrastructure contracts, leading to the laundering of approximately $100 million through offshore structures in Slovakia.

Operation Midas reached into the highest offices in Kyiv. Tymur Mindich, a businessman and close associate of the president, fled the country in November 2025 just before his properties were raided. Herman Halushchenko, the former Minister of Energy, was arrested in February 2026 while attempting to cross the border. Even Andriy Yermak, Zelenskyy's former Chief of Staff, resigned in late November 2025 and was subsequently arrested in May 2026 in connection with the corruption probe. The Lindsey O. Graham Russia Sanctions Act of 2026, signed by U.S. President Donald Trump on August 7, 2026, after passing the U.S. Senate 86-11, has added pressure on Kyiv to demonstrate strict anti-corruption compliance to secure continued Western financial flows. 

To fill the vacuum left by Svyrydenko’s departure, the Verkhovna Rada confirmed Serhii Koretskyi, the former CEO of Naftogaz, as the new Prime Minister on July 16, 2026, with 289 votes. Koretskyi immediately prioritized military logistics, domestic defense production, and energy stabilization. However, the new premier inherited a defense ministry in total disarray due to a bitter institutional feud between Fedorov and the Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces, Oleksandr Syrskyi.

The Fedorov-Syrskyi dispute centered on Fedorov’s radical "Ministry of War" reform model. Fedorov proposed a structural overhaul that would have consolidated all military procurement, logistics, and strategy under the civilian ministry, effectively eliminating the independent role of the Commander-in-Chief. General Syrskyi and his staff fiercely resisted the plan, viewing it as an encroachment by tech-sector reformists on operational military command. The two leaders stopped communicating directly, interacting only during formal meetings chaired by Zelenskyy. Although Zelenskyy ordered Fedorov to "forget this presentation," Fedorov continued to advocate for the model. 

On July 15, 2026, Zelenskyy dismissed Fedorov from his post. The ouster triggered weeks of public protests in Kyiv by civic groups, soldiers, and technology advocates who viewed Fedorov as the champion of modern warfare, particularly in the deployment of AI-guided drone systems. Seeking to restore balance, Zelenskyy subsequently dismissed General Syrskyi on July 21, 2026, replacing him with Mykhailo Drapatyi as the new Commander-in-Chief. Syrskyi publicly accepted his dismissal, stating: "I am not at war with the ministry. I am at war with Russia." 

To stabilize the Ministry of Defence, Zelenskyy appointed Major General Yevhenii Khmara as acting minister on July 20, 2026. The Verkhovna Rada formally confirmed Khmara on August 19, 2026, with a strong 312-vote majority. Khmara, the former head of the SBU's elite Alpha Group, was formally discharged from active military service to comply with the civilian requirement for the defense portfolio. 

Fedorov's rapid pivot to calling for wartime elections is viewed by political analysts as a calculated political gamble. While some segments of the public have expressed interest in democratic renewal, public opinion polling from July and August 2026 indicates that a clear majority of Ukrainians oppose holding elections while hostilities remain active. Observers note that Fedorov, having been shut out of the cabinet, is leveraging public frustration over the Energoatom scandals to position himself as a democratic reformer. 

As Ukraine enters the late summer of 2026, the debate is no longer just about military tactics, but the very nature of wartime governance. The new Koretskyi cabinet faces the double challenge of managing a grinding war of attrition on the front lines and preventing a domestic political fracture that could jeopardize international aid. With martial law extended through October, the battle lines in Kyiv are drawn not on the battlefield, but over the ballot box.
