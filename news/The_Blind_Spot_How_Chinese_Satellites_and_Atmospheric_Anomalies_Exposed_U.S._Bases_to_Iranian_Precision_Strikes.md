# The Blind Spot: How Chinese Satellites and Atmospheric Anomalies Exposed U.S. Bases to Iranian Precision Strikes

A bombshell investigation published today, May 6, 2026, by the Washington Post has fundamentally rewritten the public understanding of recent Iranian strikes on U.S. military installations across the Middle East. Utilizing a combination of commercial satellite imagery, leaked Pentagon damage assessments, and unclassified intelligence reports, the Post's deep dive reveals a chilling reality: U.S. air defense systems are being systematically bypassed, and the true scale of infrastructural damage and personnel injury has been deliberately minimized by the White House. The investigation focuses extensively on recent strikes targeting Camp Buehring in Kuwait and Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia. Previously, the Department of Defense characterized these incursions as largely unsuccessful or intercepted, resulting in minimal damage. However, the newly released satellite forensics paint a starkly different picture. At Prince Sultan Air Base, Iranian munitions struck with metre-level accuracy, obliterating early-warning radar arrays, specialized aircraft hangars, and a critical fuel depot. Despite the high value of these targets, multi-billion dollar Patriot missile batteries—long the cornerstone of U.S. force protection—failed to detect the incoming threats until it was too late.

How did a heavily fortified U.S. base suffer such precise strikes without triggering defensive interceptors? The Washington Post attributes this fatal blind spot to "radar ducting." In the unique meteorological environment of the Persian Gulf, sharp temperature inversions—where warm, dry desert air sits atop cooler water—create an atmospheric duct. This duct traps and bends radar waves along the curvature of the Earth, extending their range but simultaneously creating "shadow zones" or blind spots near the surface. During the March 2026 attack on Camp Buehring, an aging Iranian F-5 Kowsar jet flew directly through one of these ducting holes. Exploiting the radar shadow zone, the aircraft approached undetected, bombed the base's command center, and retreated before the Patriot batteries could achieve a lock. The system did not fail mechanically; it was blinded by physics.

But exploiting a blind spot requires knowing exactly where to strike, and this is where the Washington Post report highlights a paradigm shift in Iranian capabilities. The precision demonstrated at Prince Sultan Air Base was not the result of Iran's domestic Noor satellite network, which lacks high-resolution capabilities. Instead, Iran has integrated seamlessly into a foreign intelligence kill chain. According to the investigation, the IRGC has been heavily reliant on the TEE-01B, a Chinese-built spy satellite launched by Earth Eye Co., which provides 0.5-meter resolution imagery. 

This raw data is then processed using software from MizarVision, a Chinese firm specializing in AI-enhanced imagery analysis. The AI automatically identifies and tags U.S. military assets—distinguishing between dummy aircraft and active fighters, and pinpointing fuel depots and radar systems. Furthermore, the munitions were guided not by the U.S. GPS network, which is susceptible to localized jamming, but by China’s BeiDou-3 navigation system. Russian intelligence sharing provided the final piece of the puzzle, with Russian satellite ground stations allegedly transmitting post-strike damage assessments directly to Tehran. This robust, multi-national network effectively nullified U.S. electronic warfare countermeasures.
