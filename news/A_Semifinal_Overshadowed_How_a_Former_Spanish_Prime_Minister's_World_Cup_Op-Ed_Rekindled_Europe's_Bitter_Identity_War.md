# A Semifinal Overshadowed: How a Former Spanish Prime Minister's World Cup Op-Ed Rekindled Europe's Bitter Identity War

The heat in Arlington, Texas, is already sweltering as the grounds crew at AT&T Stadium prepares the pitch for Match 101, the highly anticipated 2026 FIFA World Cup semifinal between France and Spain. On paper, the Tuesday, July 14 fixture promises to be a tactical masterclass, pitting Spain’s youthful, electric wing play against France’s seasoned, clinical pragmatism. Yet, as both squads arrived in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex, the sporting narrative was completely derailed by a diplomatic firestorm originating from Madrid.

The spark was lit on July 10, 2026, when Mariano Rajoy, who served as Spain’s Prime Minister from 2011 to 2018, published a column in the conservative Spanish digital outlet *El Debate*. Titled "Hoy llegó el desquite" ("Today revenge has arrived"), the piece initially celebrated Spain’s quarterfinal triumph over Belgium before turning its attention to their semifinal opponents. Rajoy praised the French team’s formidable history, tactical organization, and high FIFA ranking. Then, he delivered the sentence that would ignite an international scandal: "Tiene, además, una plantilla de altísimo nivel. Eso sí, sin franceses." ("It also has a squad of the highest level. Mind you, without Frenchmen.")

The subtext of Rajoy’s remark was instantly clear to observers across Europe. It was a direct, nativist jab at the multicultural composition of the French national team, whose stars—including captain Kylian Mbappé—are predominantly Black or of North African descent. The reaction was swift and furious. On July 12, current Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez took to X to deliver a scathing public rebuke. "Hay quien todavía mide la pertenencia por el apellido, el lugar de nacimiento o el color de piel," Sánchez wrote. "Otros la medimos por el arraigo a un país y la voluntad de contribuir a él. España es de quien la ama y la trabaja. No de quien la avergüenza con declaraciones xenófobas." He concluded with a pointed message for the upcoming match: "Francia, nos vemos en semifinales. Que gane el mejor y que pierda el racismo."

Spain's Foreign Minister, José Manuel Albares, joined the chorus of condemnation, calling Rajoy’s remarks "hurtful and dangerous" and stating that "everything that conceals racism and xenophobia is despicable." In Paris, the backlash was equally intense. French Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez, who took office in October 2025 after serving as the Prefect of Police of Paris, appeared on BFMTV to brand the column "absolutely unacceptable" and "aberrant," declaring that it did not reflect the values of the French Republic. The French Embassy in Spain responded with a quiet but devastating fact-check: of the 26 players representing France at the 2026 World Cup, all 26 are French citizens, and 23 of them were born on French soil.

The controversy has exposed a gaping double standard, particularly given the makeup of Spain's own squad. The standout stars of Spain’s 2026 campaign have been Lamine Yamal, the brilliant teenager born in Catalonia to a Moroccan father and an Equatorial Guinean mother, and Nico Williams, born in Pamplona to Ghanaian parents who crossed the Sahara desert barefoot to reach Spain. On Reddit’s r/soccer, users quickly pointed out this irony. "Rajoy is stuck in the 1950s," one popular comment read. "If he applied that logic to Spain, he’d have to throw out the very players carrying his team to the semifinal." Another user noted: "It’s a dog whistle for *El Debate*’s ultra-conservative readers. They love Yamal when he scores, but they support Rajoy’s views on immigration."

Critics and political analysts argue that Rajoy's column is a calculated effort to appeal to Spain's conservative base. By injecting nativist rhetoric into a sporting event, the op-ed has energized the right-wing opposition while drawing fierce criticism from Sánchez's progressive coalition. This political exploitation of the national team is part of a broader, systemic trend. Only weeks earlier, after France defeated Paraguay 1-0 in the Round of 16, Paraguayan Senator Celeste Amarilla sparked an international incident by calling Kylian Mbappé a "colonized Cameroonian" on social media. The French Football Federation (FFF) took the unprecedented step of filing a criminal complaint with the Paris prosecutor's office for aggravated public insults and incitation to hatred, prompting a formal investigation that drew a formal repudiation from the Paraguayan Senate.

The debate over the French national team’s identity is almost as old as the modern squad itself. In 1996 and again in 2006, far-right politician Jean-Marie Le Pen infamously claimed the players did not know how to sing the national anthem and that the team was "artificial." When France won the World Cup in 1998, the "Black-Blanc-Beur" (Black, White, Arab) squad was hailed as a triumphant symbol of integration, only for that narrative to crumble a decade later during the disastrous 2010 tournament in South Africa. In 2018, when France won its second star, American television host Trevor Noah joked that "Africa won the World Cup," drawing a sharp, formal letter of protest from the French Ambassador to the United States, Gérard Araud, who insisted that "by calling them an African team, it seems you are denying their Frenchness."

Mbappé himself has spoken out frequently about this precarious dual identity. "When I win, I am French. When I lose, I am an immigrant," he famously remarked. As the teams prepare to walk out onto the pitch in Arlington, the match has transcended sport. It has become a high-stakes arena where Europe's unresolved tensions over race, migration, and national belonging are played out in front of a global audience of billions.
