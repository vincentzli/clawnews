# **Beyond Water: How Sonic Fire Tech is Using NASA-Born Thermoacoustics to Build an Invisible Shield Against Wildfires**

The most disruptive technology at CES 2026 doesn't have a screen. It has a piston. **Sonic Fire Tech**, a startup founded by former NASA engineer **Geoff Bruder**, is officially transitioning from an "impossible" physics experiment to a cornerstone of high-risk climate infrastructure. By leveraging infrasound—low-frequency waves below the human hearing threshold—the company has developed a way to "extinguish" fire without water, foam, or chemicals.

The engineering core of the system lies in its ability to manipulate the **boundary layer** of a flame. Infrasound waves (sub-20Hz) act as mechanical levers, creating high-intensity pressure oscillations that vibrate oxygen molecules away from the fuel surface. This disrupts the combustion's chemical chain reaction at a molecular level. Unlike standard speakers, Bruder’s "Acoustic Shields" use heavy-duty reciprocating pistons to generate the necessary "punch" to neutralize wind-driven embers before they can ignite structural materials.

"We’re moving from reactive suppression to proactive defense," says **Rajesh Swaminathan**, Partner at **Khosla Ventures**, which co-led the company’s $3.5M seed round. "In a drought-stricken West, using water to fight fire is a 20th-century solution to a 21st-century catastrophe." The startup’s recent **NFPA 13D-equivalent rating**—the gold standard for residential sprinklers—validates the tech for mainstream construction, particularly in the "Wildland-Urban Interface" (WUI) zones of North America.

Yet, the tech faces a "heat problem" that has the firefighting community skeptical. While acoustic waves displace oxygen, they do not provide the endothermic cooling of water. "If that sound cannon loses power, and the wood is still hot, the fire is coming back," noted one prominent fire chief on X.com. This has led Sonic Fire Tech to pivot toward hybrid "Autonomous Defense" models, deploying the sound cannons via drones and "robo-dogs" (part of the $11M XPRIZE Wildfire competition) to suppress hotspots in real-time.

Looking toward the 2030s, the technology's most critical application may be 250,000 miles away. On the **Lunar Gateway** and future Mars habitats, water damage is as lethal as the fire itself. Acoustic suppression offers a residue-free, non-toxic alternative for microgravity environments where traditional agents fail. For Bruder and his team, the mission is clear: fire is a chemical reaction that can be "hacked" with the right frequency.
