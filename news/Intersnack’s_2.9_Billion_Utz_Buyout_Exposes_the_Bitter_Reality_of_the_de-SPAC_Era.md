# Intersnack’s $2.9 Billion Utz Buyout Exposes the Bitter Reality of the de-SPAC Era

The public market experiment for one of America’s oldest family-controlled snack empires is coming to an abrupt, cash-out end. On July 21, 2026, shares of Utz Brands (NYSE: UTZ) surged 89% to close at $14.06 after Germany-based Intersnack Group announced a definitive agreement to take the Hanover, Pennsylvania-based potato chip manufacturer private. Under the terms of the deal, Intersnack will acquire all outstanding Class A shares for $14.25 per share in cash. The transaction, valued at approximately $2.9 billion including debt, represents a 91.3% premium over Utz's closing price of $7.45 on July 20, 2026. While the board has unanimously approved the deal, the buyout has triggered a fierce debate between value-seeking strategic buyers and a wounded class of retail investors who are being forced to realize steep losses.

The transaction highlights a growing trend in the mid-cap food sector, where strategic buyers are hunting for undervalued assets that went public during the blank-check boom of 2020 and 2021. Utz initially transitioned to the public markets in August 2020 through a business combination with Collier Creek Holdings, a special purpose acquisition company (SPAC) founded by former Pinnacle Foods executives. That transaction valued the snack maker at a $1.56 billion enterprise value. In the euphoric peak of the SPAC cycle, retail investors bid UTZ shares up to an all-time high of $30.09 on May 3, 2021. For these investors, the $14.25 buyout represents a forced liquidation at a more than 50% discount to their peak cost basis. 

"Another de-SPAC bite the dust," lamented one retail investor on r/stocks. "I’ve been bagholding this since it was $22, thinking a defensive consumer staple brand would protect me from inflation. Instead, we get bought out at a massive loss just as the brand starts turning around." Another user on r/wallstreetbets echoed the sentiment: "Forced liquidation at $14.25 is a slap in the face. The SPAC era was a transfer of wealth from retail to sponsors."

Yet, from the perspective of strategic buyers, Intersnack’s move is a masterclass in market arbitrage. Led by CEO Howard Friedman, Utz has grappled with the structural challenges of being a public mid-cap company in a high-interest-rate environment. The company has carried a heavy leverage profile, reporting a total gross debt of $854.0 million and cash of $73.7 million at the end of the first quarter on March 29, 2026. This resulted in a net debt position of $780.3 million. In a world where the Federal Reserve has maintained elevated benchmark rates, servicing this debt has severely limited Utz’s ability to fund regional brand expansion and capital expenditure.

Operational headwinds also plagued the company in the months leading up to the buyout. In May 2026, Utz was forced to initiate a voluntary recall of its Zapp’s and Dirty brand potato chips due to potential Salmonella contamination in a seasoning ingredient from a third-party supplier. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration subsequently upgraded this to a Class I recall in late June 2026, putting pressure on Utz’s adjusted gross profit margins and consumer trust, even though no illnesses were reported.

Despite these headwinds, Utz's underlying brand value remained strong. In fiscal year 2025, the company posted net sales of $1,438.8 million, a 2.1% increase year-over-year, and adjusted EBITDA of $216.5 million, up 8.1%. For the first quarter of 2026, net sales rose 2.6% to $361.3 million, while adjusted EBITDA grew 6.2% to $47.9 million. Trailing twelve-month adjusted EBITDA stood at $219.3 million. At a $2.9 billion enterprise value, Intersnack is paying an implied multiple of approximately 13.2x TTM adjusted EBITDA and 13.4x 2025 adjusted EBITDA.

Intersnack, led by CEO Maarten Leerdam, is capitalizing on these compressed public multiples. Intersnack operates across Europe, Australia, and Asia, but has lacked a direct operational foothold in the lucrative U.S. salty snack market, which is dominated by PepsiCo’s Frito-Lay. By taking Utz private, Intersnack avoids the quarterly earnings pressure of the New York Stock Exchange and can absorb Utz's debt onto a much larger, global balance sheet. 

Crucially, the deal is structured as a 50/50 partnership between Intersnack and the Rice and Lissette Family Entities, who are descendants of the founders William and Salie Utz. The founding family will retain half of the company, and former CEO Dylan Lissette will transition to the role of Executive Chair of the newly privatized company. This structure ensures that the regional heritage of the 105-year-old brand remains intact while gaining the financial backing of a European conglomerate.

Wall Street analysts have generally validated the transaction as a pragmatic exit. Jefferies downgraded Utz Brands from "Buy" to "Hold" following the announcement, but raised its price target to $14.25, writing that the buyout represents a "fair valuation" given the persistent category volume headwinds and the company's leverage. Securities firm Stephens & Co. similarly downgraded the stock to "Equal-Weight," signaling that a higher competing bid is highly unlikely given the family's control of the company's Class B voting shares.

For the broader market, the Utz privatization is a preview of the end-game for de-SPACs. Between 2020 and 2022, over 600 companies went public via SPACs. A significant portion have filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy or faced delisting. The healthy survivors, however, are increasingly being acquired by strategic buyers and private equity firms who are bypassing the traditional IPO route. These buyers recognize that while the public markets have lost appetite for small- and mid-cap consumer equities, the underlying brand loyalty, distribution networks, and cash flows of these businesses remain highly valuable when shielded from public market volatility. For retail investors, however, it remains a bitter pill: they took the early-stage risk of funding these public transitions, only to be forced out of the upside just as the assets are consolidated into private hands.
