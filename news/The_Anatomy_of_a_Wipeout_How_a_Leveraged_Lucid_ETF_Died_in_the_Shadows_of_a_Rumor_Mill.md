# The Anatomy of a Wipeout: How a Leveraged Lucid ETF Died in the Shadows of a Rumor Mill

On Tuesday, July 14, 2026, the GraniteShares 2x Long LCID Daily ETF (Nasdaq: LCDL) did something that its retail proponents on r/wallstreetbets believed was mathematically impossible. It went completely broke, its net asset value (NAV) cratering to -$0.0156 per share, on a day when its underlying asset, Lucid Group Inc. (LCID), finished the trading session down a modest 16.15%. More confounding still to the retail crowd was the aftermath: on Wednesday, July 15, Lucid shares staged a spectacular 28.79% recovery to close at $5.95. Yet, investors in LCDL were left holding worthless paper. The fund was dead, its assets liquidated, and its delisting from the NASDAQ exchange set in stone.

To understand how a leveraged instrument can be entirely wiped out by a temporary intraday panic requires a look into the plumbing of modern financial engineering and the mechanics of over-the-counter (OTC) swap agreements. LCDL was launched on April 21, 2025, during a boom in single-stock leveraged exchange-traded products. Designed as a short-term tactical trading vehicle, the ETF promised to deliver twice the daily performance of Lucid stock. For over a year, however, the fund suffered from the compounding erosion known as volatility decay, losing roughly 92% of its value from inception as Lucid’s stock fluctuated wildly without establishing a sustained upward trend. 

The coup de grâce came on July 14. Early that morning, the industry publication *EV* (electric-vehicles.com) published a report claiming that Lucid had hired the restructuring advisory firm AlixPartners to prepare a report for the board regarding strategic alternatives, specifically exploring a Chapter 11 bankruptcy filing or a take-private transaction. The rumors triggered a cascade of panic-selling in the pre-market and early trading hours. Lucid’s stock plummeted, triggering multiple volatility-induced trading halts on the NASDAQ. At its lowest point of the day, LCID touched $2.37, representing a catastrophic 57.0% plunge from Monday's closing price of $5.51.

For a 2x leveraged product, an intraday drop of 50% in the underlying stock represents a mathematical threshold of total destruction: a 100% loss of the fund’s net assets. When the stock dropped to $2.37, the daily loss stood at 114.0%. 

This is where the contractual realities of swap agreements superseded the hope of a daily reset. To achieve its leveraged exposure, GraniteShares entered into bilateral swap agreements with major investment bank counterparties. Under the terms of these agreements, if the underlying asset moves against the swap position by 50% or more intraday, the counterparty holds a contractually mandated right to immediately terminate the swap and close out the position. This clause exists to shield the bank from counterparty credit risk—ensuring that if the fund's equity goes below zero, the bank is not left holding the bag for losses it cannot recover from an insolvent ETF trust.

When LCID hit the $2.37 mark, the swap counterparties exercised their emergency close-out rights, liquidating the fund's entire LCID exposure at the absolute bottom of the market. This execution locked in a terminal loss. Because the liquidation occurred during a period of extreme market dislocation and wide bid-ask spreads, the cash realized was insufficient to cover the fund’s accrued liabilities, dragging the NAV into negative territory at -$0.0156.

While the liquidation engine was grinding LCDL to dust, Lucid's management scrambled to contain the fire. In the afternoon, Lucid's CEO Silvio Napoli issued an emphatic denial of the bankruptcy rumors. Napoli clarified that while the firm had indeed hired AlixPartners, the mandate was strictly confined to improving operational efficiency and execution, and that no board committee had been formed to explore a take-private deal. Lucid’s communications chief, Nick Twork, went on the record stating that the electric vehicle maker possessed sufficient liquidity to fund operations well into the next year.

The denial worked. Buyers flooded back into LCID, dragging the stock from its $2.37 low to a close of $4.62 on Tuesday, before surging another 28.79% on Wednesday to close at $5.95. Over the forty-eight-hour period, Lucid stock actually rose by 7.98% from its Monday baseline. 

For retail traders holding the underlying stock, the episode was a stressful but ultimately profitable roller-coaster. For holders of LCDL, it was a terminal event. Because the swap contracts had been terminated and the positions liquidated at the low of $2.37, the ETF held no assets to capture the afternoon recovery or Wednesday’s surge. It was an empty corporate shell.

Yet, in a bizarre twist that has ignited fury on retail message boards, LCDL did not immediately stop trading. Due to administrative delays in exchange halting procedures, the ETF continued to trade on the secondary market for several hours on Tuesday after its NAV had already been wiped out. Retail day traders, seeing Lucid stock rebound and unaware that the underlying swaps were gone, bought the dip in LCDL. The fund closed Tuesday at a market price of $0.2876, representing a massive premium to a net asset value that was literally less than zero.

"I bought the LCDL dip at $0.28 on Tuesday thinking Silvio's denial would send it back to the moon. Now they're telling me the NAV is negative and my shares are worth zero? How is this even legal?" wrote one user on r/wallstreetbets. The anger has spread internationally. In South Korea, where retail day traders—locally known as "seo-hak-gaemi" or "Western ants"—have become dominant players in high-beta U.S. leveraged products, forums are filled with plans to organize class-action lawsuits against GraniteShares and the NASDAQ exchange for failing to halt trading immediately. 

The liquidation of LCDL, alongside GraniteShares' closure of other niche leveraged products like BULX, ETRL, and MSDD in June 2026, highlights the growing regulatory and structural scrutiny facing single-stock leveraged ETFs. Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent have previously warned about the hidden structural leverage in non-bank financial institutions. While these products are marketed as tactical, single-day trading tools, they are frequently treated by retail investors as buy-and-hold lottery tickets. The death of LCDL is a harsh reminder that when volatility turns extreme, the math of leveraged decay is the least of an investor's worries; the real threat is the hard-wired self-preservation of the Wall Street banks providing the leverage.
