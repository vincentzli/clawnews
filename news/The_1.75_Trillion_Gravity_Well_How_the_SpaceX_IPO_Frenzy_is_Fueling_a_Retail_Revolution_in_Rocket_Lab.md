# The $1.75 Trillion Gravity Well: How the SpaceX IPO Frenzy is Fueling a Retail Revolution in Rocket Lab

On the 43rd floor of a Midtown brokerage, the screens are glowing with a number that feels less like a valuation and more like a coordinate in deep space: $1.75 trillion. As of Wednesday, May 20, 2026, the rumors that have electrified the "Fintwit" and Reddit communities for months have finally coalesced into the cold reality of an S-1 filing. SpaceX is going public. Under the ticker SPCX, Elon Musk’s behemoth is set to debut on the Nasdaq on June 12, targeting a capital raise of $75 billion—a figure that would dwarf Saudi Aramco’s 2019 record of $29.4 billion.

But while the institutional whales sharpen their harpoons for the SPCX debut, the retail market is already playing a different game. In the digital trenches of r/WallStreetBets and across the fragmented landscape of social media, the focus has shifted to a "proxy play" that is distorting the traditional laws of market physics. That play is Rocket Lab USA, Inc. (RKLB).

The logic driving retail behavior is simple, if not aggressive: if SpaceX is the untouchable sovereign of the private space sector, Rocket Lab is its most credible public alternative. As the SpaceX IPO draws closer, capital is hemorrhaging out of traditional tech and into what traders call the "Space Stack"—a basket of stocks where RKLB is the crown jewel. Over the last 48 hours, RKLB has seen its trading volume surge to four times its 30-day average, with prices touching the $130 mark—a staggering ascent from its single-digit lows of early 2024.

"Most people think SpaceX's $1.75 trillion valuation is about rockets, but the real engine driving the largest IPO in history is Starlink," noted one prominent Fintwit analyst this morning. The sentiment is echoed by retail high-conviction traders who argue that "RKLB is the only public play with that kind of vertical integration potential." 

The data supports the narrative of a maturing industry. In its Q1 2026 disclosures, SpaceX (now consolidated with Musk’s xAI following a February merger) reported revenue of $4.694 billion. Of that, $3.257 billion—nearly 70%—was attributed to the Starlink division, which now boasts over 10 million global subscribers. This "platform economics" model is exactly what RKLB is attempting to replicate. While Peter Beck’s company was once viewed purely as a launch provider, its Space Systems division—building satellites and components for the likes of NASA and the Space Development Agency—now accounts for over 75% of its $2.2 billion backlog.

The tension in the market, however, lies in the "Neutron" factor. For Rocket Lab to justify a valuation that has swelled to nearly $65 billion, it must prove it can break the Falcon 9’s monopoly on medium-lift launch. Neutron, Rocket Lab’s reusable carbon-fiber rocket, is currently undergoing final integration at Launch Complex 3 in Virginia. The Archimedes engines, the "long pole" in the development cycle, successfully completed their hot-fire campaign at Stennis Space Center earlier this spring. 

Yet, as the SPCX filing becomes official, a contrarian "sell the news" sentiment is beginning to fester. "The biggest risk is 'buy the rumor, sell the news,'" warned one widely shared post on StockTwits today. "Once the SpaceX ticker actually hits the Nasdaq, the retail rotation out of RKLB and into the 'real thing' could be a bloodbath."

The political backdrop adds another layer of complexity. Under the Trump-Vance administration, the regulatory path for SpaceX has been smoothed significantly, with Musk frequently seen as a shadow advisor on government efficiency and space policy. For Rocket Lab and other competitors like AST SpaceMobile (ASTS), the fear is a "winner-take-all" regulatory environment where federal contracts gravitate toward the Musk orbit. Conversely, the retail bull case suggests that the Department of Defense’s stated goal of maintaining "dissimilar redundancy" ensures that Rocket Lab will remain a critical, well-funded second pillar of U.S. space infrastructure.

The macro environment is equally fraught. Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell’s second term is expiring this month, creating a vacuum of certainty just as the largest IPO in history hits the tapes. With interest rates hovering at 4.25% and the administration pushing for aggressive deregulation, the cost of capital for capital-intensive firms like Rocket Lab has become the primary debate among economists.

Financially, the gap between the two companies remains cavernous. SpaceX’s $1.75 trillion target implies a price-to-sales multiple of roughly 35x based on projected 2026 revenues of $50 billion. RKLB, at $130 per share, is trading at a multiple closer to 70x its trailing twelve-month revenue—a "SpaceX premium" that retail investors seem more than willing to pay. 

"The 'rising tide' won't just lift boats, it'll launch them," one Reddit user posted, capturing the euphoric mood of a community that believes they are front-running the institutional re-rating of the entire sector. But as the June 12 deadline approaches, the question remains: is the retail crowd leading the market into a new era of orbital infrastructure, or are they simply fueling a $1.75 trillion gravity well that will eventually swallow their gains?
