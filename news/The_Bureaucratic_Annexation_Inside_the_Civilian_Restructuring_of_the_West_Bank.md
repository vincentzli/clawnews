# The Bureaucratic Annexation: Inside the Civilian Restructuring of the West Bank

In a closed-door gathering of political allies near the West Bank settlement of Revava, Israeli Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich laid bare an administrative blueprint designed to alter the core governance of the occupied territories. “I tell you that it’s mega-dramatic,” Smotrich said in an audio recording obtained and verified by investigative reporters. “Such changes change a system’s DNA.” Smotrich was not describing a military conquest or a prime ministerial decree proclaiming formal sovereignty over Judea and Samaria. Instead, he was detailing a systemic administrative overhaul: stripping the Israel Defense Forces’ Central Command of civil governance over Area C and transplanting statutory authority directly into Israeli civilian ministries. The stated rationale, Smotrich told his audience, was diplomatic shielding: to construct a parallel civilian apparatus so that “it would be easier to swallow in the international and legal context, so that they wouldn’t say that we are now doing annexation.”

By late September 2026, that architecture is no longer theoretical; it has become an entrenched administrative reality. Through a coordinated sequence of cabinet decisions, statutory revisions, and institutional realignments executed over the past eight months, the Israeli government has steadily displaced the military occupation framework that governed the 60 percent of the West Bank designated as Area C under the 1995 Oslo II Accord. What international diplomats long warned would trigger an immediate geopolitical crisis has arrived not via public annexation proclamations, but through the dry, procedural mechanics of ministerial regulations, land registries, and housing tenders.

The structural transformation accelerated in February 2026, when the Israeli security cabinet enacted a series of regulatory overhauls across Area C. Operating through the Settlements Administration (Minhelet HaHityashvut)—a civilian body Smotrich established within the Ministry of Defense, directed by civilian appointee Hillel Roth—the government formally initiated a comprehensive "settlement of land title" (land registration) procedure. Backed by a treasury allocation of NIS 244 million covering the 2026–2030 period, the process allows the state to systematically adjudicate land ownership in Area C. Under existing administrative mechanisms, undocumented plots where Palestinian ownership cannot be established through Ottoman, British Mandate, or Jordanian records are designated as state land and made eligible for state-backed development. This policy was paired with the security cabinet’s repeal of Jordanian Law No. 40 of 1953, a statute that historically restricted land sales in the territory to Jordanian and Arab nationals, thereby clearing the way for Israeli citizens and corporate entities to acquire West Bank land directly under domestic Israeli contract law.

At the same time, planning, municipal zoning, and infrastructure standards in Israeli settlements were aligned directly with domestic Israeli line ministries. For the more than 500,000 Israeli settlers residing in the West Bank, statutory building regulations, environmental standards, and water governance are now managed through civilian administrative channels rather than the military chain of command.

This bifurcated governance model deepened further in August 2026, when Defense Minister Israel Katz instructed the IDF to draft a comprehensive plan to transfer responsibility for civilian law enforcement regarding Israeli settlers from the military to the Israel Police. The announcement followed a week-long standoff and settler siege outside the Palestinian village of Qusra. Defending the shift, Katz argued that the military must concentrate on core security missions and countering Palestinian armed groups, stating that IDF soldiers should not be deployed to “chase after boys on the hills.” 

Katz’s announcement ignited sharp public debate on platforms like X and Reddit, where legal analysts and regional observers underscored the escalating disparity in governance. Critics pointed out that while Palestinians in Area C remain subject to martial law, military courts, and indefinite administrative detention, Jewish settlers in the same territory are treated under civilian domestic law—particularly after Katz ended the use of administrative detention against Jewish suspects in late 2024 while maintaining and expanding its application against Palestinians. Online discussions among legal monitors on X emphasized that delegating settler policing to the civilian Israel Police—which falls under the authority of National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir—effectively codifies a dual-legal regime. Simultaneously, senior police officials pushed back in the Israeli press, pointing out that civilian police cannot realistically operate in volatile sectors of the West Bank without military escorts and combat support.

On the ground, this regulatory restructuring has been matched by the physical fragmentation of the territory. On August 18, 2026, the Ministry of Construction and Housing published tenders for 1,234 housing units in the strategic E1 corridor, advancing components of the 3,401-unit master plan (Tender No. 460/2025) that connects East Jerusalem to the settlement of Ma’ale Adumim. E1 encompasses roughly 12 square kilometers of rugged terrain east of Jerusalem. For decades, successive United States administrations treated construction in E1 as a non-negotiable red line because building an urban corridor there severs the West Bank into disconnected northern and southern zones—separating Ramallah and Nablus from Bethlehem and Hebron—while cutting East Jerusalem off from its Palestinian hinterland.

Israeli rights groups, including Peace Now and Ir Amim, lodged urgent petitions with the Jerusalem District Court seeking an injunction against the tenders. Peace Now warned that the government was moving swiftly to finalize commercial contracts with private developers ahead of Israel’s October 2026 Knesset elections, cautioning that once construction contracts are signed, reversing the development becomes legally and financially prohibitive.

The confluence of these moves ignited a diplomatic storm this week at the 81st United Nations General Assembly in New York, as world leaders concluded the annual General Debate on September 26, 2026. Addressing a high-level ministerial event on the sidelines of the assembly, UN Secretary-General António Guterres delivered a blunt appraisal of the situation. Warning that international backing for a two-state solution must “go beyond words,” Guterres stated that the territorial and demographic prerequisites for an independent, contiguous Palestinian state are being “systematically erased.” Moving forward with construction in the E1 corridor, Guterres warned, would deliver an irreversible “deathblow” to the two-state framework, adding that settlement expansion and displacement are actively raising the “spectre of ethnic cleansing.”

In Brussels, European Union diplomats have initiated preliminary discussions on potential trade and diplomatic responses. European officials are weighing scrutiny under Article 2 of the EU-Israel Association Agreement, which links trade preferences to human rights standards, alongside proposals for targeted sanctions against entities involved in E1 infrastructure projects.

The administrative shift has also provoked serious pushback from within Israel's security establishment. Commanders for Israel’s Security (CIS), a network of over 500 retired generals and former chiefs of the Mossad, Shin Bet, and police, has publicly opposed the civilianization of West Bank authority. Maj. Gen. (ret.) Gadi Shamni, a former commander of the IDF Central Command, warned that Smotrich’s administrative restructuring has "neutered" military authority in the field. Shamni argued that removing civilian oversight from the military has effectively “tied the IDF Central Command’s hand behind its back,” depriving operational commanders of the holistic authority needed to maintain stability and avert broad regional escalation.

As international scrutiny mounts at the United Nations, the systematic restructuring of Area C demonstrates how structural facts on the ground can supersede diplomatic frameworks. Through statutory planning, selective title registration, and civilian administrative transfers, the boundaries separating sovereign Israel from the West Bank have been methodically transformed, presenting the world with an entrenched civilianized reality.
