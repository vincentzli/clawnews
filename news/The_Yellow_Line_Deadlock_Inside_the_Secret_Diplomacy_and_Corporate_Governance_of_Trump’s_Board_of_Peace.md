# The Yellow Line Deadlock: Inside the Secret Diplomacy and Corporate Governance of Trump’s Board of Peace

The mid-August heat in Cairo did little to ease the diplomatic friction as U.S. envoy Jared Kushner and a delegation from the "Board of Peace"—including former British Prime Minister Tony Blair and Bulgarian diplomat Nickolay Mladenov—sat down with a Hamas delegation led by Khalil al-Hayya. The meetings, aimed at salvaging a fragile ceasefire signed in October 2025, centered on a U.S.-backed 15-point roadmap unveiled on July 31, 2026. The plan outlines a transitional framework where Hamas would disarm and transfer governing authority to the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), a 15-member Palestinian technocratic body. While Hamas agreed in principle to the roadmap, their delegation demanded that the Board of Peace compel Israel to withdraw its forces as an absolute prerequisite. Days later, Kushner traveled to Jerusalem to meet with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. The response there was a flat rejection of the roadmap's core sequencing. Netanyahu declared that the Israel Defense Forces (IDF), which currently control between 60% and 70% of Gaza, will not withdraw a single soldier until Hamas is fully and verifiably disarmed under the supervision of a U.S. general.

This diplomatic deadlock exposes the widening gap between the geopolitical ambitions of U.S. President Donald J. Trump and the stark realities on the ground in Gaza. At the heart of the administration's strategy is the "Board of Peace" (BoP), an international organization established by Trump in late 2025 and launched in January 2026 under the auspices of UN Security Council Resolution 2803. But the BoP is not a traditional diplomatic body. Its charter, which names Trump as its Chairman for life, has sparked intense international and domestic controversy. The charter requires any nation wishing to secure permanent membership to contribute $1 billion to a fund controlled by the board. This financial structure has drawn sharp criticism, with congressional Democrats and online commentators labeling the enterprise a "slush fund" and a "pay-to-play grift." In Washington, lawmakers have introduced the "PEACE Act" to block unauthorized federal funding for the organization, while on forums like Reddit’s r/politics, critics argue that the charter’s extensive privileges and immunities shield it from standard oversight. As one prominent user summarized, the board is "a multi-billion dollar private club masquerading as a diplomatic foundation, operating completely outside the checks and balances of the United States government."

While the Board of Peace attempts to manage reconstruction funds and governance from its international offices, the physical reality of Gaza is defined by the "Yellow Line." Established during the October 2025 ceasefire, this tactical boundary was designed to separate Israeli-controlled sectors from Palestinian-administered zones. Originally, the agreement left approximately 53% of the Gaza Strip under Israeli military control, with the expectation of a phased withdrawal. Instead, the IDF has steadily moved the yellow-painted concrete blocks and earthen barriers westward. By mid-2026, UN and humanitarian monitors reported that the Israeli military zone had expanded to encompass between 64% and 70% of the territory, pushing Gaza’s civilian population into an increasingly congested strip along the Mediterranean coast.

This creeping expansion has turned the Yellow Line into a lethal boundary. Local health officials and monitoring groups report near-daily skirmishes, mortar exchanges, and localized IDF operations targeting remaining tunnel infrastructure. Though Netanyahu reportedly ordered a temporary halt to the westward movement of the line in mid-August following pressure from Kushner, the military operations have not ceased. The risk of a total collapse of the ceasefire grows daily, which analysts warn would plunge the region back into full-scale conflict and render the transition plans obsolete.

The proposed transition vehicle, the NCAG, remains paralyzed by the standoff. Led by Chief Commissioner Ali Shaath, the committee of 15 Palestinian technocrats was designed to assume civilian administration and public services under the joint framework established by Resolution 2803. In July 2026, Hamas announced the dissolution of its own "Government Emergency Committee" to demonstrate its willingness to hand over power. However, the NCAG has been unable to deploy effectively inside Gaza. Shaath and his commissioners have repeatedly been blocked from entering the territory due to Israeli security restrictions, leaving the municipal administration in a vacuum.

Netanyahu's refusal to accept the 15-point roadmap reflects severe domestic political pressures. His coalition partners, including National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich, have threatened to bring down the government if the IDF withdraws from Gaza before achieving "total victory"—defined as the complete dismantling of Hamas's civil and military wings. Netanyahu’s insistence that Hamas surrender both heavy and light weapons under the supervision of a U.S. general is a condition that Hamas’s leadership, operating under intense internal pressure from its military wing, views as a demand for unconditional surrender. While Kushner’s mid-August meetings resulted in an agreement to establish two joint working groups on disarmament and demilitarization, these groups face an intractable sequencing dilemma: Israel refuses to pull back without total disarmament, and Hamas refuses to disarm while Israeli troops occupy up to 70% of Gaza.

Meanwhile, the financial foundation of the Board of Peace remains highly unstable. While Gulf nations have engaged in talks, many Western allies have balked at the $1 billion entry fee, refusing to participate in an organization where Trump holds lifetime unilateral authority to rewrite the charter and direct funds. In June 2026, the administration attempted to reclassify the BoP from a public international organization to an international non-governmental organization (INGO), a maneuver legal experts at Just Security described as an attempt to bypass congressional oversight and shield the board's finances from public disclosure. Although the World Bank was designated to administer the board's reconstruction fund, reports indicate that the official accounts have remained largely unspent, even as private banking channels, including JPMorgan, have been scrutinized for handling parallel board funds. The legislative pushback from former President Joe Biden's administration in early 2025 had sought to preemptively restrict such structures, but the current executive branch has proceeded regardless.

For the residents of Gaza, the diplomatic impasse means that the ceasefire is a peace in name only. As long as the Board of Peace remains stalled by its own controversial structure and the IDF continues to fortify the Yellow Line, the prospect of a stable post-conflict governance remains a distant diplomatic mirage. The working groups established in Jerusalem have yet to meet, and along the yellow concrete barriers of the demarcation line, the sound of gunfire is a constant reminder of how close the ceasefire is to breaking.
