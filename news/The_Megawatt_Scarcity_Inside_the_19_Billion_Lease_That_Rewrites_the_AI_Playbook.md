# The Megawatt Scarcity: Inside the $19 Billion Lease That Rewrites the AI Playbook

In Hawesville, Kentucky, the massive smokestacks of the former Century Aluminum smelter have sat cold for years, a rusting monument to the decline of American heavy industry. But the high-voltage transmission lines terminating at the site’s dedicated substation are suddenly humming with a different kind of energy. On July 6, 2026, TeraWulf Inc. (NASDAQ: WULF) announced that it had signed a landmark 20-year lease agreement with AI startup Anthropic to transform this industrial relic—which TeraWulf acquired earlier in 2026—into the 401-megawatt "Justified Data" campus. The agreement is projected to generate $19 billion in contracted revenue over its term, making it one of the largest single infrastructure commitments in the history of the artificial intelligence boom. Yet, as the initial euphoria fades, the deal is exposing the raw structural tensions of a market that is rapidly shifting its focus from silicon chips to the electrical grid.

The financial scale of the agreement is staggering, but so are the execution challenges. The 401 megawatts (MW) of critical IT load designed for the Hawesville campus will not come online overnight; TeraWulf expects the first phase to be operational in the second half of 2027, with the full capacity slated for early 2028. To fund the massive development costs, TeraWulf simultaneously announced the sale of its 50.1% interest in the Abernathy Joint Venture—a 168 MW site in Texas—to partner Fluidstack for $530 million. But building out 401 MW of state-of-the-art AI data center capacity typically costs between $8 million and $10 million per megawatt, implying a total capital expenditure of $3.2 billion to $4.0 billion. The cash from the Abernathy sale, paid in installments through April 2027, leaves a massive multi-billion-dollar shortfall that TeraWulf must plug through debt or equity markets.

This capital overhang immediately cooled Wall Street’s initial enthusiasm. WULF shares closed at $22.21 on July 6, giving the company a market capitalization of approximately $11.0 billion. But by the close of trading on July 7, the stock had tumbled 8.87% to $20.24, erasing nearly a billion dollars in market value. The pullback reflected a classic "sell the news" dynamic, compounded by retail anxiety over potential shareholder dilution. On the popular Reddit forum r/stocks, one user summarized the skepticism: "Everyone is looking at the $19 billion headline, but nobody is asking how WULF is going to finance the capex without diluting shareholders into oblivion. The cash from Fluidstack is a drop in the ocean."

The scramble for power has also ignited intense speculation across the sector. Shares of Sydney-based IREN Limited (NASDAQ: IREN) surged 13.11% on July 6, closing at $43.91, following reports that the company is on a shortlist for a confidential tender by Anthropic. The AI startup, led by CEO Dario Amodei, is reportedly seeking to secure a massive 1.4 gigawatts (GW) of data center capacity in Australia, a project estimated to be worth between $12 billion and $15 billion. However, like TeraWulf, IREN saw its gains pared the following day, closing at $41.01 on July 7 as investors digested the sheer scale of the capital expenditures required to build out gigawatt-scale infrastructure.

The core tension of the AI trade in 2026 is no longer about who can buy the most graphics processing units (GPUs) from NVIDIA. It is about who can plug them in. Over the past year, the market has realized that the ultimate bottleneck for frontier LLMs is the availability of baseline electricity. Because the development of new power generation and high-voltage transmission lines is gridlocked by regulatory delays, existing industrial sites with pre-arranged grid interconnections have become the most valuable real estate in the digital economy. Co-CEOs Daniel and William Roberts of IREN and Paul Prager of TeraWulf have spent years accumulating these power assets, originally for Bitcoin mining. Now, they are leasing them to AI developers at premiums that would have been unimaginable two years ago. 

This transition is occurring against a complex macroeconomic backdrop. The Trump administration, with Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, has prioritized energy deregulation and grid modernization. Yet, the physical reality of grid capacity cannot be solved by executive order. The Federal Reserve, under Chairman Kevin Warsh, has kept a close eye on the capital expenditure boom in AI infrastructure, which threatens to keep baseline inflation sticky as demand for copper, steel, and electrical equipment surges. 

Furthermore, the credit risk of these multi-decade contracts remains an open question. Analysts at several Wall Street firms pointed out that the credit support for Anthropic’s lease with TeraWulf remains pending. While Anthropic is backed by billions from Google and Amazon, it remains a privately held startup that is burning massive amounts of cash to train its models. If the commercial viability of generative AI stalls before the Hawesville campus reaches full capacity in 2028, TeraWulf could be left holding a partially constructed, highly specialized facility with a bankrupt tenant. For now, the market is pricing in a future where power is the new oil, but the road to bringing that power online is paved with immense capital risk.
