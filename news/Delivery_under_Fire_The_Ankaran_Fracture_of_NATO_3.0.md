# Delivery under Fire: The Ankaran Fracture of NATO 3.0

As the Turkish sun beat down on the Beştepe Presidential Complex in Ankara on July 7, 2026, the diplomatic choreography of the 36th NATO Summit was swiftly overtaken by the friction of unilateral politics. Secretary General Mark Rutte opened the proceedings with a call for "delivery" and "NATO 3.0"—a framework designed to translate the ambitious defense targets agreed upon at the 2025 Hague Summit into deployable military capabilities. That agreement mandated that allies reach a combined 5 percent GDP spending target for defense and security infrastructure by 2035, divided between a 3.5 percent core military requirement and a 1.5 percent allotment for broader security and resilience. Yet, even as Rutte attempted to showcase a united front, the proceedings were overshadowed by a digital blitz from Washington.

U.S. President Donald Trump, arriving in Ankara amid heightened security and a ban on public demonstrations, took to Truth Social to lambast what he characterized as a "one-sided" and "ridiculous" financial arrangement. Sharing charts illustrating the disparity in spending, Trump highlighted the United States' estimated $999 billion defense budget compared to the United States' closest allies: the United Kingdom at $90.5 billion, France at $66.5 billion, Italy at $48.8 billion, and Poland at $44.3 billion. Although analysts have long pointed out that these figures represent total national defense budgets rather than direct contributions to NATO's common administrative funding, the political message was clear. Trump's frustration was not merely about budget spreadsheets; it was rooted in the ongoing military conflict in Iran, which commenced on February 28, 2026.

Following U.S. and Israeli airstrikes on February 28, 2026, which killed several high-ranking Iranian officials including Supreme Leader Ayatollah Ali Khamenei, the region has been engulfed in warfare. While a temporary ceasefire was brokered in April and a Memorandum of Understanding signed in June, the conflict flared up again on July 7, 2026. The U.S. military launched a series of powerful airstrikes against Iranian coastal surveillance, air defenses, and port facilities in response to attacks on three commercial vessels in the Strait of Hormuz. European allies, however, have largely demurred. Several key members restricted the U.S. military from using European airbases for offensive operations against Iran, infuriating the American executive. On July 2, Trump posted on Truth Social: "They were not there for us!!!"

European leaders have scrambled to counter this narrative of non-reciprocity. At the NATO Summit Defence Industry Forum in Ankara, allies announced several multi-billion-dollar joint procurement projects. Among the most significant was a coalition of 11 allies—including Belgium, Canada, Germany, and Sweden—to acquire up to 10 Saab GlobalEye aircraft. The Swedish-made platform, built on the Bombardier Global 6500 business jet and equipped with Erieye Extended Range radar, is intended to replace the alliance's aging E-3 Sentry fleet. This decision came after plans to acquire the Boeing E-7 Wedgetail were cancelled, marking a victory for European defense industrial integration. Additionally, seven allies, including France, Spain, and the UK, launched a multinational airlift project centered on the Airbus A400M, mimicking the MRTT tanker model. Furthermore, a coalition of the US, Germany, Poland, the Netherlands, and Sweden signed an agreement to explore the establishment of a European PAC-3 Missile Maintenance Facility to service PAC-3 MSE and CRI interceptors.

Yet the diplomatic friction was not limited to procurement. On July 5, Trump shared a photo of himself and Italian Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni with the caption, "Restraining order needed." The post reignited a feud that began at the June 2026 G7 Summit in France, where Trump claimed Meloni had "begged" for a photo, prompting Meloni to retort, "I and Italy never beg." In Ankara, Italian Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani sought to downplay the post, stating that transatlantic relations "go far beyond individual statements," while Defense Minister Guido Crosetto noted that "people come and go, but relationships must remain." Underneath the polite public dismissals, however, Italian opposition figures criticized Trump's behavior as outright bullying.

Amid the transatlantic tension, Turkish President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan seized the opportunity to advance Ankara's strategic objectives. Following a bilateral meeting at the Beştepe complex, Trump announced his intention to lift U.S. sanctions on Turkey, calling it "time" to do so, and added that re-admitting Turkey to the F-35 fighter jet program—from which it was expelled in 2019 after purchasing Russian S-400 systems—was "something certainly we'd consider." Erdoğan expressed confidence in a "favorable decision," recalling commitments for five F-35 aircraft and remarking that "Mr. Trump always stands by his word." The announcement immediately sent ripples through the Eastern Mediterranean, drawing sharp concern from Greece and explicit opposition from Israel, both of whom fear a shift in the regional balance of power. Trump's unilateral pronouncements in Ankara have laid bare the central paradox of NATO 3.0: Europe is being pushed to integrate its defense to satisfy American demands, yet the United States remains willing to bypass collective allied consensus to reward bilateral partners. This dynamic has amplified debates over the long-term reliability of U.S. security guarantees, accelerating European efforts to build autonomous defense capabilities.
