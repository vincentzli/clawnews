# The Hermon Impasse: How Israel’s Forward Buffer Zone Stalled Washington’s Post-Assad Normalization Push

Across the volcanic plains of the Quneitra countryside and along the sub-alpine ridges of Mount Hermon, the regional security order that held the Levant in uneasy balance for half a century has collapsed. In its place stands an entrenched military frontier—and a stalled diplomatic offensive. In the autumn of 2026, security and normalization discussions between Israel and the post-Assad transitional authority in Damascus have frozen, frustrating a sustained attempt by the United States to draw post-Ba'athist Syria into an expanded Abraham Accords framework.

Following the collapse of the Bashar al-Assad regime in December 2024, the second Trump administration sought to capitalize on the expulsion of Iranian assets and the weakening of Lebanese Hezbollah. U.S. Special Envoy to the Middle East Steve Witkoff and Secretary of State Marco Rubio initiated direct security dialogues, coordinating backchannel meetings in Baku, Azerbaijan, and establishing working groups in Amman, Jordan. The diplomatic objective was ambitious: parlay sanctions dismantling and Western reconstruction capital into a bilateral non-aggression pact, laying the groundwork for eventual diplomatic normalization.

That diplomatic initiative has foundered against physical fortifications on the ground. At the root of the breakdown is Israel’s tactical posture along the 1974 Separation of Forces Agreement lines. In December 2024, as rebel forces entered Damascus, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz announced that the 1974 accord was null and void, asserting that the dissolution of the Syrian Arab Army left a dangerous security vacuum along the Golan frontier.

Israeli forces promptly pushed beyond the 1974 "Alpha Line," occupying the United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF) demilitarized Area of Separation and establishing forward positions past the eastern "Bravo Line." What was initially designated as a temporary cordon has hardened into permanent military infrastructure. According to spatial monitoring and reporting by the Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED), the Israel Defense Forces have established at least nine fortified bases and outposts extending from Mount Hermon through Quneitra into western Daraa. Israeli engineering units have built a paved strategic military highway across agricultural terrain, reinforced with fortified checkpoints, telecommunications relays, and helicopter pads. ACLED documented 84 separate Israeli ground incursions into Quneitra and Daraa in May 2026 alone.

Israeli defense planners view the retention of these forward positions as an existential imperative rather than a negotiable asset. Perched atop the Syrian summit of Mount Hermon (Jabal al-Sheikh) at an elevation exceeding 2,800 meters, IDF signal intelligence and radar arrays maintain direct line-of-sight surveillance over Damascus, located just 40 kilometers to the northeast. Following the systemic intelligence failures of October 7, 2023, Israeli security officials argue that topographical depth cannot be conceded to a transitional regime led by former militant figures.

Defense Minister Israel Katz underscored this posture during an operational visit to Israeli units on the Syrian side of Mount Hermon on August 25, 2026. Speaking directly to Damascus, Katz said: "When you wake up in the morning in the palace in Damascus and look up at Mount Hermon and see the IDF, you know that we are here to defend our communities and our border." Katz emphasized that the Israeli military would maintain its presence along the high ground and the southern Syrian buffer zone indefinitely to protect the Golan Heights and Galilee from potential militant infiltration.

In Damascus, that visual reality has foreclosed the possibility of compromise. Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa, who assumed the presidency in January 2025 and centralized authority by abolishing the prime minister’s office in March 2025, leads a fragile transitional cabinet balancing domestic nationalist expectations and hardline factions. Acceding to Israeli military bases within sovereign territory would destabilize his administration from within.

During indirect sessions in Baku and security coordination meetings in Amman, Syrian envoys consistently rejected any political normalization or incorporation into the Abraham Accords. Damascus has maintained that an Israeli withdrawal to the 1974 disengagement lines and the full re-empowerment of UNDOF within the Area of Separation are non-negotiable preconditions for any security protocol. Al-Sharaa has publicly drawn a clear line between Syria and signatories like the United Arab Emirates or Bahrain, reiterating that Syria is an immediate border state locked in active territorial conflict over the Golan Heights, making standard normalization frameworks politically inapplicable.

The diplomatic standoff escalated into an open confrontation at the United Nations in New York on September 17, 2026. In an address to the UN Security Council, Syria’s Permanent Representative, Ibrahim Olabi, condemned Israel’s buffer zone operations as "the greatest threat facing the stability of the entire region." Olabi mobilized 44 member states to co-sign a formal protest against Israel’s high-level military inspections on Mount Hermon, accusing the IDF of violating Syrian sovereignty, disrupting local agriculture through herbicide clearance, and detaining civilians.

Israel’s Permanent Representative to the UN, Danny Danon, rejected the accusations, describing the deployment as a "tiny defensive buffer" necessary to block incursions by ISIS fragments, Hezbollah cells, and Iranian-aligned proxies. Danon directed the Council's attention toward Ankara, arguing that the body maintained a double standard by censuring Israel while ignoring the presence of approximately 20,000 Turkish soldiers and more than 100 military posts controlling five percent of Syrian territory. Turkish Ambassador Ahmet Yıldız countered Danon's assertions, stating that Turkish forces operated with the understanding of Syrian authorities to preserve state stability.

The intractable nature of the frontier has generated intense debate among strategic analysts and online defense forums. On Reddit’s r/geopolitics, contributors tracking Levant affairs have drawn extensive comparisons to Israel's 1982–2000 presence in southern Lebanon, warning that maintaining a unilateral buffer zone inside Syrian territory creates long-term friction that risks transforming a defensive screen into a permanent source of insurgent conflict. On X, foreign policy analysts noted that U.S. efforts to apply the transactional diplomacy of the Abraham Accords to post-Assad Syria ran directly into structural realities: normalization cannot bridge a divide where sovereign high ground remains militarily occupied.

With the UN Security Council extending UNDOF's mandate through December 31, 2026, international peacekeepers find themselves encircled and constrained by Israeli operational checkpoints. The diplomatic channels in Baku and Amman remain dormant. Across the frontier, peace remains elusive, held hostage by the hardened concrete of nine military redoubts and the surveillance masts towering over Mount Hermon.
