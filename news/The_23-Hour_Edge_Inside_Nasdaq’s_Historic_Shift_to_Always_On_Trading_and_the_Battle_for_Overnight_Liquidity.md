# The 23-Hour Edge: Inside Nasdaq’s Historic Shift to "Always On" Trading and the Battle for Overnight Liquidity

For decades, the ringing of the closing bell at 4:00 p.m. ET was the definitive endpoint of the American financial day. Soon, it will be nothing more than a mid-shift marker. On April 10, 2026, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) handed down a landmark approval allowing Nasdaq to extend trading for National Market System (NMS) stocks and exchange-traded products to 23 hours a day, five days a week. It is a seismic structural shift aimed directly at competing with the 24/7 crypto markets and capturing the demand of global investors in Asia and Europe.

Under the new "Global Trading Hours" framework, Nasdaq will consolidate current sessions into a massive Day Session running from 4:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m. ET. Following a one-hour maintenance pause from 8:00 p.m. to 9:00 p.m. ET for corporate actions like splits and dividends, a new "Night Session" will bridge the gap from 9:00 p.m. to 4:00 a.m. ET. This makes Nasdaq the latest heavyweight to join the "always on" era, following the SEC’s November 2024 approval of the 24X National Exchange—which launched on October 15, 2025—and NYSE Arca’s push for a 22-hour trading window.

But the shift from a 16-hour to a 23-hour market has ignited fierce debates over market integrity, liquidity traps, and trader burnout. The central question for investors is whether this democratization of access is a genuine boon for retail traders or a hunting ground for algorithmic high-frequency trading (HFT) firms.

On platforms like Reddit's r/wallstreetbets and r/stocks, the reaction reveals deep skepticism regarding structural disadvantages. As highlighted in prominent community discussions, a primary concern is that extended hours allow "sharks" or institutional players to manipulate prices when retail participation is low, potentially leading to "gap" risks when the regular session opens. Critics on Fintwit have consistently warned of thinner liquidity, wider bid-ask spreads, and extreme volatility during overnight hours, noting that retail limit orders could easily be picked off by institutional algorithms when the order book is shallow.

The structural mechanics of this new era are highly dependent on clearing infrastructure. The SEC has mandated that the Night Session will primarily support limit orders only, prohibiting unpriced or pegged orders to prevent disastrous executions during illiquid periods. Furthermore, the Depository Trust & Clearing Corporation (DTCC), which clears virtually all U.S. stock trades, is targeting Q2 2026 to roll out its 24/5 clearing capabilities. The Securities Information Processors (SIPs) must also be fully operational to provide continuous real-time data feeds before Nasdaq can officially go live in early Q3 2026.

There is also the profound impact on corporate strategy. Historically, publicly traded companies release earnings and material news precisely at 4:01 p.m. ET to give the market time to digest the information before standard trading resumes the next day. In a 23/5 environment, the traditional "after-hours" window effectively vanishes. A massive earnings miss reported at 4:05 p.m. will now immediately be subjected to continuous trading into the Night Session, where lower liquidity will inherently amplify price swings.

As we approach the late 2026 launch date, the tension between Wall Street’s traditionalists and the modern digital trader is reaching a boiling point. The SEC’s approval fundamentally rewrites the mechanics of the traditional trading day. The $50 trillion U.S. equity market is about to empirically test the pricing dynamics of an environment where the lights never turn off.
