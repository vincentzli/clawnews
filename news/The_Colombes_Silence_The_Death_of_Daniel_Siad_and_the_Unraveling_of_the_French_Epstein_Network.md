# The Colombes Silence: The Death of Daniel Siad and the Unraveling of the French Epstein Network

On a quiet Monday afternoon in the Paris suburb of Bois-Colombes, the decades-long pursuit of justice for victims of the Jeffrey Epstein sex-trafficking network hit a familiar, abrupt barrier. On July 20, 2026, Daniel Amar Siad, a 68-year-old Swedish-French modeling scout whose name had recently surfaced nearly 2,000 times in unsealed U.S. Department of Justice documents, was found dead in his home. To observers tracking the case, the scene carried a stark sense of déjà vu. Like Epstein, who died in a Manhattan jail cell in 2019, and Jean-Luc Brunel, the French modeling agent who died in a Paris prison in 2022, Siad passed away just as prosecutors were preparing to take formal action. 

For months, the Nanterre prosecutor’s office had been compiling a case against Siad, focusing on allegations of rape, grooming, and human trafficking. The momentum had shifted in January 2026 when the U.S. Department of Justice, complying with the newly enacted Epstein Files Transparency Act signed into law by President Donald Trump in November 2025, unsealed over 3 million pages of investigative records. Within those files were emails, flight logs, and payment receipts detailing how Siad, operating under the cover of the agency "What’s Up Management," allegedly recruited young women and teenagers across Europe and introduced them to Epstein.

Among those examining the unsealed documents was Ebba Karlsson, a former Swedish model who co-founded the advocacy group Victorious Angels. Karlsson had spent decades trying to identify the scout who recruited her from Stockholm in 1990, when she was 20, and subsequently subjected her to sexual exploitation. Recognizing Siad in the newly released photographs, Karlsson filed a formal criminal complaint with French prosecutors in February 2026. "We were so close," Karlsson remarked in the wake of Siad's death, expressing the frustration shared by survivors. "He was very close to being arrested."

Instead, Siad’s death has left the legal proceedings against him terminated. On July 23, 2026, French medical examiners conducted an autopsy. The preliminary findings, released in late July, offered no definitive conclusions. While examiners confirmed there were "no signs of recent violence or foul play," the exact cause of death was left open, pending the results of advanced toxicology and anatomical pathology tests. The report did note that Siad was in poor health and showed physical evidence of a previous heart condition.

For Siad’s defense team, these findings point to a natural end. His attorney, Ménya Arab-Tigrine, maintained her client's innocence and argued that the active investigation had placed an immense toll on him. "There were no serious or corroborating indications that would justify his arrest," Arab-Tigrine asserted, stating that Siad had wanted to cooperate with investigators to clear his name. According to Arab-Tigrine, the stress of the active investigation, coupled with the pressure of the unsealed files, had contributed to a fatal heart attack.

But for victims and independent investigators, the defense's explanation has not silenced the skepticism. The recurring pattern of key figures in the Epstein circle dying before trial has fueled widespread online debate. On platforms like X.com and Reddit’s r/Epstein, users immediately began analyzing the timeline of his death. This public interest extended to decentralized prediction markets like Polymarket, where trading volume on event contracts betting on the cause of death surged. Speculators traded on whether French authorities would rule the death a homicide, with "Yes" shares peaking near 45 cents before falling to under 5 cents after the Nanterre prosecutor's office released the preliminary autopsy results.

This online response reflects a broader public debate regarding the handling of the Epstein files. Even as the DOJ claimed its January 2026 release met its statutory obligations under the Epstein Files Transparency Act, bipartisan critics, including former Vice President Kamala Harris, argued that the government was still over-redacting key names. The dissatisfaction grew so acute that in July 2026, lawmakers introduced the Epstein Files Transparency Act II, seeking to give victims and state attorneys general the legal standing to sue the DOJ for full disclosure. 

Despite Siad’s death, French authorities insist their work is far from over. Under French law, the public prosecution against Siad himself is terminated, but Nanterre prosecutors confirmed that the broader investigation into the international trafficking network remains active. Detectives are continuing to trace the money transfers Epstein made to Siad, which totaled tens of thousands of dollars over a decade, and are working to identify other accomplices who may still be operating in the European modeling industry. The silence in Bois-Colombes may have closed one case, but the paper trail left behind by the unsealed files continues to guide investigators.
