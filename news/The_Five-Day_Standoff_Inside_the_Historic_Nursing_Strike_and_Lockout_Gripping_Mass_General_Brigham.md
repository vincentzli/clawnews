# The Five-Day Standoff: Inside the Historic Nursing Strike and Lockout Gripping Mass General Brigham

At 7:00 a.m. on Thursday, July 9, 2026, hundreds of registered nurses converged on the main entrance of Brigham and Women’s Hospital at 75 Francis Street in Boston. Having just completed a scheduled 24-hour strike to protest stalled contract negotiations, the nurses expected to return to their patients. Instead, they were met by metal barricades, private security personnel, and hospital administrators holding signs directing them to turn back. As the crowd chanted "Let us in!" in the morning drizzle, they were systematically turned away. It was the beginning of a four-day lockout that has barred more than 4,000 permanent nurses from the premier Harvard-affiliated teaching hospital, turning a brief labor demonstration into the largest and most contentious work stoppage in Massachusetts healthcare history.

The standoff is the result of a calculated operational decision by the hospital’s parent system, Mass General Brigham (MGB), which is the state's largest private employer. Anticipating the July 8 strike, MGB contracted approximately 1,300 temporary replacement travel nurses to maintain operations. Because the staffing agencies supplying these workers required a minimum contract of five days, MGB executives informed the permanent nursing staff that they would not be allowed back to work until 7:00 a.m. on Monday, July 13. While MGB frames the lockout as an operational and financial necessity to secure replacement labor, the Massachusetts Nurses Association (MNA) has condemned the move as a retaliatory and punitive measure designed to break the union's resolve.

Underneath the picketing and corporate messaging lies a deep financial conflict. For seven months, BWH and the MNA have been deadlocked over the terms of an 18-month contract. The union has demanded a 3% wage increase in the first year and a 4% increase in the second year. MGB has countered with a 0% across-the-board wage increase for the majority of the nursing staff. Hospital administrators argue that the existing contract already provides a 5% annual "step" raise to eligible, mid-career nurses, keeping the hospital’s average nurse salary above $147,000, with top-tier veteran salaries reaching approximately $197,000. For those at the top of the pay scale who are no longer eligible for step increases, the hospital has offered a 2.5% raise. 

However, the union contends that these wage proposals are clawed back by MGB’s demands regarding healthcare benefits. The hospital has proposed that nurses enrolled in the Harvard Pilgrim Health Plan pay 2.5 percentage points more toward their monthly health insurance premiums. The MNA calculates that this premium hike would effectively wipe out any modest wage gains, amounting to a net pay cut for many mid-career nurses. The standoff is further complicated by a parallel labor action: approximately 450 MGB Home Care clinicians—including nurses, physical therapists, social workers, and dietitians—are currently in the midst of a separate seven-day strike that began on July 8 and is scheduled to run until July 15, as they attempt to secure their first union contract since organizing in June 2024.

MGB’s leadership, including President and CEO Dr. Anne Klibanski and BWH President Dr. Giles Boland, has maintained that the hospital's financial offer is competitive and necessary to control rising healthcare delivery costs. The health system’s financial records show a complex picture. In the fiscal year ending September 30, 2025, Mass General Brigham reported a record $22.8 billion in operating revenue, an 11% increase from the $20.6 billion recorded in fiscal year 2024. Yet, due to escalating labor costs and inflation, MGB's operating income for fiscal year 2025 was a modest $59.2 million, representing a razor-thin operating margin of 0.3%. In the first quarter of fiscal year 2026, which ended December 31, 2025, the system slipped further into the red, recording an operating loss of $85.6 million. MGB officials argue that the union’s wage demands would cost the system an unsustainable $128 million over the 18-month contract period.

Union representatives paint a different financial portrait, pointing to MGB's net income of $2.4 billion in fiscal year 2025, which was bolstered by strong non-operating investment gains. They also point to executive compensation as evidence of misaligned priorities. Dr. Anne Klibanski’s total compensation for the 2024 fiscal year was reported at $8.4 million, maintaining her salary at the same $8.4 million level she received in 2023, following a steady rise from $4.3 million in 2021. "It was defeating. It was absolutely defeating and demoralizing," said Kelly Morgan, RN, Chair of the BWH MNA Bargaining Committee, describing the moment nurses were locked out. "We want to go back to work. We only wanted to do this for 24 hours. We want to be in there and take care of our patients and that's what we're asking to do and they just won't let us."

The operational transition to an all-replacement workforce has not been seamless, leading to immediate public safety debates. On the morning of Thursday, July 9, shortly before the transition of the overnight shift, a woman suffered a medical emergency on the sidewalk directly outside the hospital. Morgan and another striking nurse on the picket line crossed their own demonstration boundary to administer aid and wheel the collapsed patient into the emergency department. Morgan later criticized the hospital’s internal response as delayed, claiming that staff inside appeared disorganized and unfamiliar with the building’s emergency protocols—a charge that MGB officials actively disputed, asserting that their emergency plans were fully operational.

Inside the wards, the absence of the permanent staff has triggered anxiety among patients and their families. Peter Caikauskas, whose father was hospitalized in the intensive care unit during the lockout, experienced the friction firsthand. When his father's condition fluctuated, the temporary replacement nurse assigned to the unit was unable to contact the specialist on call. “I asked for an attending to speak with him, and the nurse didn't know how to page an attending,” Caikauskas recalled. “So during the hardest moments, I literally couldn't get the doctor.”

In response to such accounts, Mass General Brigham has defended the quality of care provided during the lockout, stating that the 1,300 replacement nurses are highly qualified and that clinical leaders are stationed on the floor to assist with handoffs. A spokesperson for MGB stated that "the safety of care has been equivalent or better than the usual Brigham performance," adding that the union was warned well in advance of the strike vote that a five-day replacement contract would require a four-day lockout. 

As the lockout enters its second full day, pressure is mounting from state and local officials. Governor Maura Healey recently convened leadership from both the MNA and Mass General Brigham at the State House in an effort to mediate the dispute, urging both sides to remain at the negotiating table to prevent further disruptions to the region’s healthcare infrastructure. Boston Mayor Michelle Wu has similarly called for a swift resolution. With the doors of Brigham and Women’s closed to its permanent nursing staff until Monday morning, negotiations remain deadlocked as the lockout continues through the weekend.
