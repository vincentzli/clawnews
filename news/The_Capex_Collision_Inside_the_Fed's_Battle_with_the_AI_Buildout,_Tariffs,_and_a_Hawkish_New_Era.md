# The Capex Collision: Inside the Fed's Battle with the AI Buildout, Tariffs, and a Hawkish New Era

WASHINGTON — The Federal Reserve’s Semiannual Monetary Policy Report, released on Friday, July 10, 2026, represents a watershed moment for the American economy. Under the newly installed leadership of Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh, who assumed office on May 22, 2026, succeeding Jerome Powell after his term as Chair ended in February, the central bank has laid bare the structural forces preventing inflation from returning to its long-sought 2% target. In a stark departure from the boilerplate prose of the past, the July 10 report explicitly flags three primary drivers of the spring inflation resurgence: the artificial intelligence capital expenditure boom, escalating import tariffs, and geopolitical energy shocks in the Middle East. 

For months, the market has operated under the assumption that the Fed’s current monetary tightening cycle was nearing its end, with the federal funds rate held steady in the 3.50% to 3.75% corridor since December 2025. Yet the data released on June 25, 2026, showed the preferred Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price index rose by 4.07% for the 12 months ending in May, while core PCE stood at 3.41% year-over-year. Faced with inflation running at more than double the official target, Warsh has initiated a silent revolution within the Marriner S. Eccles Building, shifting the Fed's focus back toward monetarist principles and structural price stability. 

The core of the structural tension lies in the corporate sector's insensitivity to interest rates. Major tech hyperscalers—including Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, and Oracle—have collectively guided toward approximately $745 billion in combined capital expenditures for 2026, representing an 89% increase from 2025. In Fintwit and Reddit communities, this massive buildout has ignited fierce debates. On subreddits like r/stocks, users frequently draw direct parallels to the 1990s telecom crash, pointing out that Cisco was highly profitable during the dot-com boom but saw its stock price suffer for nearly two decades once the infrastructure buildout outpaced real demand. The debate is no longer just about tech valuations; it is about the macroeconomy. While Chairman Warsh has publicly characterized the AI transition as a "paradigm shift" that could eventually act as a "significant disinflationary force" by boosting productivity, the Fed's report highlights the immediate, short-term inflationary consequence: the bidding up of specialized hardware, grid capacity, and raw materials. 

This corporate spending boom is colliding with a central bank that is rapidly dismantling its own communication framework. At his inaugural Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) meeting in June 2026, Warsh abandoned the long-standing practice of "forward guidance." The committee removed all forward-looking statements from its policy release. Furthermore, Warsh refused to submit his own interest-rate projections for the Fed's Summary of Economic Projections, commonly known as the "dot plot," declaring the practice "not helpful in the conduct of policy." Even without Warsh’s dot, the June projections revealed a deeply divided committee, with 9 of 18 participants favoring at least one rate hike in 2026. 

To review the Fed's operations, Warsh has established five independent task forces, recruiting prominent outside advisers including Netscape co-founder Marc Andreessen and former Walmart CEO Doug McMillon to analyze communications, the Fed's $6.7 trillion balance sheet, and the economic impacts of AI. This institutional shake-up comes as the Fed places renewed emphasis on the money supply. According to the July 10 report, the M2 money supply rose by 4.7% over the first five months of 2026 compared to the previous year, a trend that monetarists argue is directly fueling the stubborn PCE print.

Adding to the inflation mix are supply-side shocks that lie entirely outside the scope of interest rate policy. The Fed’s report notes that earlier tariff hikes continue to elevate the domestic costs of imported goods, while the ongoing conflict in the Middle East involving Iran, which culminated in the partial closure of the Strait of Hormuz, has driven crude oil prices higher, adding upward pressure to headline inflation. 

For the bond market, the combination of structural corporate spending, tariffs, and energy shocks suggests that the path of least resistance for rates may be higher. Ahead of Chairman Warsh’s highly anticipated congressional testimonies—scheduled before the House Financial Services Committee on Tuesday, July 14, 2026, and the Senate Banking Committee on Wednesday, July 15, 2026—traders have begun aggressively repricing the curve. Short-term interest rate futures are now pricing in a 42% probability of a 25-basis-point rate hike at the FOMC's September meeting, up from just 12% a month ago.

The central tension of this economic cycle is clear. If corporate giants are determined to spend hundreds of billions of dollars building AI infrastructure, and geopolitical conflicts continue to squeeze energy supplies, the Fed's 3.50% to 3.75% funds rate may not be restrictive enough. As Warsh prepares to face lawmakers, the market is beginning to realize that the "Warsh Era" will not be defined by political accommodation, but by a hawkish insistence on price stability, regardless of the cost to Silicon Valley's capex plans.
