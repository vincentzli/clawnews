# The Swap Loophole: Inside the Federal-State Turf War Over Prediction Markets and New York’s $36 Billion Demand

On July 31, 2026, the state of New York filed a major civil enforcement action against the prediction market industry. Led by Governor Kathy Hochul and Attorney General Letitia James, the state filed a massive lawsuit seeking an estimated $36 billion in disgorgement and penalties against KalshiEX LLC. The accusation was direct and severe: Kalshi, despite its status as a federally regulated derivatives exchange, was operating an illegal, unlicensed gambling business. New York officials pointed to a glaring loophole: Kalshi allowed 18-year-olds to bet on outcomes ranging from sports to cultural events, bypassing the state's strict minimum gaming age of 21. For an industry that has pitched itself as a sophisticated playground for economic hedging, the New York lawsuit was a bracing reminder that state regulators are no longer willing to watch from the sidelines.

This high-stakes escalation in New York is only the latest front in a rapidly expanding war between state police powers and federal financial regulators. At the heart of the conflict is a fundamental legal question: Are prediction market contracts financial derivatives under the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal government, or are they simply traditional gambling dressed up in the language of Wall Street? The answer to that question has split federal and state courts, pitted the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC) against state attorneys general, and set the stage for an inevitable showdown at the U.S. Supreme Court.

Just months earlier, the industry seemed to have secured a historic federal shield. On April 6, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit issued an opinion in *KalshiEX LLC v. Flaherty*. Writing for the majority, Judge David J. Porter—joined by Chief Judge Michael A. Chagares—affirmed a preliminary injunction blocking Mary Jo Flaherty, interim director of the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement, from enforcing state gambling laws against Kalshi’s sports-related event contracts. Judge Porter accepted Kalshi's core argument: these event contracts are legally classified as "swaps" under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA). Because Kalshi is a registered Designated Contract Market (DCM), the majority held that the CEA grants the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction, preempting state-level gaming regulations under the Supremacy Clause. To the majority, New Jersey's enforcement efforts represented an impermissible obstacle to the uniform federal framework established by Congress. In her dissent, however, Judge Jane Richards Roth sounded a warning, arguing that the majority's ruling stripped states of their traditional police powers to protect consumers from unregulated gambling.

That warning has since become a rallying cry for state attorneys general across the nation. Less than five months after the Third Circuit's decision, the federal consensus began to fracture. On August 10, 2026, U.S. District Judge Vernon D. Oliver of the District of Connecticut dealt Kalshi a severe setback in *KalshiEX LLC v. Cafferelli*. Denying Kalshi's motion for a preliminary injunction against Bryan T. Cafferelli, the Commissioner of the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection, Judge Oliver rejected the Third Circuit's rationale. He ruled that Kalshi’s sports-related event contracts are not "swaps" under the CEA, characterizing them instead as "sports wagers" that fall squarely within the state's traditional authority to regulate gambling. Judge Oliver went further, writing that even if the contracts could be stretched to fit the definition of a swap, there was no evidence that Congress intended the CEA to displace the states' historic police powers over sports betting.

The conflict reached a boiling point on August 15, 2026, when Judge Oliver denied Kalshi’s emergency request for an injunction pending appeal. In doing so, he issued a sharp rebuke to the CFTC. Following the Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection's enforcement actions, the CFTC had issued an emergency administrative order permitting Kalshi to continue operating in the state. Judge Oliver flatly rejected the idea that a federal agency could override a federal court's interpretation of law. The CFTC, Judge Oliver noted, lacks the authority to issue emergency directives that conflict with judicial rulings on federal preemption. Kalshi has since filed an emergency appeal with the Second U.S. Circuit Court of Appeals, but the damage was done: the legal precedent established by the Third Circuit faced growing resistance from other jurisdictions.

While the federal courts debate the taxonomy of swaps, state courts have moved swiftly to shut down Kalshi's operations within their borders. In Washington State, Attorney General Nick Brown filed a civil lawsuit in March 2026, alleging the exchange violated the Washington Gambling Act and the Consumer Protection Act. By August 2026, King County Superior Court Judge John F. McHale issued a final order requiring Kalshi to implement strict geofencing to block Washington residents from trading on sports, elections, politics, entertainment, culture, technology, science, and "mentions." Kalshi has filed a motion for reconsideration, with a decision expected on September 2, 2026, but the platform has already been forced to erect digital walls around the state.

Even more aggressive has been the response in Arizona. On March 17, 2026, Arizona Attorney General Kris Mayes took the unprecedented step of filing a 20-count criminal information against KalshiEX LLC and Kalshi Trading LLC in Maricopa County Superior Court. The criminal charges allege that Kalshi is running an illegal, unlicensed gambling enterprise and facilitating illegal wagering on political outcomes—including the 2028 presidential race and the 2026 Arizona gubernatorial and Secretary of State elections. It marks the first time a state prosecutor has pursued criminal charges against a federally regulated prediction exchange, raising the stakes from civil compliance to potential corporate criminal liability.

The tension between federal oversight and state authority has also erupted into direct institutional conflict. In June 2026, Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel obtained a temporary restraining order in state court requiring Kalshi to suspend its sports-related contracts and void and refund existing trades made by Michigan residents. The response from Washington, D.C., was swift and combative. The CFTC, led by Chairman Michael Selig—who was sworn in on December 22, 2025, after a career representing crypto and prediction market firms—intervened. Selig issued a federal directive ordering Kalshi to defy the Michigan court's instruction to cancel trades. Selig argued that state courts could not "bully" a federally registered exchange into liquidating positions, warning that doing so would compromise the finality and integrity of the federal derivatives markets. The move effectively put a federal regulatory body in the position of instructing a regulated entity to ignore a state court order, illustrating the depth of the jurisdictional fissure.

As the regulatory wars rage in the courtroom, the platforms themselves are facing a crisis of consumer trust and legal scrutiny from their own users. The most dramatic example occurred in early 2026. In February 2026, a joint U.S.-Israeli airstrike killed Iran’s Supreme Leader, Ali Khamenei. The death of the Iranian leader immediately threw Kalshi's "Ali Khamenei out as Supreme Leader?" contract—which had attracted $54 million in wagers—into chaos. While traders holding "yes" contracts expected a payout, Kalshi's leadership, including CEO Tarek Mansour, invoked a previously obscure "death carveout" in the market rules. Under this provision, Kalshi ruled that a leader's death did not constitute "leaving office" in the traditional sense. Instead of paying out the full $1.00 face value of the contracts to "yes" holders, Kalshi resolved the market based on the last traded price before the suspension. The decision sparked widespread outrage on legal and trading forums like Reddit’s r/law and r/wallstreetbets. Commenters accused the platform of engaging in misleading practices, with one class-action lawsuit filed in California alleging that the platform lured users into betting millions on a market it had no intention of resolving under common-sense definitions.

The controversy fed into a broader skepticism among legal observers. On subreddits like r/law, critics have increasingly argued that prediction markets are using the CFTC's regulatory umbrella as a shield to run what is effectively a high-tech sports book. "If this is a swap, then a fantasy football league is a hedge fund," one commenter wrote, summarizing the view that the "exchange" label is merely "lipstick on a pig." Others pointed to the briefly contested lawsuit filed on August 11, 2026, by flight-tracking service FlightAware, which accused Kalshi of using its trademark and proprietary data without permission to host markets on flight cancellations. Although FlightAware voluntarily dismissed the lawsuit the next day—suggesting a swift, private settlement—the incident highlighted the aggressive, move-fast-and-break-things culture of a platform pushing into every corner of public data.

With states like New York demanding billions, Arizona filing criminal charges, and federal courts issuing directly conflicting rulings on the nature of "swaps," the current regulatory landscape is unsustainable. A patchwork of geofenced states—where Kalshi is blocked in Washington, Michigan, and Nevada while operating freely in New Jersey—undermines the very concept of a uniform national financial market. As the Second Circuit prepares to hear Kalshi's appeal in Connecticut and New Jersey defends its gaming revenues, the road leads inevitably to the Supreme Court. Until the high court decides whether the federal Commodity Exchange Act truly preempts traditional state police power over gaming, the prediction market industry remains a multi-billion-dollar bet on a legal knife-edge.
