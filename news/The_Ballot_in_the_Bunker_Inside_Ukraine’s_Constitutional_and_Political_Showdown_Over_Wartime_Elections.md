# The Ballot in the Bunker: Inside Ukraine’s Constitutional and Political Showdown Over Wartime Elections

Late on the night of August 18, 2026, as air raid sirens sounded across Kyiv, former Defence Minister Mykhailo Fedorov published a video address that abruptly challenged Ukraine’s wartime political consensus. Hours earlier, President Volodymyr Zelenskyy had formally submitted to the Verkhovna Rada the nomination of Yevhenii Khmara—who had stepped down from active military duty as a Major-General to fulfill statutory civilian requirements—to permanently lead the Defence Ministry following Fedorov’s July 15 dismissal. Instead of stepping back from public life, the 35-year-old former digital transformation architect questioned whether the Russian Federation should effectively determine when Ukrainian citizens can elect their leadership. Insisting that "difficult doesn't mean impossible," Fedorov urged the development of legal and logistical mechanisms to hold wartime national elections, alleging that private interests, corruption, and appointments based on personal loyalty had created a "systemic governance crisis."

The reaction was immediate and reshaped Kyiv's domestic political landscape. For weeks, central Kyiv’s Ivan Franko Square and regional administrative hubs in Lviv, Kharkiv, and Ivano-Frankivsk had seen recurring demonstrations known as the "Cardboard Maidan." Organized by veterans and civil society figures brandishing handwritten placards, the rallies initially demanded Fedorov’s reinstatement and condemned entrenched military bureaucracy. However, Fedorov’s pivot toward wartime ballots alienated the very movement that had mobilized in his support. Protest co-organizer and combat veteran Dmytro Koziatynskyi swiftly disavowed the proposal, stating he viewed wartime elections "negatively" and could not conceive of how active-duty soldiers on the front line could participate. Koziatynskyi emphasized that "street protests are definitely not the place for electoral discourse," underscored that their campaign was aimed at structural defense reform rather than individual ambitions, and thanked demonstrators for having secured the July 21 dismissal of Commander-in-Chief Oleksandr Syrskyi. Koziatynskyi's statement effectively marked the dissolution of the street rallies.

Empirical research reflected this societal pushback. Polling conducted by the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS) during the demonstrations revealed that 57% of Ukrainians opposed holding elections before the war concludes. Twenty-three percent supported voting only after a formal ceasefire accompanied by verifiable security guarantees, while just 15% favored conducting ballots while active hostilities continue. As KIIS Executive Director Anton Grushetskyi observed, the data underscored that even among citizens actively protesting government personnel decisions, there was no popular mandate for wartime elections.

The dispute highlights a foundational constitutional and statutory paradox. Ukraine’s 1996 Constitution outlines an explicit mechanism for legislative continuity: Article 83 stipulates that if the term of the Verkhovna Rada expires under martial law, its mandate is automatically extended until a new parliament convenes after martial law is lifted. For the presidency, however, the text is less explicit. While Article 103 establishes a five-year term, Article 108 provides that the sitting president continues to exercise authority until a newly elected head of state assumes office. The formal prohibition on elections does not stem from the Constitution itself, but from Article 19 of the Law of Ukraine "On the Legal Regime of Martial Law," which expressly bans presidential, parliamentary, and local elections during active martial law, reinforced by Article 10's protection against terminating executive powers.

President Volodymyr Zelenskyy rejected the prospect of wartime elections during an August 23 press briefing at Bankova, characterizing such ballots as "a tsunami for the state that will split Ukraine." Zelenskyy insisted that holding elections amidst active warfare would jeopardize national cohesion, stating that partners had offered no conditions allowing voting to proceed "safely, legitimately, and democratically" while Russian strikes continue. Presidential communications adviser Dmytro Lytvyn pointed to daily Russian ballistic missile, glide bomb, and drone strikes across Ukrainian population centers as the primary physical barrier to electoral procedures.

The logistical impediments identified by election watchdogs remain immense. More than six million Ukrainians reside abroad as refugees, and millions more remain internally displaced. Enfranchising this diaspora would require establishing consular and extraterritorial polling operations of unprecedented scale. Concurrently, hundreds of thousands of mobilized personnel remain deployed along hundreds of kilometers of active combat line. An officer at the Ministry of Defence described frontline sentiment regarding the election proposal as one of shock, noting that while institutional accountability is widely desired, securing polling booths, candidate safety, and verifiable ballots under continuous Russian bombardment is operationally unfeasible. Civil network OPORA warned that wartime balloting would introduce multiple vulnerabilities for Russian interference, disruption, and legal delegitimization.

Cross-party reaction in the Verkhovna Rada reflected broad institutional resistance. Holos party leader Kira Rudik stated that "the constitution did not change because of his dismissal," arguing that elections cannot be held under martial law and warning against division. Fellow Holos lawmaker Yaroslav Zheleznyak called the proposal "unrealistic, at least because of the constitution," while European Solidarity MP Mykola Kniazhytskyi noted the prohibitive security risks. However, former Foreign Minister Dmytro Kuleba voiced support for examining Fedorov's core concern, stating that "the political model that was formed in 2019" is "emotionally exhausted" and observing an undeniable public demand for legislative renewal.

The friction between Bankova and Fedorov originated from fundamental operational disputes within the defense apparatus. Following his appointment as Defence Minister in January 2026, Fedorov sought to implement restructuring that clashed directly with Commander-in-Chief Oleksandr Syrskyi. Zelenskyy recounted that he had rejected Fedorov’s initial blueprint for a restructured "Ministry of War" because it proposed subordinating the Chief of the General Staff directly to the ministry without an independent Commander-in-Chief. The subsequent breakdown in direct communication between Fedorov and Syrskyi paralyzed joint decision-making, prompting Zelenskyy to dismiss Fedorov on July 15, followed by Syrskyi's dismissal on July 21 and the appointment of Mykhailo Drapatyi.

Zelenskyy also pointed to strategic and fiscal friction during the spring of 2026. He detailed an episode where the Defence Ministry under Fedorov reportedly advised that SpaceX had consented to Ukrainian Starlink utilization for deep strikes inside Russian borders subject to political authorization. When Zelenskyy conveyed this to US President Donald Trump, Trump checked with Elon Musk, who rejected the claim, leading Trump to accuse Bankova of misrepresentation. Zelenskyy also cited an estimated $27 billion budget gap resulting from advanced procurement spending—a figure Fedorov contested in a subsequent interview with Reuters, stating his procurement reforms achieved substantial savings.

On August 19, the Verkhovna Rada confirmed Khmara as Minister of Defence with 312 votes. Fedorov subsequently accepted an external advisory role with Italian Defence Minister Guido Crosetto while remaining based in Kyiv, and on September 1 announced a defense technology enterprise backed by Palantir CEO Alex Karp. Although the pro-Fedorov demonstrations concluded following the confirmation of his successor and the rejection of wartime balloting, the episode exposed the unresolved constitutional, operational, and institutional tensions of governing a democracy under prolonged existential war.
