# The $1.77 Trillion Gravitational Pull: How SpaceX’s Public Ascent is Re-Plumbing the Tesla Ecosystem

In the financial universe orbiting Elon Musk, gravity behaves in unpredictable ways. When SpaceX completed its historic initial public offering on June 12, 2026, pricing its shares at $135.00 to achieve a market capitalization of $1.77 trillion, it did not merely set a record as the largest public debut in history. It also created a massive new capital sink that has fundamentally altered the dynamics of its older sibling, Tesla Inc. For years, public investors seeking exposure to Musk’s technological vision had only one vehicle: Tesla (TSLA). Now, the public listing of SpaceX (SPCX) on the Nasdaq has triggered a structural reallocation of capital, options liquidity, and investor attention, the direct effects of which are starting to register on Tesla’s trading tape.

This capital realignment was starkly visible during the week ending July 3, 2026. On July 2, Tesla released its second-quarter vehicle delivery report, announcing it had delivered 480,126 vehicles globally. The figure was a resounding beat against the consensus Wall Street estimate of 406,000 units. Yet, in a classic "sell-the-news" reaction, Tesla shares plunged 7.50% in a single trading session, closing at $393.45. While a stock drop after a delivery beat is not entirely unprecedented for the highly volatile automaker, the concurrent behavior of Tesla's options chain was highly unusual. Rather than spiking as the stock tumbled, Tesla’s 30-day implied volatility (IV30) fell by 7.50% over the week, settling into the 46% to 47% range. Historically, a sharp drop in a high-beta stock like Tesla is accompanied by an expansion in implied volatility. The drop in IV suggests that the speculative retail energy that traditionally drives Tesla’s options volume has found a new home.

The central controversy now occupying Wall Street, Fintwit, and Reddit is whether SpaceX’s growth story is actively cannibalizing capital that would otherwise support Tesla's equity valuation. On Reddit's r/wallstreetbets, the rotation of funds is openly discussed, with several retail investors indicating they are actively shifting assets. Users have repeatedly debated whether to rotate capital out of Tesla to fund purchases of SpaceX, with multiple posters sharing plans to sell TSLA shares to buy SPCX under the argument that the primary growth story has shifted from terrestrial automotive markets to orbital infrastructure. Critics on r/stocks have voiced deep skepticism over the $1.77 trillion valuation, pointing to the structural divergence between the two companies. In 2025, SpaceX generated $18.67 billion in revenue but recorded a net loss of nearly $5 billion, yielding an implied price-to-sales multiple of roughly 95x. Tesla, by contrast, operates as a highly profitable automaker with a trailing price-to-earnings ratio that, while historically elevated, is backed by billions of dollars in free cash flow. Critics argue that SpaceX is trading "divorced from valuation" and relying on "make-believe" future growth scenarios to support its multi-trillion-dollar market cap.

Despite these concerns, the technical plumbing of the market is currently working in SpaceX’s favor. The company is scheduled for inclusion in the Nasdaq-100 index on July 7, 2026, an event that has triggered massive front-running by retail traders. On subreddits like r/stocks, users are debating the mechanics of this inclusion, with bulls arguing that index trackers will be forced to buy SPCX, driving the price higher. However, counter-arguments are gaining traction, with some traders warning that "everyone has the same thesis" and that the expected index-buying surge is already priced into the stock. This speculative positioning is further complicated by SpaceX's low public float—the company raised $75 billion in the IPO, leaving the vast majority of shares locked up—and a high short interest that sits at approximately 31% of the tradable float. 

The division of capital is mirrored by the division of Musk's attention. As Musk oversees Tesla, SpaceX, xAI, Neuralink, and X, institutional investors are raising questions about key-man risk and governance. With the Federal Reserve, now led by Chair Kevin Warsh following his confirmation on May 22, 2026, maintaining a tight monetary policy alongside Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, the cost of capital remains historically high. In this macro environment, investors are scrutinizing whether Musk can manage a sprawling corporate web without siphoning resources from public shareholders. Speculation has surfaced on r/wallstreetbets about potential corporate restructurings, including theories of a "megamerger" or a scenario where SpaceX acquires Tesla, which critics have labeled as a potential "shell game" to support overextended valuations. 

For now, institutional players are taking divergent paths. ARK Invest's Cathie Wood capitalized on Tesla's 7.50% drop on July 2, purchasing approximately $38 million worth of TSLA shares. This purchase came just days after ARK acquired equity in SpaceX, demonstrating that some large-scale managers view the two assets as synergistic pieces of a single ecosystem rather than zero-sum competitors. Yet, as the July 7 Nasdaq-100 inclusion approaches and the 180-day IPO lock-up period begins its countdown, the primary question remains: Can public markets sustain two multi-trillion-dollar Musk enterprises, or will SpaceX's ascendant star continue to sap the financial gravity of Tesla?
