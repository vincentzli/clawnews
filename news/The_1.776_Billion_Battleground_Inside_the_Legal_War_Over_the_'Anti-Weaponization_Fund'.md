# The $1.776 Billion Battleground: Inside the Legal War Over the 'Anti-Weaponization Fund'

The ink is barely dry on the Department of Justice’s historic settlement with President Donald Trump, yet California Governor Gavin Newsom has already opened an unprecedented front in state-federal relations. The creation of the $1.776 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund"—billed by the administration as restitution for the unauthorized leak of the President's tax returns by former IRS contractor Charles Littlejohn—has triggered a constitutional crisis over state taxing power and executive authority.

At the core of this conflict is a January 2026 lawsuit in which President Trump sued the Treasury and the IRS for $10 billion. His legal team utilized a novel, maximalist application of Internal Revenue Code Section 7431, arguing that every individual digital view of the *New York Times* and *ProPublica* articles containing his leaked financial data constituted a separate $1,000 statutory violation. Littlejohn, who was previously sentenced to five years in federal prison for the theft, became the catalyst for what critics are calling a monumental wealth transfer.

The subsequent settlement, finalized this month and signed off by Acting Attorney General Todd Blanche, dismissed the lawsuit with prejudice. In exchange, the President received no direct cash damages but secured an extraordinary concession: the government is now "forever barred and precluded" from examining or prosecuting the current or prior tax filings of Trump, his sons, and the Trump Organization. To satisfy the monetary component of the settlement, the DOJ authorized the creation of the nine-figure Anti-Weaponization Fund.

This $1.776 billion pool is governed by a five-member commission, appointed by Blanche, tasked with distributing funds to individuals who claim they were "victims of lawfare" or politically motivated investigations during the tenure of former President Joe Biden. The initial bylaws, leaked late Tuesday, require applicants to demonstrate that their prosecution or civil penalty was motivated by "political, personal, or ideological reasons." This broad mandate leaves the door open for high-profile allies to stake claims. More controversially, the eligible applicant pool includes defendants prosecuted for their involvement in the January 6 Capitol riot.

The prospect of federal taxpayer dollars flowing to convicted rioters prompted an immediate legal challenge. Former Capitol Police Officer Harry Dunn and Metropolitan Police Officer Daniel Hodges filed a federal lawsuit in Washington, arguing the settlement is an illegal deployment of the federal Judgment Fund. Their filing characterizes the payouts as nothing short of "public financing for paramilitary organizations," noting that the Judgment Fund was designed to pay legal liabilities of the United States, not to operate as a discretionary grant program for the administration's political base.

Governor Newsom, however, did not wait for the federal courts to intervene. He announced a proposal to levy a 100% state tax on any Californian receiving money from the Anti-Weaponization Fund. Calling the fund a "criminal enterprise" and "the great grift," Newsom intends to utilize the California Franchise Tax Board to intercept the payouts the moment they hit residents' bank accounts.

Federal lawmakers have attempted to mirror Newsom’s strategy. Representative Mike Thompson (D-CA) introduced the "Stop Letting United States Heads Funnel Unauthorized Nontransparent Dollars Act of 2026." The SLUSH FUND Act seeks to impose a 100% federal excise tax on any fund payouts and mandates full Treasury Department disclosure of all recipients. Given the Republican majority in the House of Representatives, Thompson’s bill faces steep procedural hurdles, but it serves as a legislative anchor for the opposition.

The looming clash over Newsom’s proposal centers on the Supremacy Clause of the U.S. Constitution (Article VI, Paragraph 2) and the long-standing precedent of *McCulloch v. Maryland*, which established that states cannot interfere with or tax federal instruments out of existence. Legal scholars note that while states have broad taxation powers, a hyper-targeted 100% tax designed explicitly to neutralize a federal program crosses into "hostile taxation." If California can tax a federal settlement out of existence, the state could theoretically do the same to federal salaries or defense contracts.

Newsom’s legal allies counter that the settlement itself circumvents congressional appropriation, thus stripping it of standard Supremacy Clause protections. They argue the payouts are not a legitimate federal function, but a political payout masquerading as a legal settlement.

On social media, the debate has fractured along predictable political lines, augmented by intense technical and financial speculation. On X.com, one prominent legal commentator pointed out the structural novelty of the move: "The DOJ just invented a backdoor pardon that pays out dividends to the pardoned." Over on Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets, users began calculating the tax arbitrage of temporarily relocating from California to neighboring states just to cash the commission checks. "Moving to Vegas for 6 months to dodge Gavin's 100% rake on my lawfare stimmy," read one top-voted comment, highlighting the enforcement nightmare California’s tax authorities are about to inherit. Meanwhile, in r/politics, debates raged over whether the SLUSH FUND Act could realistically be forced to the floor via a discharge petition.

The timeline for this multifaceted conflict is rapidly compressing. The commission is scheduled to begin disbursing funds by the third quarter of 2026. If the Dunn-Hodges lawsuit fails to secure a preliminary injunction freezing the Judgment Fund transfers, the battle will immediately shift to the tax code. The Treasury Department is expected to challenge California’s 100% tax in federal court the moment it passes the state legislature. The coming months will test the limits of state fiscal sovereignty against the executive branch's authority to unilaterally settle litigation, setting up an inevitable Supreme Court confrontation that could permanently redefine the financial boundaries of federalism.
