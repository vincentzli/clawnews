# The 90 Percent Stalemate: Inside the Secret Talks Between Israel and Syria’s Post-Assad Government

When Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa took his seat on September 22, 2026, at an Atlantic Council forum on the sidelines of the 81st United Nations General Assembly in New York, the diplomatic corridor anticipated a plea for reconstruction capital. Instead, sitting across from former United States envoy Brett McGurk, the former insurgent commander turned head of state confirmed one of the region’s most guarded secrets: Damascus and Jerusalem had spent the better part of a year in direct and indirect security negotiations that had advanced to an astonishing 90 percent consensus threshold—before stalling over intractable disputes regarding territory and sovereignty.

The disclosure laid bare an unprecedented diplomatic track mediated by Washington that sought to institutionalize a post-war security architecture along the Levant's most volatile frontier. Yet, as al-Sharaa made clear to the foreign policy establishment gathered in Manhattan, the remaining 10 percent represents an insurmountable geopolitical chasm: the physical presence of the Israel Defense Forces inside southern Syria and the non-negotiable status of the Golan Heights.

The talks trace back to the immediate vacuum created on December 8, 2024, when the fifty-four-year rule of the Assad dynasty disintegrated in less than two weeks. Within hours of Bashar al-Assad’s flight to Moscow, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered the IDF into the demilitarized buffer zone established by the 1974 Separation of Forces Agreement, declaring the half-century-old accord defunct. Israeli armored units pushed past the UN-monitored Bravo Line into the Quneitra and Daraa governorates, seizing the strategic peak of Mount Hermon (Jabal al-Sheikh) and establishing forward fortified observation posts across Syrian territory.

Despite the military encroachment, backchannels opened swiftly. Initial contacts were facilitated by the United Arab Emirates in the spring of 2025, which led to clandestine high-level military meetings in Baku, Azerbaijan, between Syrian security officials and an Israeli delegation headed by Major General Oded Basyuk, chief of the IDF Operations Directorate. By late 2025, the track shifted to Paris under the direct mediation of U.S. envoy Tom Barrack.

Diplomatic reporting by Axios correspondent Barak Ravid revealed that five discrete rounds of Paris talks yielded tangible operational frameworks. On January 6, 2026, Syrian, Israeli, and American officials signed a trilateral accord establishing the "Joint Fusion Mechanism"—a tri-party communications and intelligence-sharing cell designed to coordinate de-escalation, prevent accidental aerial clashes, and exchange data on rogue non-state groups.

According to officials familiar with the draft text, the 90 percent consensus covered comprehensive early-warning protocols, verified limits on heavy artillery deployed east of the demilitarized zone, and an operational deconfliction channel for Israeli air interdiction strikes targeting residual chemical weapons infrastructure and long-range ballistic missile stockpiles left behind by the former regime.

The process broke down when the negotiations collided with territorial sovereignty and military geography. Damascus presented an unbending baseline: any formal security pact or non-aggression declaration was strictly contingent on an immediate Israeli military withdrawal to the pre-December 8, 2024 lines, restoring the 1974 disengagement perimeter under the United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF).

Israel flatly refused. Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz affirmed that the IDF would maintain its forward security perimeter "indefinitely." Katz argued that the military must retain physical control over Mount Hermon’s high-altitude surveillance posts and the Quneitra buffer corridor to prevent transnational jihadist cells and hostile elements from threatening northern Israeli communities. In Israeli security circles, the doctrine of strategic depth took precedence over any signed paper with a Damascus administration rooted in Islamist rebel movements.

Underneath the operational friction lies the immovable dispute over the Golan Heights, which Israel annexed in 1981 and the United States recognized as Israeli territory under President Donald Trump in 2019. In New York, al-Sharaa reiterated that Syrian sovereignty over the Golan is anchored in UN Security Council Resolution 497 and is not a subject for negotiation.

To underscore his point, al-Sharaa recounted a past diplomatic exchange with President Trump. When the U.S. president asserted that the Golan belonged to Israel, al-Sharaa countered with an acerbic quip: "Why don’t you give New Jersey to the Israelis? You don’t own the Golan Heights to give, but you own New Jersey." Al-Sharaa added that New Jersey "is full of Democrats" who did not vote for Trump, drawing laughter in the room while delivering a hard-edged diplomatic message: Damascus will not trade sovereign territory for normalized ties.

Online, the revelation ignited fierce debate across social media platforms. On X, foreign policy accounts and regional monitors dissected the exchange. As defense monitor Clash Report noted, al-Sharaa’s quip weaponized domestic American political humor to assert an unyielding legal boundary: "You don't own the Golan Heights to give, but you own New Jersey." Regional analysts pointed out that while al-Sharaa maintained an operational dialogue with Washington, the Golan remained an existential red line. On Reddit foreign affairs boards, commentators debated whether Damascus was overplaying its hand given its economic desperation, or whether Israel was squandering a historic window to formally stabilize its northern front.

The negotiations were further shaped by Washington’s dramatic shifts in economic sanctions. On October 9, 2025, the U.S. Senate passed the FY 2026 National Defense Authorization Act (NDAA) in a decisive 77–20 bipartisan vote, which included the formal repeal of the 2019 Caesar Syria Civilian Protection Act. President Trump signed the legislation into law on December 18, 2025, effectively eliminating the punishing secondary sanctions that had isolated Syria’s financial system. This was followed on August 24, 2026, by the State Department's formal rescission of Syria's designation as a State Sponsor of Terrorism.

These legal milestones granted al-Sharaa substantial economic breathing room, allowing Gulf investment capital to flow into post-war reconstruction projects without triggering secondary penalties. However, this newfound leverage also reduced Damascus’s domestic incentive to make painful territorial concessions to Israel in exchange for Western rehabilitation.

Domestic constraints now paralyze both leaderships. For al-Sharaa, any formal concession that legitimizes Israeli forces on Syrian soil or compromises claims on the Golan would ignite a violent backlash from hardline insurgent veterans and nationalist factions whose buy-in is essential for state-building and the integration of the Syrian Democratic Forces (SDF). For Netanyahu, surrendering the forward buffer zone or pulling off Mount Hermon would spark an immediate revolt from far-right coalition partners and Defense Minister Katz, who view the current military perimeter as an indispensable shield.

With full diplomatic normalization off the table, the realistic horizon remains confined to the narrow, technical parameters of the January 2026 Joint Fusion Mechanism. Damascus and Jerusalem have demonstrated that they can coordinate operational deconfliction in the shadows, but as long as Israeli armor occupies the Hermon ridge, the final 10 percent of the peace framework will remain an insurmountable front line.
