# Samsung’s 1,800% Profit Surge Triggers a Chip Sell-Off as AI Capex Panic Deepens

On July 7, 2026, Samsung Electronics delivered what would, in any ordinary market cycle, be considered an unassailable financial triumph. In its preliminary second-quarter earnings guidance, the South Korean technology giant reported consolidated sales of approximately 171 trillion Korean won and a record-breaking consolidated operating profit of 89.4 trillion won. Driven by insatiable global demand for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and enterprise SSDs, the profit figure represented a staggering 1,843% surge compared to the 4.6 trillion won reported in the second quarter of 2025. Without a massive 15 trillion to 19 trillion won provision for employee performance bonuses—tied to a landmark agreement allocating 10.5% of the semiconductor division's operating profit to worker compensation—Samsung’s operating profit would have sailed past the historic 100 trillion won threshold. 

Yet, instead of triggering a celebratory rally, the announcement sparked a bruising sell-off that reverberated across global technology markets. Shares of Samsung Electronics, which had gained 150% year-to-date, slid 6.92% to close at 296,000 won in Seoul, hitting an intraday low of 286,000 won. The selling pressure quickly crossed the Pacific, dragging down U.S. chipmakers. Micron Technology fell 4.71% to close at $938.38, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index declined by 4.14% to finish the session at 12,366.40. Nvidia, showing a rare divergence, managed a minor gain of 0.71% to close at $196.93, after dipping to an intraday low of $191.14. 

The immediate catalyst for the decline was a minor technicality: Samsung’s 171 trillion won revenue figure fell just short of the ultra-bullish consensus estimate of 172.18 trillion won. But the market's violent reaction exposed a much deeper, structural anxiety that has gripped both Wall Street and retail trading desks: the sustainability of the artificial intelligence capital expenditure supercycle. 

For retail investors, the sell-off was a baffling distortion of fundamentals. On Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets, where leveraged semiconductor trades have become a cultural staple, users reacted with open disbelief. "Samsung Q2 2026 earnings guidance: Operating Profit of 89.4T KRW, yet stock drops because of 'earnings miss'?" read one widely upvoted post, while another user remarked, "How the hell is 89.4 trillion won profit a miss? Literally printing money and the stock goes down. Classic sell the news." To retail traders, the supply squeeze in HBM and the structural demand from hyperscalers represented a risk-free compounding machine.

For institutional allocators, however, Samsung’s slightly softer revenue guidance was a warning sign that memory price increases, which have driven the sector’s parabolic rise in 2026, may be starting to taper. Memory chips, unlike proprietary graphics processing units, are ultimately commodities subject to brutal supply-demand dynamics. With hyperscalers investing hundreds of billions of dollars in AI infrastructure, the risk of overbuilding is rising. 

The numbers involved in this buildout are historically unprecedented. J.P. Morgan estimates that the four major U.S. hyperscalers—Alphabet, Amazon, Microsoft, and Meta—are collectively guiding for 2026 capital expenditures of approximately $700 billion to $725 billion. Goldman Sachs projects that S&P 500 AI-related capex will reach $754 billion to $765 billion in 2026, potentially scaling to between $920 billion and $1.4 trillion by 2027. Underpinning this massive expenditure is the transition to gigawatt-scale data centers, which J.P. Morgan projects will drive total global AI and data center spending to $5.5 trillion through 2030, with $4.1 trillion of that funded via debt. 

This heavy reliance on debt financing makes the tech sector highly sensitive to the macroeconomic policies orchestrated by Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. With the USD/KRW exchange rate hovering at 1,512.10 won, the capital cost of constructing these massive data centers remains elevated. If hyperscalers fail to show direct, software-derived revenue to justify these capital projects, a sudden pullback in capex could trigger an inventory glut, sending memory prices into a tailspin.

The competitive landscape is set to become even more intense. SK Hynix, led by CEO Kwak Noh-jung, is preparing for a massive Nasdaq ADR listing scheduled for July 10, 2026, under the ticker SKHY. The listing is expected to raise $28 billion through the issuance of 17.79 million new shares, with each ADR representing one-tenth of a common share. The proceeds are earmarked for expanding the company’s Yongin semiconductor cluster and purchasing advanced extreme ultraviolet lithography scanners from ASML. By providing U.S. investors with direct access to Samsung’s fiercest competitor in the HBM market, the listing could dilute the premium valuations currently enjoyed by Samsung and Micron. 

Ultimately, Samsung’s record-breaking quarter has laid bare the central paradox of the AI trade. While the physical layer of the AI economy is generating astronomical cash flows today, the valuation of the semiconductor sector assumes that hyperscaler capex will grow indefinitely. But as Samsung's tapering revenue growth shows, even a minor deceleration in the demand curve can trigger a sudden re-pricing. For a market that has priced in perfection, an 1,800% profit surge is no longer enough to satisfy the bears.
