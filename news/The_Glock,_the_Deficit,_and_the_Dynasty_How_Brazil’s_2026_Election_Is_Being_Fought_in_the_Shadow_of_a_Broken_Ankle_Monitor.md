# The Glock, the Deficit, and the Dynasty: How Brazil’s 2026 Election Is Being Fought in the Shadow of a Broken Ankle Monitor

On June 25, 2026, the political destiny of Brazil converged on two very different documents. The first was an official judicial filing delivered to Supreme Court Justice Alexandre de Moraes by Prosecutor General Paulo Gonet. The second was the latest PoderData polling sheet, showing incumbent President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva clinging to a narrow 40% to 36% lead over Senator Flávio Bolsonaro in the country's upcoming October 4 presidential election. For a nation preparing to vote in a highly polarized environment, the details of both documents outline the dramatic conflict that will decide the leadership of Latin America’s largest economy.

The immediate source of political tension is a 9mm Glock pistol. On June 15, military police officers at a routine checkpoint in the Brasília satellite city of Taguatinga stopped an official vehicle driven by a sergeant from the Gabinete de Segurança Institucional (GSI)—the institutional security cabinet that provides protection for former presidents. Inside, they found the Glock, registered to former President Jair Bolsonaro, lacking the mandatory transport documentation. 

What followed was a legal dispute that has split the capital. Jair Bolsonaro, who was convicted of a coup attempt on September 11, 2025, and sentenced to 27 years in prison, had been granted humanitarian house arrest in March due to failing health. As the 90-day period of that house arrest expired on June 25, the presence of the Glock outside his residence threatened to return him to a federal cell. Bolsonaro’s defense team argued that the GSI security team had removed the weapon's percussor—its firing pin—rendering it completely inoperable. They claimed this was done without the former president’s knowledge as a safety precaution because Bolsonaro was taking psychiatric medications that could affect his cognition.

However, during a formal deposition with the Civil Police on June 23, the former president offered a different explanation. Bolsonaro admitted ownership of the weapon, stating he had personally noticed a malfunction while handling the gun and instructed the sergeant to take it for repairs. "I have three women at home and cannot remain unarmed," Bolsonaro testified. 

While Gonet’s June 25 opinion advised Justice Moraes that the incident does not yet warrant a finding of a disciplinary infraction, the debate highlights the legal sword of Damocles hanging over the Bolsonaro dynasty. With the elder Bolsonaro legally barred from public office until 2030, his political mantle has passed to his eldest son, Senator Flávio Bolsonaro. 

But Flávio’s bid to unify the right is complicated by divisions within his own camp. Although Jair Bolsonaro has fully endorsed him, other conservative leaders are preparing their own bids. At the Megaleite agricultural show in Belo Horizonte earlier this month, Flávio stood alongside Governor Romeu Zema of Minas Gerais, representing the NOVO party, and Governor Ronaldo Caiado of Goiás, representing the Social Democratic Party (PSD). While the trio publicly called for a "union pact" to defeat Lula’s Workers’ Party (PT), the underlying tensions were obvious. Caiado publicly noted that the opposition candidates must show "humility" and recognize that they need to pool their resources to compete with the frontrunners. 

The economic backdrop of the campaign is defined by a deep fiscal struggle. President Lula, now 80 and seeking a fourth term, has deployed a massive R$140 billion stimulus package including tax relief, subsidized credit, and social transfers. The administration’s strategy is designed to boost consumer spending and shore up working-class support ahead of October. 

Yet this public spending has triggered market jitters. S&P Global Ratings has warned that Brazil’s weak fiscal position remains a major structural risk. The country’s nominal public sector deficit climbed to 9.41% of GDP in the 12 months ending in March 2026. Meanwhile, gross public debt has reached 80% of GDP, with analysts projecting it could reach 95% if spending is not curtailed. This fiscal expansion has fueled persistent inflation, with forecasts for 2026 rising to 5.0%–5.2%—well above the Central Bank’s 3.0% target. 

In response, the Central Bank has maintained high interest rates, with the benchmark Selic rate remaining elevated after peaking at 15% in previous tightening cycles. Lula has repeatedly criticized these rates, arguing they stifle growth, but the bank has insisted on caution to prevent inflation from running out of control. 

This domestic struggle has also drawn international attention, particularly from Washington. U.S. President Donald Trump, who returned to the White House in January 2025, has maintained close ties to the Bolsonaro family. In May 2026, Flávio Bolsonaro traveled to Washington, D.C., to meet with Trump, attempting to leverage this connection to boost his campaign. Yet Trump has also adopted a pragmatic approach toward Lula's government, hosting the Brazilian president at the White House in May 2026 to discuss trade and rare earth minerals, following an earlier meeting at the ASEAN Summit in late 2025. 

As the October 4 election approaches, Brazil remains caught between Lula’s fiscal stimulus and the conservative opposition’s populist appeal. If no candidate secures an absolute majority in the first round, a second-round runoff on October 25 will decide whether the country continues its current social welfare path or turns back to the right-wing populism of the Bolsonaro era.
