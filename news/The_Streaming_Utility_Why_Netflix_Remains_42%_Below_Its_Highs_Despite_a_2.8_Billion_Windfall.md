# The Streaming Utility: Why Netflix Remains 42% Below Its Highs Despite a $2.8 Billion Windfall

On July 16, 2026, Netflix co-CEOs Ted Sarandos and Greg Peters, alongside CFO Spence Neumann, will face investors for the company’s second-quarter earnings call. Wall Street is projecting revenue of approximately $12.57 billion to $12.58 billion and earnings per share of $0.79. The company's internal guidance targets revenue of $12.574 billion, an operating margin of 32.6%, and EPS of $0.78. Yet, despite these record-setting operating projections, Netflix common stock (NFLX) closed on July 10, 2026, at $73.37 per share, down approximately 43% from its 52-week high of $128.96. The stock is testing key support near $70, and trading volume remains elevated at 46 million shares.

This steep valuation contraction highlights a growing debate among retail traders and institutional allocators. Once the poster child for hyper-growth tech disruptors, Netflix is undergoing a fundamental reassessment. Investors on Reddit's r/stocks increasingly characterize NFLX as a "utility company," pointing to its stable but maturing cash flows. The stock's trailing price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio has compressed to a range of 24.4x to 24.6x, with forward P/E estimates sitting between 19.1x and 21.6x. This is a dramatic departure from Netflix’s five-year median trailing P/E, which historically exceeded 40x. The era of the high-multiple growth premium seems to be ending, replaced by the sober multiples of a media utility.

This transition was underscored by a major governance shift. On June 4, 2026, co-founder and long-time executive chairman Reed Hastings officially stepped down from the board of directors, ending his nearly 30-year tenure. Jay Hoag was appointed as the new chairman. The lack of market fanfare surrounding Hastings' departure reflected a company that has institutionalized its transition from a founder-led tech start-up to a corporate conglomerate.

At the heart of the growth debate is Netflix's monetization strategy. In March 2026, Netflix instituted another price hike in the United States, raising the Standard plan to $19.99 per month and the Premium plan to $26.99 per month. The Standard with Ads plan remained at $8.99 per month, while the fee to add an extra member outside the household was set at $7.99 for the ad tier and $9.99 for the ad-free tier. These hikes are designed to drive average revenue per membership (ARM), but they are testing the limits of consumer willingness to pay. On Fintwit, bears argue that the massive $11 price gap between Standard with Ads ($8.99) and Standard ($19.99) is encouraging users to downgrade, cannibalizing the company’s highest-margin ad-free tier.

Furthermore, investors are operating with limited visibility. Starting in Q1 2025, Netflix ceased reporting quarterly subscriber additions and ARM, forcing the market to focus on high-level revenue and operating margin. To satisfy demands for growth metrics, Netflix announced at its May 2026 Upfront presentation that its ad-supported tier had reached 250 million monthly active viewers (MAV) globally, up from 190 million MAV in November 2025 and 70 million monthly active users (MAU) in November 2024. However, analysts note that the shift from "users" to "viewers" (which multiplies subscriber accounts by household co-viewing estimates) makes historical comparisons difficult. Bears on r/stocks argue this is a "vanity metric to mask domestic subscriber saturation."

Netflix's growth challenges have also forced it to re-evaluate inorganic expansion. In late 2025, Netflix entered a definitive agreement to acquire Warner Bros. Discovery (WBD) in an $82.7 billion deal. The proposal was highly controversial; investors feared the addition of WBD’s massive debt load, which exceeded $40 billion, would destroy Netflix’s clean balance sheet. The deal collapsed in February 2026 when Paramount Skydance launched a rival $110.9 billion bid. WBD terminated its agreement with Netflix on February 27, 2026, triggering a $2.8 billion breakup fee paid to Netflix by Paramount Skydance on behalf of WBD.

While this cash windfall boosted Netflix’s Q1 2026 net income to $5.28 billion and diluted EPS to $1.23, the core numbers painted a more modest picture. Q1 2026 revenue grew 16.2% year-over-year to $12.25 billion, and operating income rose 18.2% to $3.96 billion, yielding an operating margin of 32.3%. Netflix used the breakup cash to ramp up capital allocation, repurchasing 13.5 million shares for $1.3 billion in Q1. However, the collapse of the WBD deal leaves Netflix without a major legacy library acquisition as the Paramount-Skydance merger proceeds through regulatory reviews under the Trump administration.

To offset the plateau in traditional streaming, Netflix is betting heavily on live events to capture premium TV advertising dollars. The centerpiece of this pivot is a 10-year, $5 billion contract with WWE to stream *Raw* starting in January 2025. Additionally, the company has expanded its NFL Christmas Day package. After streaming doubleheaders on Christmas in 2024 (Chiefs vs. Steelers and Ravens vs. Texans) and 2025 (Cowboys vs. Commanders and Lions vs. Vikings), Netflix signed an extension through the 2029–2030 season. In 2026, the package expands to include a Week 1 game in Australia, a Thanksgiving Eve matchup, and the NFL Honors. Netflix also signed a three-year rights agreement with Major League Baseball starting in 2026 to broadcast Opening Night, the Home Run Derby, and the *Field of Dreams* game.

Yet, this live sports pivot brings significant execution risk. During the live broadcast of the Jake Paul vs. Mike Tyson boxing match on November 15, 2024, which drew a peak of 65 million viewers, Netflix suffered widespread streaming failures. Over 85,000 users logged complaints on Down Detector citing lag, buffering, and total feed outages. On Fintwit, traders continue to question Netflix's infrastructure capability: "If they can't stream a single boxing match without crashing, how are they going to manage weekly live WWE Raw or multiple NFL games?" 

Production and licensing costs for live events also threaten to compress operating margins, which are targeted at 31.5% for the full year 2026. As Netflix prepares to report on July 16, the core question is whether its high-cost pivot to live sports and ad-supported tiers can restart its historical growth engine, or if the market has correctly priced NFLX as a mature, lower-multiple utility.
