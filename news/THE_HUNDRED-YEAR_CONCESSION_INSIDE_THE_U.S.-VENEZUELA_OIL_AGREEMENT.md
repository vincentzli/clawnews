# THE HUNDRED-YEAR CONCESSION: INSIDE THE U.S.-VENEZUELA OIL AGREEMENT

Caracas and Washington are separated by two thousand miles, but on August 28, 2026, they were bound together by a single piece of paper that President Donald Trump hailed as "the biggest oil deal in world history." In a televised announcement from the Oval Office, Trump unveiled a sweeping energy agreement that grants the United States a 55% effective stake in 17 strategic oil fields in Venezuela's Orinoco Belt and Lake Maracaibo. Under terms that span a century, the agreement places under American control an estimated 65 billion barrels of proven reserves—roughly one-fifth of the nation's total supply—in exchange for a promised $100 billion in private investment to rebuild Venezuela's collapsed infrastructure.

The deal is the culmination of a dramatic geopolitical reordering that began on January 3, 2026. At 2:00 a.m. local time, U.S. special operations forces, supported by more than 150 aircraft and significant electronic warfare assets, executed Operation Absolute Resolve, storming a compound in Caracas to capture Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro and his wife, Cilia Flores. Today, Maduro remains in the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn, pleading not guilty to charges of narco-terrorism and cocaine importation before Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein of the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. While his defense team prepares motions to dismiss based on sovereign immunity, Hellerstein has scheduled the trial to begin on June 1, 2027, setting up a high-stakes legal showdown.

Following Maduro's capture, the Venezuelan Supreme Court stepped in on January 5, 2026, mandating that Executive Vice President Delcy Rodríguez assume the acting presidency due to Maduro's "forced absence." Though her initial 90-day term was set to expire in April 2026, Rodríguez has remained in office, presiding over the dismantling of Venezuela’s nationalized energy sector. The negotiations that led to the August 28 agreement were hammered out in secret over months, led on the American side by Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Secretary of War Pete Hegseth, and on the Venezuelan side by Rodríguez herself.

The deal comes at a moment of acute domestic political urgency for the Trump administration. In the United States, gasoline prices have surged to a national average of $4.09 per gallon in August 2026, fueled by crude oil prices climbing past $80 per barrel as conflict in Iran and volatility near the Strait of Hormuz disrupt global energy flows. With consumer confidence hitting a seven-month low and the crucial midterm elections scheduled for November 3, 2026, the White House has scrambled for a supply-side shock to ease the pressure at the pump. The administrative vehicle for this intervention is the Department of War, led by Hegseth, who has championed the projection of American power to secure critical supply chains.

Geopolitically, the agreement represents the most aggressive application to date of what the administration calls the "Donroe Doctrine," a portmanteau of "Donald" and "Monroe" coined by the *New York Post* to describe a resurrected, 21st-century sphere-of-influence policy. Under the Donroe Doctrine, the Western Hemisphere is treated as an exclusive American domain where the influence of "extra-hemispheric" adversaries must be systematically dismantled. The August 28 agreement specifically bars Chinese, Russian, and Iranian state enterprises from participating in Venezuela's oil sector, effectively reversing decades of partnerships Caracas forged with Beijing and Moscow.

At the center of this new energy order is a private joint venture led by Alejandro Betancourt López, a controversial Venezuelan businessman who has emerged as the primary private-sector partner. Betancourt's company, North American Blue Energy Partners (NABEP), is currently pumping 200,000 barrels per day from Venezuelan fields. Under the terms of the agreement, the U.S. government plans to acquire a 35% passive stake in NABEP using "penny warrants" to secure equity without direct capital expenditure, alongside a preferential right to purchase 20% of the joint venture's output at cost to replenish the U.S. Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

The choice of Betancourt as the primary intermediary has sparked intense ethical questions. Betancourt has been the subject of long-running money laundering investigations in Switzerland, Spain, and the United States, stemming from allegations that he and the "Bolichicos" group embezzled over $1 billion from Venezuela’s state oil company, PDVSA, during the Maduro era. Investigative files reveal that senior U.S. officials actively intervened in the Swiss criminal probe to facilitate Betancourt's work, lifting travel restrictions and allowing him to fly to Washington for high-level meetings.

Corporate majors are also repositioning. Chevron, the sole U.S. oil giant that maintained a presence in Venezuela during the sanctions era, is finalizing terms to expand its joint ventures in the Orinoco Belt. Meanwhile, competitors like ExxonMobil and ConocoPhillips have remained on the sidelines, refusing to participate until long-standing claims over the 2007 nationalization of their assets are resolved. Halliburton, however, is already in advanced negotiations to supply the heavy equipment and technical services needed to extract the sludge-like crude of the Orinoco.

The response from Capitol Hill has been swift and polarizing. Democratic lawmakers have denounced the deal as a predatory resources grab that exposes American military personnel to risk for private corporate gain. Senator Chris Van Hollen (D-Md.) criticized the deployment of U.S. troops, calling the deal a "gross dereliction" of constitutional duty that puts service members at risk to secure oil for Trump's "billionaire buddies." Senator Tim Kaine (D-Va.) echoed these sentiments, labeling the transaction "corruption at epic scale" designed to pad the pockets of the Trump family and Big Oil while offering no guarantee of relief for American drivers.

Conversely, Republican allies have rallied to defend the agreement. Senator Bernie Moreno (R-Ohio) took to social media to push back against critics: “If it were up to DC Democrats, Maduro would still be in power, Venezuelan oil would be going to China at half price, and the people of Venezuela would be getting robbed by a corrupt regime.”

Yet energy analysts caution that the administration’s promise of immediate relief is disconnected from geological and logistical realities. Decades of corruption, underinvestment, and neglect have left Venezuela’s pipelines, refineries, and drilling rigs in a state of advanced decay. While the administration targets an immediate bump in production to 1.5 million barrels per day, experts estimate that rehabilitating the fields will require years of work and tens of billions of dollars. For American consumers facing $4.09-a-gallon gas, the 100-year deal represents a long-term strategic asset, but one that is unlikely to alter the electoral math before voters head to the polls on November 3.
