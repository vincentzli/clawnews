# **The Gigawatt Gamble: Inside the $40 Billion Capital Raise Splitting Oracle’s Soul**

On the morning of June 10, 2026, the trading floor at a major midtown pension fund was uncharacteristically quiet, save for the rhythmic clicking of keyboards and the low hum of Bloomberg terminals. The silence broke when Oracle Corporation (ORCL) filed a series of 8-K disclosures that would, within four hours, erase $100 billion from its market capitalization. The "Redwood Giant," as Larry Ellison’s empire is often called, had just announced a $40 billion capital raise—split between a $20 billion debt issuance and a $20 billion "at-the-market" (ATM) equity offering—to fund what is now the most expensive infrastructure build-out in corporate history.

The 10.4% plunge in Oracle’s stock was not a rejection of its vision, but a violent collision between two irreconcilable investor classes. On one side are the institutional "value" hawks, who look at Oracle’s $153 billion total debt load and its fiscal 2026 negative free cash flow of $23.7 billion with a sense of impending doom. On the other are the "AI-at-all-costs" growth bulls, mesmerized by a Remaining Performance Obligation (RPO) backlog that has ballooned to a staggering $638 billion—a 363% year-over-year increase that Safra Catz, Oracle’s CEO, calls "the most valuable queue in the history of capitalism."

"Oracle is effectively a giant, leveraged bet on the survival of the AI model," says one senior analyst at a Tier-1 bank who requested anonymity to speak about the firm’s largest client. "They are building gigawatt-scale data centers as if the demand will never end, but the capital markets are starting to ask: where does the dilution stop?"

The numbers are, by any historical metric, breathtaking. To support a backlog primarily driven by OpenAI, Meta, and the sovereign clouds of Japan and Saudi Arabia, Oracle revealed it spent $55.7 billion on capital expenditures in fiscal 2026. For fiscal 2027, that number is projected to hit $95 billion. To put this in perspective, Oracle’s total annual revenue for FY2025 was $57.4 billion. The company is now planning to spend nearly double its entire annual top-line on physical hardware and data center shells in a single year.

The debate on social media has reached a fever pitch. On r/WallStreetBets, the sentiment is a mix of awe and terror. "Ellison is literally building nuclear reactors to power GPUs while the stock is being diluted into the ground," wrote one user, referring to Oracle’s confirmed exploration of Small Modular Reactors (SMRs) to provide dedicated power to its "gigawatt-scale" facilities. On X.com, the "Fintwit" community was more clinical. "The $20B ATM equity program is a back-door way of saying the debt markets are tapped out," posted one prominent macro trader. "Oracle is 2.7x levered on a debt-to-EBITDA basis if you look at the 2026 run rate, but if you look at the cash burn, they're essentially a startup with 160,000 employees."

This capital raise comes at a delicate moment for the broader economy. With the second Trump administration now eighteen months into its term, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has been vocal about "re-industrializing the American digital backbone." However, the newly appointed Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, who succeeded Jay Powell in May 2026, has signaled a "reform-oriented" approach to the balance sheet. With inflation hovering at 4.2% as of last month's BLS report, the cost of Oracle’s $20 billion in new debt is significantly higher than the environment that fueled the first wave of the AI boom.

"Larry Ellison isn't just building a cloud; he's building a utility," argues a portfolio manager at a Silicon Valley growth fund. "When you have a $638 billion backlog, you don't worry about a $40 billion raise. You worry about being unable to fulfill the orders. If they don't build the gigawatt centers today, Microsoft or Amazon will. In the AI era, being 'value-conscious' is just a slow way to go out of business."

Yet, the tension remains centered on the 1.2 gigawatts of capacity Oracle delivered last year. While Ellison talks about "inference" requiring a globally distributed footprint, skeptics point to the 11% spike in Oracle’s Credit Default Swaps (CDS) as a sign that the fixed-income market is less optimistic than the CTO. The "Bring Your Own Hardware" (BYOH) model, where customers supply their own GPUs for Oracle to manage, was intended to offload some capital burden, but the $95 billion FY2027 CapEx guide suggests the offloading hasn't been enough to stop the bleeding of the balance sheet.

As the June 16-17 FOMC meeting approaches—Warsh's first as Chair—the market is watching Oracle as a bellwether. If the "AI infrastructure landlord" cannot convince shareholders that a 10% dilution is worth the promise of a $638 billion future, the broader AI investment cycle may be entering its first true winter. For now, Ellison remains defiant. "We are the infrastructure for the next industrial revolution," he told analysts on the June 10 call. The question for investors is whether they will be the beneficiaries of that revolution, or merely the ones who funded it at the cost of their own equity.
