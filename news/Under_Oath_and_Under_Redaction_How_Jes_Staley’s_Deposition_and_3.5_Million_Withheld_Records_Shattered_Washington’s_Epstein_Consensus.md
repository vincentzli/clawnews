# **Under Oath and Under Redaction: How Jes Staley’s Deposition and 3.5 Million Withheld Records Shattered Washington’s Epstein Consensus**

####

On August 26, 2026, when the House Committee on Oversight and Government Reform published the full 236-page transcript of Jes Staley’s closed-door deposition, it did more than expose the inner sanctum of Wall Street’s most toxic client relationship. It triggered a high-stakes constitutional collision between Capitol Hill, the nation’s largest financial institution, and the Department of Justice.

The deposition—conducted on July 24, 2026, under bipartisan questioning—marks the first time Staley, the former chief of JPMorgan Chase’s investment bank and subsequent chief executive of Barclays, answered under oath before Congress about the institutional machinery that accommodated Jeffrey Epstein for more than a decade. Staley admitted to exchanging approximately 1,200 emails with Epstein between 2008 and 2012, visiting Epstein’s private Caribbean island, Little St. James, three times, and frequenting his East 71st Street Manhattan townhouse dozens of times. Furthermore, Staley testified that he shared market-sensitive internal JPMorgan data, including pending acquisition details and high-level communications with the Federal Reserve during the height of the 2008 financial crisis, with Epstein while the financier was serving an 18-month county jail sentence in Florida for procuring a minor for prostitution.

The most legally consequential revelation in the transcript centers on a direct evidentiary conflict at the apex of American banking. Staley testified under penalty of perjury that he specifically discussed Epstein with JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon on two separate occasions: first following Epstein’s 2006 Florida indictment, and again after his 2008 conviction. Staley maintained that he personally advocated to preserve Epstein’s lucrative accounts, telling congressional investigators that within private wealth management, "closing a checking account is a tough move," while asserting he remained "completely unaware" that the accounts were facilitating sex trafficking.

That sworn testimony directly contradicts the May 2023 sworn deposition of Jamie Dimon, who testified in federal civil litigation that he had never met Epstein, never communicated with him, had no recollection of internal discussions regarding Epstein’s accounts, and was unaware of Epstein’s client status until his July 2019 arrest. JPMorgan Chase representatives immediately issued a statement disputing Staley’s account, stating that no records or internal communications corroborate the alleged meetings and citing a 2023 U.K. Upper Tribunal finding that raised severe doubts regarding Staley’s credibility during proceedings that banned him from senior executive roles in British finance.

The release of the Staley transcript arrived in the midst of an escalating federal oversight battle. On August 31, 2026, Representative Thomas Massie (R-KY) addressed the House floor, publicly naming 14 prominent associates—including Staley, billionaire investor Leon Black, retail magnate Les Wexner, and Hyatt Hotels chairman Tom Pritzker—as individuals who warrant immediate federal investigation. Massie simultaneously launched a discharge petition to bypass committee bottlenecks and force a floor vote on the *Epstein Files Transparency Act II* (H.R. 9694), a bipartisan measure introduced in July 2026 alongside Representative Ro Khanna (D-CA).

The legislative push reflects mounting congressional frustration with the Department of Justice’s handling of the original *Epstein Files Transparency Act* (Public Law 119-38), enacted on November 19, 2025, after passing the House by a 427–1 margin and clearing the Senate by unanimous consent. The statute mandated that the Attorney General publish all unclassified records related to the investigation and prosecution of Epstein and Ghislaine Maxwell in a searchable, downloadable repository within 30 days. Although the Department of Justice reported releasing approximately 3.5 million pages of material across the Federal Bureau of Investigation, the Bureau of Prisons (BOP), and the U.S. Attorney’s Office for the Southern District of New York (SDNY) by January 30, 2026, lawmakers across both parties have characterized the production as severely compromised.

Federal document processors applied blanket redactions under Freedom of Information Act Exemptions 6 and 7(C), withholding the names and activities of prominent third parties and commercial entities under the rationale of personal privacy, despite the statutory mandate requiring complete public disclosure outside narrow survivor-protection exemptions. Simultaneously, the rollout suffered major administrative breaches: in February 2026, the DOJ was compelled to retract roughly 9,500 documents from its public portal after privacy advocates revealed that processors had inadvertently published the unredacted personally identifiable information (PII), medical histories, and residential addresses of nearly 100 survivors. The fallout led to the introduction of the REDACT Act, intended to establish financial liability and compensation mechanisms for survivors impacted by government data disclosures.

These compliance failures prompted the Department of Justice Office of the Inspector General (DOJ OIG), led by Inspector General Michael Horowitz, to formally initiate an audit on April 23, 2026. The OIG inquiry is evaluating the criteria used by the DOJ to collect, redact, and withhold records, while investigating why an estimated 64,000 files—including raw FBI 302 interview memos and SDNY prosecutorial workbooks—were temporarily removed or remain absent from the public archive.

Congressional committees reviewing the OIG’s initial findings have concentrated on unreleased administrative records and missing chain-of-custody documentation from the Metropolitan Correctional Center (MCC) in Manhattan regarding August 9 and 10, 2019, when Epstein died in the Special Housing Unit (SHU). While the OIG’s June 2023 investigative report documented systemic operational failures—including chronic understaffing, unperformed inmate counts, and falsified duty logs by correctional officers—lawmakers have demanded the unreleased digital video recording (DVR) files covering the 9 South tier stairwell and the complete supervisory communications regarding the removal of Epstein’s cellmate shortly before his death, materials the DOJ has continued to withhold under ongoing administrative privilege assertions.

With Representative Massie’s discharge petition seeking the 218 signatures required to force H.R. 9694 to the House floor, the Epstein oversight inquiry has transformed into a fundamental challenge to executive secrecy. If enacted, the legislation will grant state attorneys general, victims, and members of Congress direct civil standing in federal court to compel the release of unredacted records, setting the stage for an unprecedented judicial review of the government’s sealed archives.
