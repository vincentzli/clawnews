# The Back-Channel Borderlands: Inside the Secret U.S.-Mediated Security Talks Between Israel and the New Syrian State

On July 26, 2026, Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa sat before an Al Jazeera camera in Damascus and confirmed what regional intelligence agencies had reported for months: the newly established Syrian government is actively negotiating a security agreement with Israel. Speaking with the posture of a statesman rather than the militant commander who once went by the nom de guerre Abu Mohammad al-Julani, al-Sharaa described the negotiations as a necessary initiative to avoid military confrontation and establish a "gateway to comprehensive peace." Yet, as he spoke, the political and military realities of the Middle East loomed large. The talks, mediated by the United States under the Trump administration, have highlighted divergent strategic priorities between Syria’s demand for territorial sovereignty and Israel’s insistence on border security.

The geopolitical landscape of the Levant was altered on December 8, 2024, when a rebel offensive led by the Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS) coalition swept into Damascus, ending 53 years of Assad family rule. In the ensuing power vacuum, the Israeli military executed a preemptive move. Citing the collapse of the Syrian state and the risk of hostile forces filling the void, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu ordered the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) to advance across the "Alpha Line" and occupy the United Nations-monitored demilitarized buffer zone established by the 1974 Disengagement of Forces Agreement. By seizing control of strategic high points, including Mount Hermon and outposts like Tel al-Hara, Israel established a defensive line inside Syrian territory, restricting the operations of the United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF).

While Netanyahu initially framed the deployment as a temporary defensive measure, Israel's posture soon hardened. In March 2025, Israeli Defense Minister Israel Katz announced that the IDF was prepared to hold the security area in the Mount Hermon region and the broader buffer zone in southern Syria for an "unlimited amount of time" to ensure it remained demilitarized. Since then, the IDF has maintained its presence, establishing new military posts on the Syrian side of the border. For the transitional government in Damascus, this ongoing presence represents a violation of national sovereignty that hinders post-war reconstruction and complicates al-Sharaa’s efforts to build domestic legitimacy.

The United States has served as the primary mediator for these discussions. Seeking to manage the regional transition and counter Iranian influence, President Donald Trump appointed Tom Barrack as Ambassador to Turkey and Special Envoy to Syria in May 2025. In May 2026, Barrack’s mandate was expanded to Special Presidential Envoy for Syria and Iraq, placing him at the center of U.S. diplomatic policy in the region. Working alongside Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Barrack has facilitated back-channel diplomacy, including secret meetings between Syrian and Israeli officials in Paris in January 2026.

These talks focused on a preliminary framework for border stability, including provisions for intelligence-sharing to prevent the re-emergence of Islamic State (ISIS) pockets and remnants of Iranian proxies. In April 2026, speaking at the Antalya Diplomacy Forum, Barrack expressed optimism, suggesting that a non-aggression and normalization agreement between Syria and Israel could be achieved. He noted that the new Syrian government had signaled a willingness to communicate and avoid direct military confrontation.

However, the negotiations have faced significant obstacles as the two sides attempt to reconcile opposing positions. Al-Sharaa’s administration demands a complete Israeli withdrawal from the 1974 buffer zone as a condition for a security agreement. Furthermore, al-Sharaa has emphasized that Syria will not abandon its historical claim to the Golan Heights, which Israel annexed in 1981—a move recognized by the United States during Trump’s first term but rejected by Damascus.

For Israel, withdrawing from the occupied buffer zone remains a sensitive domestic issue. The Netanyahu government, facing legislative elections on October 27, 2026, faces domestic pressure regarding concessions to a Syrian leader with past militant associations. Israeli intelligence officials maintain skepticism regarding al-Sharaa’s ideological transition, viewing his diplomatic pragmatism as a strategic step to secure the removal of U.S. sanctions and obtain international recognition. Defense Minister Katz has maintained that Israel will not alter its defensive posture on Mount Hermon without robust, verifiable guarantees that southern Syria will remain free of hostile forces.

On public forums and specialized analysis communities such as Reddit's r/syriancivilwar, observers have debated the reported parameters of these negotiations. Discussions have centered on claims that Israel is seeking to maintain control over strategic outposts like Tel al-Hara and parts of Mount Hermon, alongside demands for aerial freedom of operation in Syrian airspace and strict limits on Syrian military deployments in the southern provinces. Analysts participating in these online discussions note that such terms present a severe political risk for al-Sharaa, as accepting them could provoke backlash from internal opposition factions and regional actors opposed to normalization.

As August 2026 continues, the back-channel negotiations remain stalled over these core issues. While the U.S. continues to pursue diplomatic arrangements to stabilize the region, the gap between Syria's demand for sovereignty and Israel's insistence on unilateral security guarantees remains unbridged.
