# The 34-Seat Chokepoint: Inside Erdoğan’s Constitutional Gambit for a Third Term

On September 17, 2026, Mehmet Uçum, Chief Presidential Adviser and Deputy Chair of the Presidential Legal Policies Council, laid bare the legislative and constitutional roadmap designed to enable President Recep Tayyip Erdoğan to seek an additional presidential mandate. Speaking in a national television broadcast, Uçum designated an exact date for Turkey’s next general and presidential elections: April 16, 2028. The timing was selected with deliberate precision. April 16 marks the anniversary of the 2017 constitutional referendum that narrowly dismantled Turkey’s century-old parliamentary system and replaced it with the current executive presidential model.

Behind the symbolic date lies a tightly constrained constitutional framework. Under Article 101 of the Turkish Constitution, a president is limited to serving a maximum of two five-year terms. Having been elected to the executive presidency in 2018 and re-elected in May 2023, Erdoğan is constitutionally barred from running when his current tenure expires in May 2028. 

The presidency's legal advisers have centered their strategy entirely on Article 116, clause 3 of the Constitution. This clause establishes an explicit exception: if the Grand National Assembly of Turkey (TBMM) votes to renew elections during an incumbent’s second term, the sitting president is permitted to run for an "exceptional candidacy" (*istisnai adaylık*). However, this privilege is conditional on parliamentary action. Should Erdoğan dissolve parliament unilaterally using presidential decree powers under Article 116, clause 2, he is disqualified from seeking re-election. The renewal must originate from the legislative branch.

By proposing to advance the scheduled May 7, 2028 election by three weeks to April 16, Uçum sought to categorize the vote not as a standard early election, but as a technical parliamentary renewal that satisfies the constitutional requirement for Erdoğan's candidacy. "This is not an early election in the classical sense," Uçum stated, characterizing the proposal as a controlled legislative renewal aligned with national security continuity and state policy objectives.

Yet this legal architecture faces an immediate parliamentary barrier. Enacting a decision to renew elections under Article 116 demands a three-fifths supermajority of the 600-seat assembly—requiring 360 votes. The governing People's Alliance (*Cumhur İttifakı*) lacks the numbers. Erdoğan's Justice and Development Party (AKP) holds 280 seats, while its nationalist coalition partner, Devlet Bahçeli’s Nationalist Movement Party (MHP), controls 46 seats. Together, the bloc commands 326 votes, leaving the administration 34 seats short of the required threshold.

Devlet Bahçeli has endorsed the initiative, reaffirming that Erdoğan is the alliance’s "sole option" (*tek seçenek*) for the 2028 race and stating that an election advanced by weeks carries no substantive difference from a regularly scheduled vote. The parliamentary opposition, however, has closed ranks against granting procedural clearance.

Republican People’s Party (CHP) Chairman Özgür Özel rejected Uçum's framework in an address to party officials. "Without 360 parliamentarians deciding, what they are proposing cannot occur," Özel said. "They cannot bypass the constitutional limit by moving the election forward by a matter of weeks and defining it as an early election." Özel underscored that the CHP’s previous willingness to back an early election applied strictly to the midpoint of the presidential term in late 2025, declaring that the main opposition will not provide the votes required to facilitate a third term for the incumbent.

The CHP’s posture reflects its strengthened position following the March 31, 2024 local elections. In that contest, the CHP garnered 37.7% of the nationwide vote against the AKP’s 35.5%, marking the first time in twenty-two years that Erdoğan's party finished second. The opposition maintained and expanded control over the country's most populous metropolitan areas, including Istanbul, Ankara, Izmir, Bursa, and Antalya.

Istanbul Mayor Ekrem İmamoğlu, regarded as the opposition's principal contender for the presidency, continues to operate under ongoing judicial scrutiny. İmamoğlu is currently contesting an appeal against a 2022 conviction carrying a prison sentence of two years and seven months and an associated political ban for describing election officials as "fools" following the annulled 2019 Istanbul vote. Additional investigations into municipal procurement have drawn criticism from domestic and international legal observers, who view the proceedings as an attempt to neutralize Erdoğan's primary political challenger through the courts.

The legislative arithmetic is further constrained by persistent economic headwinds. Despite Treasury and Finance Minister Mehmet Şimşek and Central Bank Governor Fatih Karahan implementing orthodox monetary measures—anchoring the benchmark policy rate at 50%—annual inflation remains elevated near 50%, down from a peak of 75.45% in May 2024. The prolonged contraction in real household income, depressed purchasing power, and stagnant pensions continue to fuel voter dissatisfaction, eroding the ruling party's traditional working-class base.

With the CHP controlling over 120 seats and refusing concessions, the ruling coalition’s mathematical options narrow to the pro-Kurdish Peoples' Equality and Democracy Party (DEM Party), which commands 56 seats. In tandem with Uçum’s constitutional remarks, government representatives introduced discourse concerning a "Terror-Free Turkey" (*Terörsüz Türkiye*) framework, generating discussion over whether the ruling bloc might seek legislative engagement with Kurdish parliamentarians. However, DEM Party officials have signaled deep skepticism, pointing to the ongoing removal of elected Kurdish mayors in favor of state-appointed trustees (*kayyum*) and the continued imprisonment of former party leader Selahattin Demirtaş.

The institutional deadlock over Article 116 has intensified attention on potential succession within the AKP. Two individuals dominate internal calculations should Erdoğan be unable to secure a ballot position: Foreign Minister Hakan Fidan, formerly the long-serving head of the National Intelligence Organization (MİT), who holds extensive influence within the state bureaucracy; and Selçuk Bayraktar, chairman and chief technology officer of Baykar, who is also Erdoğan's son-in-law.

Bayraktar, whose military drone systems have become an emblem of national industrial achievement, addressed inquiries regarding his political trajectory in an interview with The Wall Street Journal, stating: "Maybe I would say yes, but that will depend on the circumstances." While Fidan represents institutional and diplomatic continuity, Bayraktar’s high public standing across nationalist and conservative demographics positions him as a strong contender if dynastic succession becomes necessary.

On digital networks, Uçum's proposal has provoked sharp legal and political commentary. Across X.com, constitutional scholars and analysts noted that condensing an election cycle by several weeks solely to activate an incumbent exception strains constitutional intent. On Reddit's r/Turkey, an analysis of the parliamentary numbers observed: "The Palace knows they cannot amend the constitution without 360 votes, so they are attempting to rebrand May 2028 as an early election in April to bypass Article 101." Another widely circulated assessment noted: "Erdoğan is caught between Şimşek's interest rate medicine and the ballot box; delaying the vote only makes the internal succession struggle more volatile."

As long as the opposition maintains its refusal to supply the missing 34 votes, the administration's constitutional maneuvers remain contained. Without a parliamentary supermajority or an unexpected realignment before the end of 2026, Erdoğan’s path to a third executive presidency remains constrained by the very constitutional boundaries his administration established in 2017.
