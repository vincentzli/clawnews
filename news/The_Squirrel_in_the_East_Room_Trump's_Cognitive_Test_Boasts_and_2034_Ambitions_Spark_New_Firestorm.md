# The Squirrel in the East Room: Trump's Cognitive Test Boasts and 2034 Ambitions Spark New Firestorm

On the morning of May 4, 2026, the East Room of the White House was prepared for what was billed as a standard, policy-focused celebration of National Small Business Week. President Donald Trump was scheduled to address a friendly audience of business leaders, corporate executives, and entrepreneurs, highlighting his administration’s economic agenda. The official run-of-show indicated a speech centered on recent legislative pushes, specifically tax cuts on tipped wages, overtime pay, and Social Security benefits, as well as foreign policy updates regarding "Project Freedom" in the Strait of Hormuz and the rejection of recent peace proposals from Iran. Instead, the Small Business Summit shifted to an off-script address in which the 79-year-old president extensively discussed his cognitive health records, casually dismissed the United States Constitution's term limits, and inadvertently invented a new version of a standard medical exam.

The pivot from domestic economic policy and Middle Eastern geopolitics to personal medical history was abrupt and jarring. In the midst of his remarks regarding tax incentives, Trump initiated an extended defense of his mental acuity, boasting that he had taken three cognitive examinations during his time in public life—one during his first administration and two more recently. "I aced each one," he assured the audience of business owners. To provide evidence of his unparalleled intellect, the current President attempted to reconstruct the visual identification portion of the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a widely utilized clinical tool in neurology.

"The first question is very easy," Trump explained to the crowd, seemingly losing the thread of his economic address. "You have a lion, a bear, an alligator, and a—what’s another good—a squirrel, OK? Which is the squirrel?"

Within minutes, the "squirrel" comment had saturated social media and cable news networks, primarily because the president’s description of the test was entirely fictitious. The standard version of the MoCA does not feature a bear, an alligator, or a squirrel. Rather, patients are typically presented with line drawings of a lion, a rhinoceros, and a camel.

Medical professionals and neurologists immediately weighed in on the president's claims. The MoCA, created by Dr. Ziad Nasreddine, is not a standard intelligence test or a Mensa entry exam. It is a highly specific, ten-minute screening instrument designed to detect early signs of Alzheimer’s disease, dementia, and other forms of cognitive decline. Neurologists stress that "acing" the MoCA does not signify genius; it merely establishes that a patient possesses a normal, baseline level of cognitive function. Furthermore, cognitive specialists point out that taking the MoCA three times is not a standard preventative measure for healthy adults. Repeated administration of the test is typically reserved for patients whose physicians are actively monitoring them for progressive, longitudinal cognitive deterioration.

Trump's inability to accurately recall the contents of a test he claims proves his perfect memory represents an irony that was not lost on his critics. This is not the first time his recollection of the MoCA has shifted; in previous iterations of this narrative over the years, critics noted that he has claimed the test required him to identify entirely different sets of animals, including giraffes, whales, and sharks.

Despite his own fluid memory of the exam, Trump used the Small Business Summit to demand new electoral standards. "I think every person running for president should have to take a cognitive test," he declared from the podium. "I really do." This call for mandatory testing of 2028 presidential hopefuls aligns with his established political strategy. By making cognitive fitness a central campaign issue, he aims to preemptively disqualify potential rivals, a tactic he frequently deployed against former President Joe Biden during previous election cycles.

However, the cognitive test discourse was arguably overshadowed by comments Trump made regarding the physical infrastructure of the White House itself. While discussing planned, large-scale renovations to the executive mansion—which he detailed as including a new grand ballroom and an expansive underground military complex beneath it—Trump offered a timeline for his departure that conflicts with the 22nd Amendment of the U.S. Constitution.

"When I get out of office in, let's say, eight or nine years from now, I'll be able to use it... I'll be able to use it for myself," Trump stated.

The mathematics of his statement sent immediate shockwaves through political circles. Given that the summit took place in May 2026, roughly a year and a half into his second term, an additional "eight or nine years" would extend his presidency to 2034 or 2035. This casual assertion implies a third and potentially fourth term in office, acting in direct defiance of the Constitution, which strictly limits a president to two elected terms.

The public reaction to the May 4 speech fractured into two distinct lanes across the internet: mockery of the cognitive test claims and genuine alarm regarding the term limit threats. Online platforms were flooded with debates regarding the president's mental fitness, driven by the viral clips of his speech. Critics and online communities dissected the biological impossibility of his MoCA claims, pointing out the absurdity of swapping standard savanna animals for a woodland squirrel.

Meanwhile, political commentators and forum users focused heavily on the constitutional implications of his renovation timeline. The discourse centered on how the president was openly floating the idea of remaining in power far beyond 2028, using discussions about architectural blueprints to mask his ambitions for a third or fourth term. The viral nature of the event reignited widespread speculation regarding the frequency with which the 79-year-old president is being administered cognitive screenings, and exactly what his medical team might be searching for.

The May 4 Small Business Summit encapsulates the dual focus of the current administration's messaging. In a single hour, the President demanded mandatory neurological testing for his political opponents while failing to accurately describe the specific test he cited, and telegraphed an intent to challenge the constitutional limits of his office. As the nation inches closer to the 2028 election cycle, these off-script moments establish the parameters of the upcoming campaign, ensuring that both cognitive fitness and the enforcement of the 22nd Amendment will be central to the political discourse.
