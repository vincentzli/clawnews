# The Pragmatist of Damascus: Inside Ahmed al-Sharaa’s High-Stakes Gamble for a Security Deal with Israel

On July 26, 2026, Ali Al-Dhafiri, the host of Al Jazeera’s flagship interview program *Al Muqabala*, sat across from Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa in Damascus. For ninety minutes, the man who once operated in the shadows under the nom de guerre Abu Mohammad al-Julani laid out a startling vision for the future of the Middle East. The central revelation of the interview sent shockwaves through regional capitals: Damascus is actively negotiating a security agreement with Israel through international intermediaries. Al-Sharaa, the former Islamist insurgent leader who led the coalition that overthrew Bashar al-Assad in December 2024 and assumed the presidency in January 2025, framed the negotiations not as a surrender, but as a pragmatic necessity. "We are working to avoid military confrontations," al-Sharaa explained, adding that direct clashes are "not in Syria's interest" and that a localized security arrangement could serve as a "gateway to a comprehensive peace."

This diplomatic high-wire act represents the culmination of a rapid, U.S.-backed effort to rehabilitate Syria’s international standing. Since taking office, al-Sharaa has systematically dismantled the old "Axis of Resistance" structures that bound Damascus to Tehran and Hezbollah. The groundwork was laid in January 2026, when senior Syrian and Israeli delegations met secretly in Paris to establish a joint intelligence-sharing and deconfliction channel. Brokered and supervised by the United States, this "fusion mechanism" was designed to coordinate border security, prevent accidental military clashes, and share intelligence to actively counter Iranian-backed remnants and Hezbollah operatives attempting to exploit the post-Assad vacuum.

The diplomatic rehabilitation of the former rebel leader has moved at a pace that few regional analysts predicted. In May 2025, al-Sharaa met U.S. President Donald Trump in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, marking their first face-to-face encounter. By November 2025, al-Sharaa was standing in the White House—the first visit by a Syrian president since 1946—securing rapid sanctions relief. Most recently, on July 8, 2026, al-Sharaa met President Trump on the sidelines of the NATO summit in Ankara, Turkey. During the summit, Trump publicly praised al-Sharaa’s efforts to unify the country and announced plans to formally remove Syria from the U.S. State Sponsors of Terrorism list. 

Adding to this diplomatic momentum, UN Secretary-General António Guterres arrived in Damascus on July 25, 2026, marking the first visit by a UN chief to the Syrian capital since 2009. Guterres’s high-profile meetings with al-Sharaa focused on humanitarian cooperation, economic reconstruction, and the political transition. Crucially, Guterres offered international backing to al-Sharaa’s border claims, criticizing Israeli military maneuvers in the buffer zone and declaring the status quo "totally unacceptable."

Despite these diplomatic milestones, the path to a formalized security treaty remains blocked by major historical and territorial roadblocks. The first is Syria’s non-negotiable claim to the Golan Heights, the strategic plateau captured by Israel in 1967 and annexed in 1981. During his Al Jazeera interview, al-Sharaa was careful to reassure his domestic constituency and regional allies that any security arrangement would not compromise Syria’s legal sovereignty over the Golan. 

The second, more immediate obstacle is the military reality on the ground. Immediately following the collapse of the Assad regime in December 2024, Israeli forces advanced past the 1974 Disengagement of Forces lines, occupying strategic military positions inside the demilitarized buffer zone. Israel defended the deployment as a "preemptive defensive measure" to prevent jihadist elements from reaching its border. However, Israel has since reinforced these positions with permanent barriers, early-warning sensors, and troop deployments, refusing Syrian demands to withdraw back to the pre-December 2024 lines.

Adding fuel to the fire, far-right Israeli settler groups have sought to exploit the military presence in southern Syria. A fringe religious-nationalist movement founded in early 2025, calling themselves the "Pioneers of Bashan" (*Halutzei HaBashan*), has staged multiple cross-border incursions. Operating under the ideology that southwestern Syria is part of the biblical Land of Israel, the group has repeatedly cut through border fences with angle grinders to establish unauthorized outposts. In August 2025, they attempted to lay a foundation stone for a settlement named *Neveh HaBashan* in the Quneitra countryside, and in November 2025, they entered the Syrian village of Bir Ajam. While the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) have routinely detained these activists and returned them to Israel, the incursions have sparked intense anger in Damascus and deep concern among UN observers.

Inside Israel’s defense establishment, skepticism regarding al-Sharaa’s transitional administration remains exceptionally high. Senior IDF officials point to al-Sharaa's past as the leader of Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham, an organization with historical roots in Al-Qaeda, arguing that a transitional government built on a coalition of former rebel factions is fundamentally unstable. "A handshake in Paris cannot erase decades of Islamist militancy," remarked one retired Israeli intelligence officer in a sentiment echoed widely across Israeli security blogs. 

This deep-seated skepticism is mirrored in Washington. While the Trump administration has championed al-Sharaa as a pragmatic alternative to Iranian influence, internal U.S. diplomatic assessments obtained by regional observers estimate the chances of full normalization between Syria and Israel in the near term as extremely low. A classified State Department memorandum circulated mid-month noted that while the "fusion mechanism" is functional for deconfliction, domestic political constraints in both Jerusalem and Damascus make a formal peace treaty virtually impossible for the foreseeable future.

On platforms like Reddit’s r/geopolitics and r/neoliberal, as well as X.com, al-Sharaa’s Al Jazeera appearance has triggered a storm of debate. Pragmatic foreign policy analysts argue that Israel is missing a historic window to permanently sever Syria from Iran's orbit. Others remain deeply cynical, pointing out the absurdity of a former U.S.-designated terrorist with a $10 million bounty negotiating border security with Jerusalem while Israeli settlers attempt to colonize the Quneitra countryside. As one online commentator summarized: "We are watching the most bizarre rebranding in modern diplomatic history, but the physical reality of the Golan buffer zone means the war isn't actually over." For now, the "fusion mechanism" remains a quiet, vital valve to prevent a new war, but the grand peace al-Sharaa teased on Al Jazeera remains far out of reach.
