# The 90-Percent Stalemate: Inside the Secret U.S.-Brokered Security Talks Between Jerusalem and Post-Assad Damascus

When Syrian transitional President Ahmad al-Sharaa took the podium at an Atlantic Council forum in New York during the 81st United Nations General Assembly in September 2026, he publicly disclosed what regional intelligence agencies had closely guarded for months: Damascus and Jerusalem had engaged in direct, high-level diplomatic negotiations on a comprehensive border security framework. According to al-Sharaa, the two historical belligerents had "almost reached 90 percent of agreements" before talks stalled over unresolved territorial mandates and ongoing military incursions along the southern frontier.

The impasse is rooted in the strategic topography of the Golan Heights, less than 40 miles southwest of the Syrian capital. Following the fall of Bashar al-Assad’s regime on December 8, 2024, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and Defense Minister Israel Katz deployed the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) into the 235-square-kilometer United Nations Disengagement Observer Force (UNDOF) buffer zone established under the 1974 Disengagement Agreement. Under Operation Arrow of Bashan, Israeli troops captured the peak of Mount Hermon (Jabal al-Shaykh), occupied Syrian forward observation posts, and established fortified positions reaching into the rural environs of Quneitra.

Simultaneously, the Israeli Air Force executed over 500 airstrikes within the first 48 hours of Assad’s ouster, targeting residual heavy weaponry across the country. The strikes scuttled Syrian naval vessels at Latakia and Tartus, destroyed S-200 and S-300 air defense batteries, demolished Scud and Fateh ballistic missile depots, and flattened research facilities belonging to the Scientific Studies and Research Center (SSRC) in Jamraya and Masyaf. Israel Katz explicitly framed the expanded security perimeter as an indefinite reality: "The message to the president of Syria is clear: when you wake up in the morning in the palace in Damascus and look up at Mount Hermon and see the Israeli forces, you know that we are here to defend our communities and our border."

Despite the tactical friction, a backchannel emerged under the mediation of U.S. Ambassador to Turkey and Special Presidential Envoy for Syria Tom Barrack. Operating under the direction of President Donald Trump, Barrack facilitated direct and indirect contacts between Damascus and Jerusalem. In July and August 2025, Barrack hosted secret direct sessions in Paris between Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad Hassan al-Shaibani and Israeli Strategic Affairs Minister Ron Dermer, alongside French diplomatic counterparts. 

Negotiating records show that the Paris talks centered on three primary frameworks: restoring demilitarization parameters along the southwestern border, creating a tripartite "fusion cell" in Amman, Jordan—where Syrian, Israeli, and U.S. officers would share intelligence and de-escalate cross-border incidents—and establishing an internationally monitored joint economic development corridor. Speaking at the Antalya Diplomacy Forum, Barrack publicly expressed optimism that a non-aggression and security pact between Syria and Israel could be finalized "sooner than Lebanon," observing that Damascus had been "brilliant at not engaging adversarially against Israel" despite ongoing frontier operations.

Damascus’s willingness to negotiate stemmed from urgent domestic reconstruction priorities. After 14 years of civil war, Syria faced more than $400 billion in physical devastation, an electrical grid providing fewer than four hours of power per day in major urban centers, and a poverty rate exceeding 90 percent. Al-Sharaa required the lifting of international sanctions to restore the banking sector and attract capital from Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Qatar. 

This diplomatic pivot yielded substantial economic breakthroughs in Washington. Following Executive Order 14312 in June 2025, which initiated the termination of the comprehensive Syria sanctions regime, al-Sharaa conducted an official visit to the White House on November 10, 2025—the first visit by a Syrian head of state since 1946. On December 18, 2025, President Trump signed the FY 2026 National Defense Authorization Act, whose Section 6211 formally repealed the Caesar Syria Civilian Protection Act of 2019. On August 24, 2026, the U.S. State Department officially rescinded Syria’s designation as a State Sponsor of Terrorism.

However, the domestic latitude of the transitional government reached an absolute limit when confronted with Israeli territorial demands. At the Atlantic Council, al-Sharaa emphasized that formalization of any security treaty remains conditional upon an Israeli military withdrawal to the pre-December 2024 lines of the 1974 agreement. "What happened on October 7 should not determine Israeli policy toward Syria. It is not logical, and it creates additional security threats," al-Sharaa stated. Addressing the broader territorial dispute, he added: "The Golan Heights is Syrian territory under recognition of the United Nations… So there is no discussion."

Any compromise on sovereign borders risks dismantling the transitional administration's domestic legitimacy. Syrian civic groups, military factions, and political organizations have mobilized against Israeli incursions, while the Arab League has repeatedly cautioned against Israeli territorial consolidation in Syrian border areas. Friction escalated further when Israeli airstrikes targeted northern installations, including the Abu Duhur airbase, without notifying Washington or Ankara—prompting Barrack to publicly criticize the raids as an "unnecessary escalation that does not advance regional stability."

In Jerusalem, defense officials remain deeply divided. While intelligence strategists argue that a formal non-aggression accord could permanently sever Syria from Iranian influence and secure Israel’s northern theater, senior IDF commanders and right-wing cabinet members maintain that al-Sharaa’s jihadist background requires robust, physical buffer zones rather than diplomatic pledges. Israeli negotiators demanded continued observation rights on the heights of Mount Hermon, sweeping demilitarization across southern Syria encompassing Daraa and Suwayda, and operational freedom to intercept emergent threats. 

Until the gap between Damascus’s insistence on the 1974 disengagement lines and Jerusalem’s forward defense perimeter is bridged, the 90 percent agreed upon in Paris and Amman remains frozen behind fortified military outposts on the slopes of Mount Hermon.
