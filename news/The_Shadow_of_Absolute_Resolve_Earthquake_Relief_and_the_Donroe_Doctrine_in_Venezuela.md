# The Shadow of Absolute Resolve: Earthquake Relief and the Donroe Doctrine in Venezuela

On the evening of June 24, 2026, the earth beneath Venezuela ruptured twice in less than a minute. The seismic doublet—a magnitude 7.2 shock followed 39 seconds later by a magnitude 7.5 mainshock—tore through the Boconó-Morón-El Pilar fault system. While the epicenter was located in the Veroes Municipality of Yaracuy state, the seismic energy was funneled directly toward the country's densely populated north-central coast. The result was catastrophic. In Caracas, apartment buildings pancaked, and along the coastal strip of La Guaira, entire neighborhoods built on steep hillsides dissolved into landslides of concrete and mud. As of early July, local emergency operations report a toll of 3,685 dead and 16,740 injured, with some coastal municipalities seeing as much as 80 percent of their buildings collapsed. The Port of La Guaira, the country's economic windpipe, was rendered inoperable, its docks buckled and gantry cranes collapsed.

Into this crisis sailed the U.S. Navy. On June 28, the amphibious transport dock USS Fort Lauderdale (LPD 28) and the littoral combat ship USS Billings docked at the Port of La Guaira, marking the first time in decades that American warships had berthed in Venezuelan waters. The ships brought engineering units, medical staff, and heavy equipment to clear the runways at Simón Bolívar International Airport and restore basic operations at the port. Off the coast, a humanitarian air bridge was established, with cargo planes from Airlink and Amazon flying weekly supply missions from Miami to Maiquetía. The U.S. government announced a commitment of $386 million in emergency humanitarian assistance, funneled through organizations like Catholic Relief Services and UNICEF.

Yet the arrival of U.S. troops has been met with profound skepticism. For many Venezuelans, the humanitarian deployment is indistinguishable from a military occupation. This skepticism is rooted in the events of January 3, 2026, when U.S. special operations forces launched Operation Absolute Resolve, capturing President Nicolás Maduro and transporting him to New York to face federal narco-terrorism charges. In the vacuum, the Venezuelan Supreme Court declared a "forced absence" of the executive, allowing Vice President Delcy Rodríguez to assume the acting presidency on January 5. The intervention was widely interpreted as the first physical application of the "Donroe Doctrine"—the second Trump administration's aggressive reassertion of U.S. hegemony in the Western Hemisphere, named after President Trump's assertive adaptation of the 19th-century Monroe Doctrine. Under this framework, Secretary of State Marco Rubio has signaled that Washington will maintain a direct role in managing Venezuela's transition, prioritizing the eradication of foreign influence from Russia, China, and Iran.

The tension between humanitarian aid and U.S. geopolitical control has ignited a fierce debate online. On platforms like X and Reddit, critics have highlighted the apparent hypocrisy of the Trump administration offering $386 million in emergency relief while keeping billions of dollars of Venezuelan state oil revenues frozen under an ongoing "oil quarantine." Since January, the U.S. has enforced a tight naval blockade on unsanctioned tankers attempting to export Venezuelan crude, routing the proceeds of authorized oil sales—largely managed under Treasury licenses by companies like Chevron and Vitol—into offshore accounts controlled by Washington. "We are sending them boxes of bottled water while keeping their entire national treasury under lock and key," read one widely shared post on r/politics. Activists argue that the quarantine has crippled the country's public infrastructure, making the earthquake's damage far worse than it would have been otherwise.

Meanwhile, the financial world is dealing with its own fallout from the January intervention, centering on the prediction market platform Polymarket. In the weeks leading up to Operation Absolute Resolve, over $10.5 million was wagered on whether the U.S. would "invade Venezuela." However, the operation was plagued by a 72-hour delay in its execution, pushed back due to adverse weather and intelligence gathering limits before the holidays. When U.S. forces finally struck on January 3, the operation was executed as a targeted "snatch-and-extract" raid rather than a territorial invasion. Polymarket subsequently resolved the market as "No," ruling that the extraction did not meet the definition of a "military offensive intended to establish control over any portion of Venezuela." The decision sparked outrage among traders, who accused the platform of semantic hairsplitting. The controversy deepened when federal prosecutors indicted U.S. Army Master Sergeant Gannon Ken Van Dyke for insider trading, alleging he used classified intelligence of the raid's timing to bet against the invasion on the platform.

The transition under Acting President Delcy Rodríguez has fundamentally rewritten South American alliances. Rodríguez has sought a pragmatic path, cooperating with U.S. officials on oil exports and allowing international relief teams—including a notable Israeli military delegation—into the country to assist with reconstruction. Yet, this cooperative stance has alienated Venezuela’s traditional leftist allies. Colombia’s Gustavo Petro and Brazil’s Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva have expressed deep concern over the permanent U.S. military footprint on their borders. For Washington, the earthquake has provided a convenient justification to cement its presence. The USS Fort Lauderdale and USS Billings are no longer just humanitarian vessels; they are the floating symbols of a new geopolitical reality, where disaster relief and strategic oversight are increasingly intertwined.
