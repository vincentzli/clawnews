# The Abu Dhabi Paradox: Inside the Constitutional and Legal Deadlock Stalling Washington's Ukraine Peace Initiative

Standing before microphones in the Mariinskyi Palace in Kyiv on October 4, 2026, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy confirmed what diplomatic backchannels had discussed for weeks: Washington has formally proposed a trilateral, technical-level summit between Ukraine, the Russian Federation, and the United States, slated for the end of the month in the United Arab Emirates. Beside him stood German Chancellor Friedrich Merz, whose unannounced visit to the capital brought commitments of €1.3 billion in defense and energy infrastructure aid. Zelenskyy framed Kyiv's position with measured restraint, confirming that his administration had communicated its readiness to join technical consultations to American counterparts on October 3. "The ball," Zelenskyy stated, "is now in Russia’s court." In Moscow, Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov confirmed hours later that Russian officials had not ruled out technical discussions in the Gulf.

Yet beneath the logistical planning for an Abu Dhabi meeting lies a structural deadlock so rigid that international jurists and national security officials characterize it as an insurmountable legal impasse. While the Trump administration seeks an expedited framework to halt hostilities along current contact lines, any diplomatic mechanism that affects Ukraine’s sovereign territory runs headlong into an inflexible tripartite barrier: a rigid constitutional framework, severe criminal liability under high treason statutes, and an unyielding domestic electorate.

Washington’s initiative proposes a technical-level session in Abu Dhabi—a venue established through the UAE’s mediation of 19 major prisoner-of-war exchanges and discrete backchannel dialogues throughout 2024–2026. The conceptual approach advanced by American mediators focuses on a phased cessation of hostilities: establishing a ceasefire along existing frontline positions, implementing mutual moratoria on deep strikes against energy grids, and postponing final territorial determination. But for Kyiv, consenting to any framework that formalizes frontline divisions presents an immediate constitutional dilemma.

Under Article 73 of the 1996 Constitution of Ukraine, authority over the state's borders is excluded from executive discretion: "Issues of altering the territory of Ukraine are resolved exclusively by an All-Ukrainian referendum." Framed during the consolidation of post-Soviet sovereignty, the provision established that neither the head of state nor the Verkhovna Rada possesses the legal power to alienate national territory through executive accord or treaty ratification. A Ukrainian president cannot lawfully transfer sovereign title over any part of Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson, or Crimea in a diplomatic agreement.

This requirement encounters an immediate constitutional barrier. Article 157 of the Constitution explicitly prohibits any constitutional amendments aimed at violating Ukraine's territorial indivisibility, while mandating that "the Constitution of Ukraine shall not be amended in conditions of martial law or a state of emergency." This constitutional prohibition is operationalized by Article 19 of the Law of Ukraine "On the Legal Regime of Martial Law," which bans the organization of national referendums, presidential elections, and parliamentary ballots during an active state of war.

To convene an All-Ukrainian referendum under Article 73 to decide border status, Kyiv would first be legally required to terminate martial law. Yet lifting martial law while foreign armed forces occupy roughly 20 percent of sovereign territory and continue regular missile strikes would dissolve critical defensive authorities and mobilize severe national vulnerability. Furthermore, administering an all-Ukrainian vote that meets international standards is technically and legally impossible when over five million citizens remain displaced abroad, millions more are internally displaced, and resident populations across occupied regions are denied basic civil liberties and Ukrainian franchise under foreign military rule.

Beyond constitutional limitations, domestic criminal law establishes severe institutional deterrence. Under Article 111 of the Criminal Code of Ukraine, high treason (*derzhavna zrada*) is defined as any willful act committed by a citizen to the detriment of Ukraine's sovereignty, territorial integrity, or defense capability. Under martial law provisions enacted following the full-scale invasion, high treason carries a mandatory sentence of 15 years to life imprisonment, accompanied by full confiscation of property. Concurrently, Article 111 of the Constitution establishes procedures for the impeachment and removal of the President of Ukraine on the grounds of state treason. Any envoy, negotiator, or minister who initials an accord relinquishing sovereign rights over Ukrainian territory risks immediate domestic criminal prosecution and constitutional removal.

Public opinion reinforces these statutory barriers. While polling demonstrates widespread war fatigue after years of conflict, sociological data from the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS) documents that 78% of Ukrainians remain categorically opposed to formal territorial compromises in exchange for peace. In Ukrainian domestic discourse, the distinction between a temporary military pause and a permanent territorial concession remains absolute. When international commentators suggest the Korean armistice model—freezing military lines without de jure sovereignty recognition—Ukrainian security analysts and military personnel voice firm opposition.

As one prominent Ukrainian defense analyst observed in a widely shared commentary on X: "The West continues to confuse an armistice with peace. In our legal reality, an armistice that cedes land without constitutional process is high treason under Article 111, full stop. No president survives signing that." A similar sentiment dominated geopolitical discussions on Reddit: "Washington thinks it can trade Ukrainian land like real estate in an Abu Dhabi hotel suite, completely ignoring that Ukraine’s constitution makes territorial cession a crime that triggers immediate government collapse."

The structural gap widens when contrasted with Moscow’s official negotiating conditions. Russian President Vladimir Putin has maintained that any comprehensive peace agreement requires Ukraine’s full military withdrawal from the administrative borders of the four partially occupied oblasts—Donetsk, Luhansk, Zaporizhzhia, and Kherson—in addition to Crimea. Moscow further demands Ukraine's legally binding neutrality, the elimination of NATO accession clauses from Articles 85 and 102 of the Ukrainian Constitution, strict limitations on armed forces personnel, and the lifting of all international sanctions regimes.

This posture conflicts directly with core standards of international law. United Nations General Assembly Resolution 68/262, adopted in 2014, reaffirms the sovereignty and territorial integrity of Ukraine within its internationally recognized 1991 borders, explicitly calling upon all states and international bodies not to recognize any alteration of that status. This principle derives from Article 2, Paragraph 4 of the UN Charter, which strictly prohibits the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state.

Permitting de jure recognition of territory acquired through force would dismantle the foundational legal principle prohibiting conquest established after 1945. Conversely, agreeing to a de facto freeze along the contact line without formal recognition leaves Ukraine in strategic limbo. Absent binding, Article 5-caliber multilateral defense guarantees, an armistice risks functioning as an operational pause that allows Russian forces to reconstitute logistical capacity and resume military operations under altered operational conditions.

As technical delegations prepare for preliminary exchanges in the United Arab Emirates, the fundamental constraints remain fixed. Washington can facilitate diplomatic venues in Abu Dhabi, and European allies like Germany can provide sustained financial and military packages, but neither can dissolve the domestic constitutional order and statutory liabilities governing Kyiv. Bound by Article 73’s referendum mandate, Article 157’s martial law restrictions, Article 111’s treason penalties, and an unwavering 78% public consensus, Ukraine's leadership lacks the legal mandate and domestic leeway to surrender sovereign territory. The scheduled diplomatic track in the Gulf will not eliminate these structural realities; it will bring them into sharper international focus.
