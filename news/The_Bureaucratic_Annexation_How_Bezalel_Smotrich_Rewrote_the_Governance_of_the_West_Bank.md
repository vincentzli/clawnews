# The Bureaucratic Annexation: How Bezalel Smotrich Rewrote the Governance of the West Bank

On decentralized crypto prediction platforms like Polymarket, traders wagering on Middle Eastern geopolitical outcomes currently price the probability that Israel will formally annex any portion of the West Bank before December 31, 2026, at a meager 4.5 percent. Under the contract’s strict legal terms, a positive payout demands an explicit legislative act or cabinet proclamation declaring Israeli state sovereignty over the territory. Yet two floors above the military command rooms at the Kirya defense compound in Tel Aviv, Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich has spent the past three years rendering that formal vote almost irrelevant.

Through a meticulous dismantling of sixty years of military governance, Smotrich—who simultaneously serves as a minister within the Ministry of Defense—has engineered an irreversible, civilianized administrative apparatus across Area C. What international capitals treat as a prospective diplomatic crisis is already an accomplished administrative fact.

The operational blueprint for this transition was laid bare in a recording obtained from an off-the-record gathering of settler leadership. Addressing supporters, Smotrich was blunt about circumventing international legal scrutiny: "We created a separate civilian system," he explained, calling the structural overhaul "mega-dramatic" and designed to "change a system’s DNA." By housing the new agency under the Defense Ministry rather than passing an overt annexation bill in the Knesset plenum, the government crafted an arrangement that Smotrich acknowledged was "easier to swallow in the international and legal context," preventing global powers from concluding that Israel had officially annexed the territory. The goal, he stated unequivocally, was "to settle the land, to build it, and to prevent its division and the establishment of a Palestinian state."

At the heart of this bureaucratic transition sits the Settlement Administration (Minhelet HaHityashvut). Established under the 2022 coalition agreements, the agency systematically stripped the civilian powers of the Israel Defense Forces’ Coordinator of Government Activities in the Territories (COGAT) and its operational arm, the Civil Administration. For six decades, international humanitarian law dictated that the West Bank be governed as occupied territory by a military commander charged with balancing temporary security needs against the welfare of the protected local population.

Smotrich severed that chain of command. In May 2024, the head of IDF Central Command executed military orders transferring sweeping regulatory authorities over real estate, land allocation, zoning, municipal bylaws, and building enforcement to a civilian political appointee: Deputy Head of the Civil Administration for Civil Affairs Hillel Roth, a veteran ideological compatriot of Smotrich. 

Rather than serving as a temporary wartime measure, this bureaucratic apparatus was systematically cemented into Israel's permanent civil service. In July 2026, the Civil Service Commission formally codified the director of the Settlement Administration as a permanent civil service post and extended Roth’s tenure to six years. The institutional maneuver ensures that even if Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s governing coalition collapses, Smotrich’s civilianized planning apparatus will endure under future cabinets. As Israeli human rights attorney Michael Sfard observed, the operational shift "transfers vast areas of administrative power from the military commander to Israeli civilians working for the government," creating an "apparatus headed by an Israeli minister... whose only interest is to advance Israeli interests."

The tangible consequences on the ground are unprecedented in scope. According to planning records and tracking data compiled by watchdog organizations including Peace Now and Yesh Din, the current government has authorized or retroactively legalized 104 Jewish outposts since taking power in late 2022. That total was accelerated by a Security Cabinet decision regularizing 34 settlements in a single wave, encompassing 10 previously unauthorized outposts and 24 new settlement divisions. 

Simultaneously, more than 160 pastoral and agricultural herding outposts have received state logistical coordination, water infrastructure, and defense backing, securing hundreds of thousands of dunams of open land in Area C while squeezing Palestinian pastoralist communities out of traditional grazing zones. In the assessment of Yesh Din’s leadership, "annexation is not galloping or crawling, it is already here."

This administrative absorption has crossed into civil law enforcement and cultural heritage. In August 2026, Defense Minister Israel Katz directed defense authorities to draft operational plans transferring public order and civil enforcement jurisdiction over Israeli settlers from the IDF to the civilian Israel Police—a force answering to far-right National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir. The move strips away the last pretense of military jurisdiction over Israeli nationals residing in the West Bank, cementing a bifurcated legal architecture where settlers enjoy domestic Israeli civil and criminal procedure while neighboring Palestinians remain subject to military tribunals. 

In the Knesset, right-wing lawmakers advanced legislation establishing a specialized Judea, Samaria and Gaza Heritage Authority. While the legislative bill was subjected to coalition procedural delays following its first reading, the substantive policy moved forward administratively: backed by a NIS 113 million allocation championed by Smotrich from state coffers, the government applied domestic antiquities oversight to historical sites across the West Bank, deploying inspectors into Areas A and B under the rubric of archaeological preservation.

Smotrich’s domestic campaign operates in lockstep with his ideological manifesto. In September 2025, he formally presented a territorial blueprint proposing Israeli sovereignty over 82 percent of the West Bank. Under that framework, approximately 3 million Palestinians would remain confined to six disconnected urban enclaves covering the remaining 18 percent, stripped of territorial contiguity, sovereign airspace, and border control—a structural manifestation of his guiding doctrine of "maximum territory with minimum Arabs."

Smotrich pushed these territorial ambitions further during his plenary address at the National Conservatism Conference (NatCon) held at the International Convention Center in Jerusalem. Speaking on strategic relations, Smotrich declared that Israel must permanently retain and extend sovereignty over captured zones in the Gaza Strip and southern Lebanon. Framing territorial conquest as the only viable deterrent, Smotrich told attendees: "A side that started a war and lost it will lose territory to the victorious side." He coupled this assertion with a renewed demand for regional Arab states to facilitate the "voluntary migration" of Gaza’s population, insisting that depopulating the enclave "is not racism" but a legitimate strategic remedy.

These maximalist pronouncements arrive against an unforgiving domestic electoral clock. With general elections for the 26th Knesset set for October 27, 2026, Smotrich’s Religious Zionism party has been fighting for political survival, consistently hovering dangerously near the mandatory 3.25 percent electoral threshold. Facing competition from rival national-religious factions, Smotrich executed a defensive consolidation, forming a technical list with Moshe Feiglin’s Zehut party to poll consistently between 5 and 6 seats. On October 6, Likud signed a formal surplus-vote agreement with Religious Zionism, with Netanyahu warning conservative voters that fragmented ballots could hand power to the opposition. For Smotrich, accelerating outpost legalizations and expanding ministerial footprints are not merely ideological goals; they are the primary currency keeping his political career alive.

The divergence between market perceptions and physical facts on the ground has created a major analytical blind spot. On Wall Street and trading desks, prediction markets interpret the low probability of a de jure declaration as proof of political paralysis. Yet international jurists recognize that the administrative integration has already breached the legal barrier. In its July 19, 2024 advisory opinion, the International Court of Justice determined that Israel’s sustained transfer of civilian authority, outpost regularizations, and exploitation of natural resources in the Palestinian territory constitute illegal annexation under Article 49 of the Fourth Geneva Convention and international prohibitions against territorial acquisition by force.

This institutional absorption is precipitating acute diplomatic friction. While the White House under President Donald Trump balances regional security coordination against Israeli unilateralism, European foreign ministries in London, Paris, and Brussels have expanded targeted sanctions lists against outpost leaders and associated financing conduits. More perilously, Arab normalization partners view the institutional absorption of the West Bank as a systemic threat. The United Arab Emirates has repeatedly cautioned that unilateral annexation constitutes a violation of the foundational understanding behind the Abraham Accords, while Saudi Arabian officials have maintained that bilateral normalization remains closed without a credible, irreversible process toward an independent Palestinian state.

As Israel heads into the October 27 vote, the true conflict over the West Bank is no longer whether a future government will decide to annex the territory. Through hundreds of civilian regulatory decrees, sixty-year-old military orders have been hollowed out. In their place stands a permanent civilian infrastructure that operates as an organic extension of the state of Israel.
