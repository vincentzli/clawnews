# The $272 Million Mirage: Why Deere’s Earnings Beat Is a Warning Sign for the 2026 Ag Cycle

On the trading floors of Chicago and the digital forums of Fintwit, the math behind Deere & Co.’s (DE) fiscal second-quarter earnings report on Thursday felt like a Rorschach test for the American industrial economy. On the surface, the numbers were a triumph of operational resilience: a quarterly earnings per share (EPS) of $6.55, obliterating the analyst consensus of $5.81. Revenue clocked in at $13.37 billion, a 5% year-over-year climb that surpassed the $11.44 billion expected by Wall Street. Yet, as the opening bell rang, the market delivered a cold verdict. Shares of the Moline, Illinois-based giant tumbled 3% in pre-market action before sliding as much as 7.5% to $545.00 by midday, erasing billions in market capitalization in a matter of hours.

The disconnect lies in the quality of the beat. While the headline figures sparkled, the internal plumbing of the report revealed a reliance on what r/wallstreetbets users quickly dubbed "accounting fluff." Specifically, Deere’s bottom line was propped up by a $272 million one-time refund related to the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) tariff recoveries—a byproduct of the Trump administration's shifting trade protocols. Without this windfall, the $6.55 EPS would have looked significantly more like a "whimper," as one Reddit trader noted. More concerning for the "Bulls of the Plains" was management’s refusal to raise full-year net income guidance, which remained frozen at the $4.5 billion to $5.0 billion range. In a market where "guidance is king," the message was clear: the second half of 2026 is going to be a slog.

"Classic DE. Beat on the top and bottom line because of a one-time tariff recovery, but the core ag business is absolutely bleeding," wrote one investor on r/stocks. The bleeding is most evident in the Production & Precision Agriculture segment—the crown jewel of Deere’s high-margin future. Sales in this division cratered 14% to $4.503 billion, but the real shock was a 39% plunge in operating profit. This is the "normalization" that CEO John May and his team have been warning about, but for many value investors, the speed of the descent is startling. Management’s insistence that 2026 represents the "bottom of the current cycle" is being met with deep skepticism, especially as U.S. farm income is projected to flatline at $153 billion this year, a sharp retreat from the $182 billion record seen in 2022.

The macro-environment in mid-2026 provides little cover for the equipment manufacturer. With President Donald Trump’s second-term trade policies in full swing, Deere expects to face a net tariff headwind of $1.2 billion for the remainder of the fiscal year. On the monetary side, the transition at the Federal Reserve has kept investors on edge. This week, Jerome Powell serves as Chair Pro Tempore, bridging the gap until Kevin Warsh is sworn in as the 17th Fed Chair tomorrow, May 22. Warsh, confirmed by the Senate just last week in a 54–45 vote, inherits an economy where interest rates remain high enough to pin down the CAPEX budgets of American farmers who are already struggling with a 15% to 20% projected decline in demand for large agricultural equipment.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, confirmed earlier this year, has championed an "America First" fiscal policy that has yielded some tariff refunds for industrials, but the net cost of imported components continues to squeeze margins. While the Construction & Forestry segment provided a rare bright spot—with sales surging 29% to $3.79 billion on the back of domestic infrastructure spending—it wasn't enough to offset the rot in Large Ag. For the bulls, the narrative remains centered on "Normalized Earnings Power." They argue that Deere’s shift toward a "Tech-as-a-Service" model, with over 500 million engaged acres, will eventually decouple the stock from the hardware cycle.

However, the current price action suggests the market isn't buying the software-multiple dream just yet. "Construction and Small Ag are carrying the whole company on their backs," noted an analyst on r/investing. "If it weren't for infrastructure spending, DE would be trading in the $400s." The disagreement among investors is fundamental: is this a "normalized" trough that offers a generational buying opportunity, or is it a "beat and bleed" scenario where the real pain is still ahead? As the inventory of high-horsepower tractors in North America falls by 45% year-over-year, the "clearing of the decks" is underway, but for those holding the stock at $545, the wait for the 2027 recovery feels like an eternity.
