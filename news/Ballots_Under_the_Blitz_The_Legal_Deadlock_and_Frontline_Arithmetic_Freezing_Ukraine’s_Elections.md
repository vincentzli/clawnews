# Ballots Under the Blitz: The Legal Deadlock and Frontline Arithmetic Freezing Ukraine’s Elections

When the Verkhovna Rada convened under air raid alerts in Kyiv to pass the twentieth extension of martial law, extending emergency powers through October 31, 2026, the parliamentary vote was swift and overwhelmingly decisive. Yet outside the domed legislative hall, the constitutional clock has become one of the most contentious geopolitical flashpoints of the war. Across Western capitals and Russian state media, the question of whether a democratic republic engaged in an existential war for territorial survival can—or legally may—hold national elections has deepened an acute legal, logistical, and military deadlock.

In Moscow, Russian President Vladimir Putin has weaponized the calendar to mount an ongoing delegitimization offensive against Kyiv, claiming that Volodymyr Zelenskyy ceased to be the lawful head of state once his initial five-year calendar term expired on May 20, 2024. Pointing to selective interpretations of Ukrainian statutes, Putin told reporters in Tashkent that "according to preliminary estimates, the only legitimate authority in Ukraine now is the parliament and the speaker of the Rada." In Washington, where President Donald Trump has maintained intense scrutiny over U.S. financial and security assistance to Ukraine and repeatedly questioned Kyiv's governance timelines, the debate has placed Ukrainian diplomats under sharp transatlantic pressure.

Within Ukraine’s own legal architecture, however, the statutory and constitutional constraints leave zero administrative discretion. The prohibition against voting is codiﬁed in ordinary legislation: Article 19 of the 2015 Law of Ukraine "On the Legal Regime of Martial Law" explicitly bans presidential, parliamentary, and local elections while the emergency regime remains in force. Contrary to external commentary characterizing the postponement as an executive decree by the Presidential Office on Bankova Street, constitutional scholars emphasize that the system was deliberately constructed to avert constitutional vacuums during state emergencies.

Under Article 83 of the Ukrainian Constitution, if the parliamentary term expires during martial law, the powers of the Verkhovna Rada are automatically prolonged until the first session of a newly elected parliament convened after martial law is formally rescinded. For the executive branch, Article 108 provides an interlocking continuity clause, dictating that "the President of Ukraine shall exercise his or her powers until the assumption of office by the newly elected President of Ukraine." Chairman of the Verkhovna Rada Ruslan Stefanchuk rejected Moscow's calls for parliamentary succession, stating that constitutional provisions transferring temporary presidential powers to the speaker apply exclusively to premature terminations—such as voluntary resignation, death, or parliamentary impeachment—not to the expiration of a statutory term during active wartime mobilization.

This legal consensus extends across Ukraine’s entire political spectrum. During the ninth Jean Monnet Dialogue, convened under European Parliament auspices on November 10–12, 2023, in Mukachevo in the Transcarpathian region, leaders of every parliamentary faction—including Zelenskyy’s Servant of the People, Petro Poroshenko’s opposition European Solidarity, and the Holos party—signed a joint memorandum. The cross-party agreement formally resolved that national elections will not be conducted during hostilities, mandating that the electoral process commence no earlier than six months after the total cancellation of martial law to allow for legislative revisions and institutional audits.

Beneath the legal consensus lies an intractable operational reality. According to comprehensive technical assessments conducted by Ukraine’s Central Election Commission (CEC) and the civil society monitoring network OPORA, organizing a nationwide vote under present circumstances would result in structural disenfranchisement. Approximately 20 percent of sovereign Ukrainian territory remains under Russian military occupation, cutting off millions of citizens living under forced administrative integration from participating in Ukrainian constitutional processes. Domestically, the war has uprooted 3.7 million internally displaced persons (IDPs), while missile strikes have destroyed administrative archives across Kharkiv, Donetsk, and Zaporizhzhia, throwing the State Voter Register into operational disarray.

The external electorate presents an even steeper barrier. More than 6.5 million registered Ukrainian refugees currently reside abroad, predominantly in Poland, Germany, and the Czech Republic. In normal peacetime balloting, Ukraine maintained only 101 foreign diplomatic precincts across roughly 72 countries. Under wartime conditions, those consular posts would be forced to process an unmanageable average of over 60,000 voters per single diplomatic mission, creating logistical gridlock and severe public safety hazards in European capitals. As Olha Aivazovska, Chair of the Board of OPORA, warned: "Consular registration cannot become a bottleneck that disenfranchises millions of citizens abroad, yet establishing hundreds of ad-hoc voting centers outside sovereign embassies requires complex bilateral treaties that European hosts cannot swiftly deliver."

On the home front, the security arithmetic is lethal. Ukrainian cities face persistent salvos of Russian ballistic missiles, glide bombs, and drone swarms. The Central Election Commission and defense analysts estimate that designating fixed polling stations would create high-value civilian target clusters for Russian missile strikes, while a nationwide air raid alert on election day would legally freeze polling operations and invalidate turnout. Diverting state funds would also impose severe costs: the CEC estimated that administering a wartime election would divert between 10 billion and 15 billion hryvnias (approximately $250 million to $380 million) directly away from military procurement.

Moreover, the process confronts the hazard of disenfranchising roughly 800,000 active-duty military personnel entrenched across a 600-mile frontline. Conducting trench voting under direct artillery and drone observation poses catastrophic battlefield risks, while holding competitive partisan campaigns among active soldiers violates the apolitical cohesion of the armed forces and compromises the constitutional guarantee of a secret ballot under a rigid military chain of command.

Ukrainian society has aligned decisively behind these physical and institutional realities. Longitudinal survey data from the Kyiv International Institute of Sociology (KIIS) demonstrates that public opposition to wartime elections has remained rock-solid. Across iterative survey waves, over 78 percent of Ukrainian citizens consistently stated that national elections must only take place after the war ends and martial law is lifted. Anton Hrushetskyi, Executive Director of KIIS, observed that citizens perceive external calls for immediate wartime balloting as an artificial, destabilizing pressure that would fracture domestic unity while diverting resources from national defense.

On social media platforms, the debate continues to provoke intense engagement. Across discussions on X and Reddit’s r/worldnews, observers and historians have increasingly cited the British precedent of World War II, noting that the United Kingdom passed successive Prolongation of Parliament Acts to freeze general elections for a full decade between 1935 and 1945. As one widely circulated comment on X encapsulated the dilemma: "Holding an open ballot box while Russian ballistic missiles are overhead isn't democracy; it's operational suicide." For Ukraine, the suspension of elections remains not a suspension of democracy, but the constitutional defense of its prerequisite: national survival.
