# The Flag and the Frontline: Inside the Information War over Kostyantynivka

On July 3, 2026, inside a military command post, Chief of the Russian General Staff Army General Valery Gerasimov delivered a televised briefing to President Vladimir Putin. Russian forces of the "South" group, Gerasimov reported, had fully captured the city of Kostyantynivka in Ukraine's Donetsk region. Putin publicly praised the operation, characterizing the city as a major industrial and transport hub and a significant tactical objective in the advance toward the Kramatorsk-Slovyansk agglomeration. State-run media channels quickly broadcast the footage, accompanied by video clips of soldiers raising the Russian flag over damaged municipal buildings.

However, on-the-ground reporting and satellite intelligence contradicted this report. Less than twenty-four hours after the televised briefing, Brigadier General Oleksandr Bakulin, commander of the Ukrainian Ground Forces’ 19th Army Corps, released geolocated video footage showing Ukrainian patrols moving through the central districts of the city. The discrepancy highlighted the contested status of Kostyantynivka, which has become the focus of what military analysts describe as cognitive warfare—the use of premature victory claims to influence political decision-making and public perception before military control is established on the ground.

For several months, Kostyantynivka has been a primary objective of Russia’s spring-summer offensive. Located in the Donetsk Oblast, the city is a critical southern anchor of Ukraine’s "fortress belt," which includes Druzhkivka, Kramatorsk, and Slovyansk. The capture of Kostyantynivka would provide Russian forces with a launchpad for offensives against these remaining Ukrainian strongholds.

According to Roman Pohorilyi, co-founder of the Ukrainian open-source intelligence project DeepState, Russian forces have employed a tactical approach in the sector that he characterizes as "controlled chaos." Rather than relying on traditional, consolidated armored columns, Russian units deploy small, dispersed infantry and sabotage groups to slip through Ukrainian defensive lines. The primary objective of these small groups is often informational: to reach a prominent structure, plant a flag, and record video footage to assert territorial control.

"They use the ambiguity of the frontline to exert psychological pressure," Pohorilyi explained in an interview with Radio NV. "They create a gray zone and then claim it is fully under their control, trying to influence narratives before territory is actually secured."

This tactical pattern has generated significant debate on social media platforms. On Reddit communities such as r/CombatFootage and r/UkraineWarVideoReport, users have analyzed the geolocated footage of the Russian flag-raising events. Observers noted that many of these clips were recorded on the northern outskirts or in isolated pockets where Russian troops did not maintain long-term positions. Some users described the attempts as "flag-waving operations" designed for propaganda value rather than sustainable military occupation, pointing out that several infiltration groups were neutralized shortly after filming. 

In response to the Kremlin's victory announcement, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy issued a public challenge, sarcastically inviting Putin to meet him in Kostyantynivka to verify the status of the city. Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov declined the suggestion, stating that Zelenskyy should travel to Moscow if he wished to hold discussions with the Russian president.

Within the city, the defensive layout organized by the 19th Army Corps—established in May 2025 under Operational Command South—is structured to counter these small-scale incursions. Defended by units from the 24th, 28th, and 93rd Mechanized "Kholodnyi Yar" Brigades, the Ukrainian forces have established fortified strongpoints in the urban core rather than maintaining a rigid frontline on the periphery. 

The primary counter-tactic utilized by the 19th Army Corps is the deployment of search-and-strike FPV drone teams. These drone units monitor the city’s boundaries to identify and target Russian infantry groups before they can establish permanent positions. Due to heavy Russian drone and artillery strikes on the primary ground supply routes, Ukrainian defenders have also utilized unmanned aerial vehicles to deliver critical supplies, including ammunition and medical kits, to isolated forward positions.

This drone dominance explains the Ukrainian rejection of a localized ceasefire proposed by Russian representatives in early July. Pohorilyi and other military analysts labeled the proposal a tactical move designed to halt Ukrainian drone operations, which would allow Russian infantry to reinforce their positions within the gray zone.

The timing of Gerasimov’s July 3 briefing was highly strategic, occurring just days before the NATO Summit in Ankara, Turkey, on July 7–8, 2026. At the summit, Western allies were scheduled to debate an €70 billion (approximately $80 billion) military aid package for Ukraine and discuss a proposal by U.S. President Donald Trump to grant Ukraine a license to domestically manufacture PAC-3 Patriot interceptor missiles.

Analysts from the Institute for the Study of War (ISW) noted that the premature announcement of Kostyantynivka's capture was timed to influence these debates. By projecting a narrative of a collapsing Ukrainian defense, the Russian Ministry of Defense aimed to convince Western policymakers that further military aid would not alter the course of the conflict.

Despite the information campaign, the NATO allies proceeded to approve the €70 billion funding package. President Trump also confirmed the U.S. commitment to the Patriot interceptor manufacturing license, citing the utility of Ukraine's domestic drone infrastructure and defensive resilience as key factors. While the battle for Kostyantynivka continues as a contested urban conflict, the 19th Army Corps maintains control of the city center, demonstrating that symbolic flag-plantings have not translated into operational control.
