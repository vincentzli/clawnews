# The Silent Primary: Inside the Early Battle for the Post-Trump Era

With more than two years to go before the first ballots of the 2028 presidential election are cast, the machinery of American politics is already running at full throttle. On the Republican side, Donald Trump’s second term is term-limited, opening up the GOP nominating field for the first time in over a decade. On the Democratic side, the party is wrestling with an identity crisis as the traditional establishment collapses in the polls, giving way to an intense ideological struggle between progressives and moderates. This shadow campaign, known to insiders as the "silent primary," is rapidly heating up behind closed doors, driven by shifting voter demographics, institutional warfare over the nominating calendar, and quiet donor outreach.

A University of New Hampshire (UNH) Granite State Poll released on July 22, 2026, has sent shockwaves through the political establishment, revealing that both nomination battles are wide open. The poll, which surveyed 1,458 New Hampshire residents between July 15 and July 20, 2026, shows that front-runner status is a fragile commodity. 

In the Republican primary, Vice President J.D. Vance remains the nominal front-runner, but his support among likely GOP primary voters has plummeted from a dominant 53% in February 2026 to 36% in July. Concurrently, Secretary of State Marco Rubio has experienced a massive surge, climbing from just 7% to 26% over the same period. The margin of error for the 579 likely Republican primary voters surveyed was +/- 4.1%, placing Rubio within striking distance of the Vice President and turning the Republican contest into a neck-and-neck battle. 

This shifting dynamic on the Republican side highlights a deeper ideological and demographic split within the party. Vance’s support remains heavily concentrated among self-described core conservatives who favor his populist-nationalist agenda. Rubio, by contrast, has built his surge by consolidating support among moderates and libertarians, presenting himself as a seasoned foreign policy hand who can unite the party's disparate factions. The poll also revealed a curious paradox regarding Donald Trump’s influence: while two-thirds of likely Republican primary voters want the 2028 nominee to build on Trump’s presidency rather than go in a different direction, less than half (48%) stated that an endorsement from Trump would make them more likely to support a candidate. 

This data shows that while the base remains loyal to the policy achievements of the Trump administration, many likely primary voters are maintaining an independent stance when it comes to choosing his successor. The findings have also drawn scrutiny from political watchdogs, who point to the potential conflicts of interest when high-ranking executive branch officials like Vance and Rubio utilize their official national platforms and official travel schedules during the unofficial campaign season. While neither has formally declared a candidacy or established a federal campaign committee for 2028, both are actively cultivating relationships with major GOP donors and appearing at key political functions.

On the Democratic side, the ideological lines are drawn even more sharply. The UNH poll has identified Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (AOC) at 22% and former Transportation Secretary Pete Buttigieg at 21% as the two primary front-runners among likely Democratic primary voters, with a margin of error of +/- 3.8% for the 648 likely Democratic voters. Other prominent figures are falling behind; California Governor Gavin Newsom stands at 8%, while former Vice President Kamala Harris has slipped to just 5%, continuing a steady decline that began after the Biden-Harris administration left office in January 2025. 

The division between Ocasio-Cortez and Buttigieg represents a clear battle for the soul of the Democratic Party. Ocasio-Cortez draws much of her support from self-described socialists and progressives, while Buttigieg's support is distributed more evenly across progressives, liberals, and moderates. Both candidates enjoy high favorability ratings within the party, with Ocasio-Cortez at +55 net favorability (64% favorable, 9% unfavorable) and Buttigieg close behind at +54 net favorability. 

When asked about the poll, Ocasio-Cortez sought to downplay the horse-race aspect of the early numbers. "I think there's two things: One is that you know polls are polls, and a horse race is a horse race. I am, of course, I think, very deeply humbled and grateful to you know folks who participate and convey that confidence," she said, before adding that her immediate focus remains on helping Democrats win back the majority in the House of Representatives in the upcoming 2026 midterm elections. 

Buttigieg has adopted a similarly cautious public posture, though his actions suggest a candidate keeping his options wide open. During a Connecticut Forum event in January 2026, he remarked that it was "too early" to make a decision about a 2028 run, calling it a "family decision." However, during an April 2026 appearance on MSNBC, when host Al Sharpton asked him about a potential future campaign, Buttigieg quipped, "You save me a seat." His recent travels to early-voting states like Iowa and New Hampshire have further fueled speculation, even as he insists his current priority is campaigning for Democratic candidates in the midterms.

Beyond the candidate matchups, a bitter institutional war is raging over the primary calendar itself. On July 24, 2026, the Democratic National Committee's (DNC) Rules and Bylaws Committee voted 26 to 4 to recommend a restructured 2028 nominating calendar that places South Carolina first on January 22, 2028, followed by Nevada on February 1 and New Hampshire on February 8. The move is part of a deliberate effort by the national party to prioritize more racially diverse states at the start of the primary process. 

The decision has met with fierce resistance from New Hampshire Democrats, setting up a repeat of the calendar feud that shadowed the 2024 cycle. New Hampshire Democratic Party Chair Ray Buckley slammed the vote, stating that party insiders had "learned nothing from 2024." Buckley warned that the committee's vote does not change the "realities governing New Hampshire's primary," referencing a state law that requires the secretary of state to schedule the primary at least seven days before any other similar nominating contest. 

New Hampshire’s congressional delegation—including Senators Jeanne Shaheen and Maggie Hassan, and Representatives Chris Pappas and Maggie Goodlander—released a scathing joint statement condemning the DNC's proposal. They declared the decision "wrong for Democrats, wrong for New Hampshire, and wrong for the country," defending the state's traditional first-in-the-nation status as a "tested proving ground" where candidates must engage in face-to-face retail politics rather than relying on expensive media campaigns.

If New Hampshire defies the DNC and schedules its primary ahead of South Carolina, the national party is expected to strip the state of its delegates once again. Such a move could complicate the strategies of the front-runners. A rogue primary would force candidates like Ocasio-Cortez and Buttigieg to choose between campaign appearances in an unsanctioned state or adhering to the DNC's rules to protect their delegate counts. 

As the 2026 midterm elections approach, the shadow of 2028 hangs heavily over both parties. In this quiet phase of the campaign, where fundraising and strategic positioning occur out of public view, the battle lines are already being drawn. In a post-Trump political era, the path to the presidency will not be determined by early name recognition alone, but by how successfully these candidates navigate the deep demographic, ideological, and institutional divides within their own parties.
