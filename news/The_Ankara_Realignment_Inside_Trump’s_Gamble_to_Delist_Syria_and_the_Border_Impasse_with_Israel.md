The Ankara Realignment: Inside Trump’s Gamble to Delist Syria and the Border Impasse with Israel

**

**
On July 8, 2026, on the sidelines of the NATO summit in Ankara, Turkey, U.S. President Donald Trump sat opposite Syrian President Ahmed al-Sharaa. The meeting, which would have been unimaginable less than two years ago when al-Sharaa was known by his militant nom de guerre Abu Mohammad al-Julani, culminated in a historic announcement: the United States would initiate the removal of Syria from its State Sponsors of Terrorism list. Secretary of State Marco Rubio formally notified Congress of the administration's intent the same day, triggering a mandatory 45-day congressional review period. The delisting, which is expected to pass without a blocking joint resolution, represents a sweeping realignment of American policy in the Middle East, aimed at unlocking international trade, encouraging private investment, and prying Damascus away from the orbits of Tehran and Moscow.

Yet, as the diplomatic gears turn in Washington and Damascus, the reality on the ground remains frozen by a tense military standoff along the Syrian-Israeli border. Following the dramatic collapse of the Bashar al-Assad regime in December 2024, the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) declared the 1974 Disengagement Agreement void, moving heavy armor and troops into the UN-monitored demilitarized buffer zone. Over the past eighteen months, Israel has consolidated this footprint, establishing fortified military bases and checkpoints inside Syrian territory, particularly in the provinces of Quneitra and Daraa. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu has repeatedly asserted that the Israeli military has no intention of withdrawing, citing severe security concerns over al-Sharaa's past as the leader of the Islamist armed group Hay'at Tahrir al-Sham (HTS).

For President al-Sharaa, who transitioned from a rebel commander to the transitional president of Syria and was formally appointed on January 29, 2025, the Israeli military presence is a direct violation of Syrian sovereignty. Damascus has adamantly refused to discuss the normalization of relations with Jerusalem until a complete military withdrawal to the pre-2024 border occurs. This diplomatic impasse has complicated the Trump administration's vision of a stable, economically integrated Middle East. While Washington has urged restraint, Israel views the new Syrian administration with deep suspicion. The Israeli defense establishment points to the appointment of several former Al-Qaeda-affiliated figures to key ministerial positions in Damascus and reports that Syria is actively seeking to rebuild its conventional military capabilities with external support.

The geopolitical gamble by the White House is driven by economic pragmatism. To facilitate Syria's reconstruction, the Trump administration has already dismantled layers of the previous sanctions regime. In late 2025, the U.S. repealed the Caesar Act, which had previously blocked foreign investment in government-controlled areas. This was followed in February 2026 by the United Nations removing HTS from its anti-terrorist list, clearing the path for the group's political wing to govern. In Congress, the move to delist Syria has found surprising bipartisan support. Senators Jeanne Shaheen and Elizabeth Warren, alongside Representative Joe Wilson, have argued that maintaining the terrorism designation—first imposed on December 29, 1979, by the Carter administration—is counterproductive to American interests. They argue that the legal grounds for the 1979 designation, which focused on the Assad regime's support for Palestinian militant groups and Hezbollah, no longer apply to al-Sharaa's government, which has provided formal counterterrorism assurances to Washington.

The delisting has triggered intense debates across online platforms, reflecting the deep polarization surrounding the policy. On Reddit's r/politics, users debated the moral hazard of the decision. One user remarked that engaging with al-Sharaa is a "pragmatic necessity," writing, "you have to deal with the people who actually run the place if you want to stabilize the region and get them away from Iran." Conversely, others criticized the political double standard of the move, with one poster noting, "if a Democratic administration had tried to delist a former Al-Qaeda affiliate, the opposition would have called for immediate impeachment."

This polarization is mirrored in the financial and prediction markets, where traders have closely watched the diplomatic maneuvering. On platforms like Polymarket, geopolitical contracts tracking the likelihood of Syria's removal from the State Sponsor of Terrorism list by the end of 2026 surged to an 85% probability following the Ankara summit. However, contracts predicting the normalization of relations between Israel and Syria before the end of the year have plummeted to under 12%. The stark contrast reflects a market consensus that while the U.S. is eager to normalize Damascus to facilitate reconstruction, the border dispute remains an intractable bottleneck.

The situation has been further inflamed by domestic actors within Israel. In April 2025, far-right Israeli activists founded the settler organization Pioneers of Bashan (*Halutzei HaBashan*). The group advocates for the permanent settlement of Jewish families in southern Syrian territory, which they refer to by its biblical name, Bashan. Pioneers of Bashan has organized multiple border incursions, with families attempting to cross the ceasefire line and establish outposts near Mount Hermon. Although the IDF has routinely detained these activists and handed them over to civilian police, their actions have heightened tensions, providing Damascus with domestic leverage to justify its refusal to engage in diplomatic talks.

For the Trump administration, the stakes are high. Removing Syria from the terrorism list after more than 46 years is intended to spark a reconstruction boom, drawing in capital from Gulf states and Western developers. French President Emmanuel Macron’s historic visit to Damascus in early 2026 highlighted Europe’s willingness to re-engage with a stabilized Syria. But as long as Israeli troops remain dug into Quneitra and Syrian rearmament continues, the threat of localized border conflict looms over the reconstruction effort. The 45-day congressional clock is ticking, but the path to true regional stability remains blocked by the barbed wire of the Golan buffer zone.
