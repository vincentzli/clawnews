# 1. Draft

## 1.1 Headline
The $600 Billion Money-Go-Round: Inside Nvidia's Megadeal to Guarantee OpenAI’s Ohio Datacenter and the Rise of Tech Vendor Financing

## 1.2 Body
On July 27, 2026, the global semiconductor market suffered a sudden tremor, exposing the delicate scaffolding supporting the artificial intelligence boom. Shares of Nvidia Corp. tumbled 5.23% to close at $196.02, wiping out nearly $260 billion in market capitalization. The slide was catalyzed by two concurrent disclosures. First, reports emerged that a state-backed Chinese consortium led by Shanghai Micro Electronics Equipment (SMEE) has successfully begun mass-producing domestic immersion deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography machines, threatening to bypass Western export controls and erode ASML Holding NV’s hardware monopoly. Second, and more controversially, details leaked of a massive financing arrangement: Nvidia is in discussions to provide a staggering $250 billion financial guarantee to back OpenAI’s lease of a planned 10-gigawatt data center campus in Piketon, Ohio.

The sheer scale of the Ohio project, which is being developed by SoftBank Group Corp.’s power subsidiary, SB Energy, has stunned project finance experts. Slated for the decommissioned federal Portsmouth Gaseous Diffusion Plant—a Cold War-era uranium-enrichment site—the campus represents a total infrastructure spend that could exceed $500 billion when fully outfitted. Because OpenAI remains a private, heavily unprofitable enterprise without an investment-grade credit rating, it cannot access the private debt capital markets at viable interest rates. To bridge this gap, Nvidia is negotiating a "credit wrap"—a corporate guarantee that places Nvidia's own balance sheet behind OpenAI's long-term lease and construction liabilities. 

Yet the financial commitments do not end at the physical walls of the Piketon site. Nvidia and OpenAI are separately negotiating an additional $350 billion in direct financing packages to cover the purchase of Nvidia's own next-generation AI accelerators. Taken together, Nvidia’s potential financial exposure to the Ohio megaproject reaches $600 billion. The transaction has sparked intense debate among retail and institutional investors alike, raising structural questions about vendor financing that echo the late-1990s telecommunications crash. 

On forums like r/wallstreetbets and r/stocks, the reaction has been fiercely divided. "Nvidia is literally lending money to its customer so the customer can buy its own chips. This is a massive ponzi/circular financing loop," wrote one retail trader. Bears argue that this represents classic circular financing. Under this view, Nvidia is using its massive cash generation to prop up demand for its own products, inflating its current revenues while taking on catastrophic tail risk if the AI software monetization engine fails to materialize. Another user on r/stocks warned: "If OpenAI fails or cannot generate enough revenue to pay back these leases, Nvidia's balance sheet will be absolutely wrecked."

Conversely, bulls view the transaction as a masterstroke of vertical integration and strategic resource capture. In an era where power availability has replaced silicon yields as the primary bottleneck for artificial intelligence, securing a 10-gigawatt allocation is a generational moat. The site’s power needs will be supported by a proposed $33 billion natural gas plant, funded in part under a bilateral trade agreement with Japan and overseen by U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent. With Masayoshi Son's SoftBank orchestrating the physical buildout, bulls argue that Nvidia is merely using its cash surplus to secure its technological hegemony. 

To understand the scale of this gamble, one must look at the financial profiles of the counterparties. Nvidia’s financial performance has been historic. For the first quarter of fiscal year 2027, which ended April 26, 2026, the company reported revenue of $81.6 billion and a net income of $58.3 billion. In its SEC filings, Nvidia disclosed a cash and cash equivalents balance of $24.5 billion, alongside $37.098 billion in marketable debt securities and $30.237 billion in marketable equity securities. While this represents a colossal war chest, the $600 billion in combined guarantees and chip financing is nearly 7.5 times the company's total liquid assets. 

OpenAI, on the other hand, is burning capital at an unprecedented rate. Audited financial documents for 2025 showed that while Sam Altman’s firm generated $13.07 billion in revenue, it incurred total costs and expenses of $34 billion, resulting in an operating loss of $20.92 billion and a net loss of $38.5 billion. For 2026, OpenAI is targeting annualized revenues of $25 billion to $30 billion, but GAAP losses are projected to reach as high as $33 billion due to compute costs and stock-based compensation.

This financial disparity is what makes the vendor financing structure so volatile. During the dot-com boom, Cisco Systems used its financing arm, Cisco Capital, to extend billions of dollars in credit to speculative competitive local exchange carriers (CLECs) and internet service providers. At the peak in 2000, vendor financing accounted for roughly 10% of Cisco's $20 billion in annual revenue. When those startups went bankrupt in 2001, Cisco was forced to write off $900 million in bad loans, write down $2.2 billion in inventory, and watch its stock price crash by 80%.

If the AI monetization curve flattens, Nvidia faces a similar systemic threat. The company currently trades at a trailing price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio of 31.5 and a forward P/E of 22 to 24. While this represents a significant compression from its historic multiples of over 60x, these multiples assume a clean, high-margin revenue stream. If Nvidia's revenues are increasingly supported by its own credit guarantees, the quality of its earnings deteriorates. 

Furthermore, the macro environment is tightening. Under Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell, the central bank has signaled that interest rates will remain elevated to combat persistent services inflation. Higher borrowing costs increase the hurdle rate for these capital-intensive projects. At the same time, the geopolitical moat is cracking. While the Chinese domestic immersion DUV machine is limited to an estimated 5 deliveries in 2026 and 20 in 2027, the fact that SMIC and ChangXin Memory Technologies can bypass U.S. restrictions suggests that the long-term addressable market for Western chipmakers in China is shrinking. If foreign demand declines while domestic AI builders require multi-hundred-billion-dollar balance sheet lifelines, Nvidia’s historic valuation may face a structural re-rating that no amount of circular financing can prevent.
