# The Margin Paradox: Inside SK hynix's Record Q2 Earnings and the Brutal AI Stock Sell-Off

On the trading floors of Seoul, the flashing red screens told a story of sudden, computerized panic. On July 29, 2026, South Korea’s KOSPI index suffered a consecutive plunge, dropping 6% after having fallen as much as 12.6% in intraday trading. This came on the heels of a devastating 10.84% crash the previous day, forcing regulators to repeatedly trigger market-wide circuit breakers to halt the stampede. At the center of this financial vortex stood SK hynix, the world's leading supplier of high-bandwidth memory. Hours earlier, the semiconductor giant had released its financial results for the second quarter of 2026, reporting a staggering 79.32 trillion won in revenue and a monumental 60.54 trillion won in operating profit. The company's operating margin reached a record-high of 76%. Yet, instead of celebrating a historic milestone, investors aggressively dumped the stock, dragging the entire South Korean tech sector down with it.

The roots of the sell-off lie in the mismatch between SK hynix's record-breaking numbers and the sky-high expectations of global analysts. Wall Street and Seoul consensus estimates had projected Q2 revenue of approximately 84 trillion won and an operating profit of 64 trillion won. Despite delivering a 257% year-over-year increase in revenue and a 557% explosion in operating profit, the company fell short of these projections. The disappointment rippled through the global AI hardware ecosystem, feeding investor anxiety that the multi-year AI capital expenditure boom might be reaching a peak and that valuation multiples for hardware makers have outpaced reality.

On online forums such as Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets and various financial platforms on X, retail traders—known in Korea as the "ants"—expressed disbelief at the market's reaction. "How can a company with a 76% operating margin trigger a market halt?" asked one user. Another prominent retail trader posted, "This is a classic 'sell the news' panic. The institutional algos are dumping on a 5% operating miss while completely ignoring the massive HBM4 ramp." Some retail investors who had taken leveraged bets suffered heavy losses, prompting calls for government intervention.

However, institutional analysts pointed to a structural friction within the memory market that explains the earnings miss. The core issue was a shift in the sales mix. While SK hynix's High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) is highly profitable, the growth rate of its Average Selling Price (ASP) was lower than expected. The underlying cause was the unexpected price surge in the conventional DRAM market. Throughout late 2025 and early 2026, standard DDR5 RDIMM server memory prices spiked by 50% to over 90% as major manufacturers shifted their production lines to HBM, creating a massive supply squeeze for legacy memory. Because HBM requires three to four times the silicon wafer capacity of conventional DRAM, the industry's shift to AI memory cannibalized standard DRAM supply. 

SK hynix had secured a significant portion of its HBM3E and HBM4 supply with large hyperscalers through fixed-price Long-Term Agreements (LTAs) to guarantee stable revenue. However, some analysts argue this strategy limited short-term flexibility. The locked-in prices under these LTAs prevented SK hynix from capturing the full pricing upside of the volatile spot-market "melt-up" in conventional DRAM. The traditional price premium that HBM commanded over DDR5 collapsed from the bottom up, narrowing the profitability gap between the two segments and making SK hynix's heavy HBM mix look less optimal to analysts who had modeled higher ASP growth.

A bright spot in the earnings report was SK hynix’s net profit, which hit an astonishing 93.92 trillion won, far exceeding its operating profit. This bottom-line surge was boosted by one-time investment gains, most notably from the sale of its stake in Japanese memory manufacturer Kioxia. SK hynix's involvement in Kioxia dates back to 2018, when it invested 395 billion yen (approximately 4 trillion Korean won) through a Bain Capital-led consortium to acquire Toshiba Memory. Following Kioxia's listing on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in December 2024, Bain Capital began a structured divestment of its stake, concluding in July 2026. While the sale generated massive cash proceeds and boosted SK hynix's net profit, institutional investors largely discounted this non-core windfall, focusing instead on the core operational miss in semiconductor sales.

Looking ahead, the competitive battlefield is shifting to the sixth-generation HBM4 chips. SK hynix confirmed that it began mass production and shipments of HBM4 during the second quarter and plans to ramp up production in the second half of 2026. This transition is crucial for maintaining its lead over Samsung Electronics and Micron Technology. NVIDIA's next-generation Vera Rubin platform, which relies on HBM4 to deliver up to 22 TB/s of memory bandwidth for next-generation AI workloads, is scheduled to ramp up shipments in the second half of the year. To support this, SK hynix has formed a "one-team" alliance with TSMC, integrating its memory stacks with the Taiwanese foundry's advanced logic processes for the base dies. SK hynix has also developed proprietary system-level testing equipment to identify defects after integration, a task traditionally handled by the foundry.

The market crash has forced South Korean financial authorities to step in. Finance Minister Koo Yun-cheol announced that regulators are reviewing market stabilization measures, including potential restrictions on single-stock leveraged ETFs that have exacerbated price swings. The political pressure is mounting on President Lee Jae Myung's administration to protect domestic retail investors, particularly as US President Donald Trump continues to implement protectionist policies under the CHIPS Act, which could force Korean chipmakers to relocate high-value manufacturing to the United States.
