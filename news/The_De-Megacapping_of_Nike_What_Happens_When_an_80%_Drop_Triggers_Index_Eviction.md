# The De-Megacapping of Nike: What Happens When an 80% Drop Triggers Index Eviction

At 4:00 p.m. on Friday, September 18, 2026, the closing cross on the New York Stock Exchange completed an institutional purge nearly two decades in the making. As trading desks finalized the quarterly rebalance orchestrated by S&P Dow Jones Indices, index-tracking algorithms executed the total liquidation of Nike Inc. from the S&P 100 index, effective at the opening bell on Monday, September 21. For 18 years, the Beaverton, Oregon-based athletic apparel pioneer occupied an undisputed seat among America’s hundred most valuable blue-chip enterprises. Yet after a 79.8% peak-to-trough collapse from its November 2021 all-time intraday high of $179.10 to $35.51, Nike’s market capitalization has shrunk from $264.2 billion to $53.6 billion. S&P Dow Jones Indices replaced Nike, along with Colgate-Palmolive Co., Simon Property Group Inc., and Honeywell International Inc., with enterprise software and semiconductor infrastructure stalwarts: Dell Technologies Inc., Palo Alto Networks Inc., Arista Networks Inc., and SanDisk. The substitution codified a broader transformation across capital markets, swapping consumer discretionary legacy brands for hardware and cybersecurity powering the artificial intelligence buildout.

The mechanical execution of the S&P 100 rebalance triggered an estimated $350 million to $400 million in mandatory liquidation across passive institutional portfolios and exchange-traded products, led by the $20.5 billion iShares S&P 100 ETF (OEF), where Nike’s residual 0.15% weighting was zeroed out. Trading volume in Nike surged past 24.2 million shares during Friday’s quadruple-witching session, roughly 140% of its 30-day average daily volume of 17.3 million shares. Yet unlike standard index deletions that accelerate downward spirals, the forced selling encountered aggressive, contrarian liquidity from retail brokerage accounts and deep-value asset managers. On retail trading forums like Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets and r/stocks, individual accounts have rallied around the ticker, framing the sub-$36 price as an historic mispricing. Forum participants cited Nike’s trailing price-to-earnings ratio of 17.2—a 50% discount to its 10-year historical mean of 32.4—and an annualized dividend yield of 4.62% ($1.64 per share on an annual basis), marking the highest yield in the company’s history as a public entity.

"At $35, you're buying the most recognized consumer brand on earth at 2014 prices," argued one widely shared post on r/stocks, which garnered over 1,400 upvotes as retail traders debated whether the deletion represented a capitulation bottom. "They don't need double-digit top-line growth to double your money; they just need normalized operating margins and debt discipline." On r/wallstreetbets, where short-dated call option sweeps on the October $40 and January 2027 $45 strikes saw open interest swell by more than 38,000 contracts over five sessions, the consensus was framed as a classic "inverse WSB" pivot: betting against the institutional capitulation that discarded an empire after four consecutive quarters of flatlined earnings.

Behind the tactical crosscurrents of index arbitrage and retail accumulation, however, lies an existential financial question: has Nike’s economic moat suffered irreversible erosion, permanently impairing its return on invested capital (ROIC)? Between fiscal 2021 and fiscal 2024, Nike maintained an enviable ROIC averaging 34.9%, generating $51.36 billion in revenue during the twelve months ended May 31, 2024. But the company’s fiscal 2025 results revealed severe operational fractures under former Chief Executive John Donahoe’s "Consumer Direct Acceleration" strategy, which aggressively severed ties with legacy wholesale partners like Foot Locker Inc. and specialty regional running stores in a bid to capture direct-to-consumer (DTC) digital margins. When digital customer acquisition costs spiked and demand softened, fiscal 2025 revenue contracted 9.8% to $46.31 billion, while EBIT margins compressed from 13.1% to 8.2%. In fiscal 2026, closed on May 31, revenue stagnated at $46.40 billion, operating margins hovered at 9.0%, and ROIC cratered to 14.8%—less than half of its historical peak.

While Nike retreated into promotional clearance to unclog bloated inventory across its Dunk, Air Force 1, and Jordan 1 franchises, specialized competitors seized its premium shelf space. Deckers Outdoor Corp.’s Hoka franchise generated $2.59 billion in fiscal 2026 (ended March 31, 2026), marking a 15.9% year-over-year surge following a 23.6% jump in fiscal 2025 to $2.23 billion; Hoka now represents 47.3% of Deckers’ $5.47 billion enterprise top line. Concurrently, Zurich-based On Holding AG (ONON) posted fiscal 2025 net sales of CHF 3.01 billion, up 30.0% year-over-year while defending gross margins north of 60.5%, and delivered second-quarter 2026 sales growth of 13.5% to CHF 850.3 million. In specialty running doors across North America, Nike’s market share fell from over 32% in 2019 to roughly 23% by mid-2026, according to channel checks, as runners permanently migrated toward the cushioning innovations of Hoka's Clifton and Bondi series and On’s Cloudmonster.

The structural loss of premium footwear share directly undermines the bull case championed across social media. Skeptics on Fintwit quickly pushed back against the retail dip-buyers. "People calling Nike at $35 a generational buy are ignoring that gross margins can't get back to 46% when you have to crawl back to wholesale partners on your hands and knees," observed one portfolio manager on X. "Foot Locker and Dick's are giving the best endcaps to Hoka, On, and New Balance. Nike is having to offer wholesale allowances and concessions just to buy back shelf space they gave away for free."

Tasked with arresting the decline is Chief Executive Elliott Hill, a 32-year Nike veteran who came out of retirement in October 2024 to replace Donahoe. Hill has dismantled the rigid matrix structure that centralized product distribution away from category-specific athletics, implementing a restructuring plan dubbed the "Sport Offense." Hill’s operational mandate involves repairing fractured wholesale agreements with Dick’s Sporting Goods Inc. and JD Sports Fashion Plc, while intentionally throttling back production runs of retro heritage sneakers to restore artificial scarcity. In September 2026, Nike also appointed Alexandre Arnault, the 34-year-old executive vice president at Tiffany & Co. and son of LVMH chairman Bernard Arnault, to its board of directors, signaling an effort to regain high-fashion cultural relevance. 

Yet Hill’s pivot comes with heavy balance sheet trade-offs. Nike finished fiscal 2026 with $12.1 billion in total debt against $2.8 billion in cash and short-term equivalents. With annual free cash flow compressed to roughly $2.4 billion, the company’s $1.64 per share dividend obligation absorbs nearly $2.47 billion annually—pushing the dividend payout ratio to an unsustainable 78.4% of net income. In a macroeconomic climate where the Federal Reserve benchmark rate sits at 4.25% to 4.50% and 10-year Treasury yields linger near 3.95%, Nike’s 4.6% equity dividend yield provides minimal risk premium over risk-free paper if cash flow fails to rebound.

For passive indexers, the S&P 100 deletion is an automatic bookkeeping adjustment, removing an underperforming consumer stock whose index weight had already dwindled to inconsequential levels while Nike remains securely within the broader S&P 500 and the 30-stock Dow Jones Industrial Average. For fundamental investors and retail contrarians, however, the September 21 milestone crystallizes the core dilemma of modern value investing: whether an 80% valuation discount reflects a temporary inventory cycle awaiting executive execution, or the market’s accurate repricing of a former monopoly that permanently lost its technological and cultural pricing power.
