# The Prediction Market Border War: How a Federal Circuit Split Over Sports Bets Threatens to Reshape the Financial Frontier

####

The legal battle over prediction markets has escalated into a high-stakes constitutional showdown between federal financial regulators and state gaming authorities. On August 28, 2026, the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit handed down a unanimous 3-0 decision in *KalshiEX, LLC v. Assad*, setting off shockwaves across the financial and legal landscape. The ruling, which dissolved a preliminary injunction that had previously protected Kalshi from state gaming regulators in Nevada, has drawn a sharp line between traditional financial derivatives and sports gambling, creating a direct circuit split that seems destined for the U.S. Supreme Court.

Writing for the Ninth Circuit panel, Circuit Judge Ryan D. Nelson rejected Kalshi’s arguments that sports-related event contracts are federally regulated "swaps" under the exclusive jurisdiction of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). Instead, Nelson characterized the contracts as sports betting in a financial wrapper. "The CFTC is not a national gambling regulator," Judge Nelson wrote. "No one suggested it was until over a decade after the law was passed." The court went on to point out that Kalshi’s own marketing had previously pitched its platform as "the first app for legal sports betting," a detail that undermined the company's claim that its contracts were designed as sophisticated financial hedging tools.

The decision directly conflicts with an April 6, 2026, ruling by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit in *KalshiEX, LLC v. Flaherty*. In that case, a 2-1 majority led by Judge David J. Porter concluded that New Jersey’s state gambling laws were preempted by the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA) because the sports contracts met the federal definition of a swap. Under the Third Circuit's interpretation, because these contracts carry financial consequences, they fall under the CFTC’s exclusive regulatory umbrella, shielding them from state-level enforcement. Backed by Governor Mikie Sherrill's administration, New Jersey state gaming regulators had sought to block Kalshi from offering sports wagers, arguing that they bypassed state taxes and licensing requirements.

The Ninth Circuit, however, took a far more restrictive view of what constitutes a swap. Judge Nelson argued that the Third Circuit's logic "knows no limiting principle because anything could be defined as an event." The panel distinguished between the occurrence of an event—such as the Super Bowl taking place—and the outcome of the event, namely which team wins. By ruling that sports-outcome contracts are not swaps, the Ninth Circuit cleared the path for Nevada to enforce its strict gaming statutes.

The ruling is a major victory for Nevada regulators. George Assad, a retired Las Vegas Municipal Court judge and a member of the Nevada Gaming Control Board, has long been a vocal opponent of prediction markets operating without state licenses. Assad previously dismissed the platforms' arguments as a "word salad" designed to disguise sports wagers, famously warning that "the gig is up" for operators seeking to bypass state oversight. The Chairman of the Nevada Gaming Control Board, Mike Dreitzer, welcomed the Ninth Circuit's decision, viewing it as a vital defense of state sovereignty and consumer protection.

The regulatory crackdown comes at a time of unprecedented growth for prediction markets. In July 2026, the global prediction market sector reached an all-time monthly high of $50.6 billion in trading volume, with Kalshi alone capturing $37.7 billion, driven by World Cup contracts. Sports contracts have become the lifeblood of Kalshi's business model, accounting for roughly 72% of the platform's trading volume in 2026. The rapid expansion of these platforms has raised concerns among state regulators who argue they lack the consumer safeguards, age verification checks, and tax contributions required of traditional sportsbooks. Under CFTC Chairman Michael Selig, the federal agency has faced a massive surge in event contract proposals, prompting a group of 44 state attorneys general to sign a joint letter urging federal authorities to halt the expansion of prediction markets that reference sports and elections.

The Ninth Circuit's ruling has also cast a shadow over other prediction platforms. A consolidated case, *Robinhood Derivatives LLC v. Dreitzer*, saw similar preemption arguments rejected by the court. Robinhood, which has aggressively expanded its event contracts business through its acquisition of MIAXdx assets, faces the prospect of navigating a patchwork of state-by-state regulations. State regulators have already moved quickly to restrict operations; in June 2026, an Ingham County Circuit Court issued a temporary restraining order against Kalshi, threatening fines of $120,000 per day if it failed to geoblock Michigan residents. In August 2026, a Washington state court ordered Kalshi to implement geofencing for its sports and election contracts by September 2, 2026.

Adding to the complexity, the Ninth Circuit's decision was not a complete defeat for prediction markets. Rather than dismissing the entire case, the court remanded the portion of the lawsuit dealing with election contracts back to the district court for further consideration, keeping the regulatory fate of political prediction markets separate from sports betting.

While state regulators celebrate the ruling, prediction market advocates warn of unintended consequences. On forums like Reddit's r/wallstreetbets and other online prediction market communities, users have pointed out that restricting regulated, domestic exchanges will not stop the public from trading. Instead, many argue that retail volume will simply migrate back to decentralized, crypto-native offshore platforms like Polymarket, which recorded a peak monthly volume of $25.7 billion in March 2026. Because offshore platforms operate outside the reach of U.S. geofencing orders, critics argue that state-level bans merely hand a monopoly to unregulated foreign entities while stripping domestic consumers of CFTC protections.

With two federal appeals courts now in direct conflict over whether sports event contracts are swaps or bets, the legal framework governing prediction markets is fractured. The stark division between the Third and Ninth Circuits makes a petition for certiorari to the U.S. Supreme Court almost inevitable, as both sides seek a definitive national ruling on who holds the authority to regulate this multi-billion-dollar industry.
