# The Iron Fist vs. The Total Peace Legacy: Colombia’s 2026 Election Enters a Digital and Territorial Blackout

BOGOTÁ — In the gilded corridors of the Club El Nogal and the dust-choked streets of Quibdó alike, a singular silence has descended over Colombia. With exactly five days remaining before the first round of the 2026 presidential election, the country has entered its mandatory "statistical blackout," a legally mandated period where the publication of polls is prohibited. But the silence is deceptive. Behind the curtain of the blackout, a 28% undecided voting bloc—the largest in modern Colombian history—is being bombarded by a sophisticated arsenal of AI-generated "electoral fantasies" and a security narrative that feels more like a war report than a political platform.

The choice for Colombia’s 50 million citizens has narrowed to a stark ideological conflict between two men who embody the nation’s deepest fractures. On one side is Senator Iván Cepeda, the soft-spoken but steely standard-bearer of the Historic Pact, who is fighting to preserve the "Total Peace" legacy of the previous administration. On the other is Abelardo De La Espriella, a high-octane criminal lawyer known as "The Tiger," who has surged from the periphery to the center of the race by promising a "Bukele-style" overhaul of the Colombian state.

"Bukele would be a Boy Scout next to me," De La Espriella told an interviewer earlier this cycle, a quote that has since become the rallying cry for a middle class exhausted by the "territorialization of conflict." His platform is as radical as his rhetoric: he proposes reducing the Colombian state by 40%, closing the Special Jurisdiction for Peace (JEP), and constructing ten private mega-prisons in remote jungles where cellular signals will be permanently jammed. It is a vision of "Mano Dura" (Iron Fist) that resonates in a year where security metrics have hit a decade-long nadir.

The data fueling De La Espriella’s rise is harrowing. Reports from the *Fundación Ideas para la Paz* (FIP) indicate that illegal armed groups grew by 84% between 2022 and 2025. The "Total Peace" policy, intended to bring groups like the ELN and the Clan del Golfo to the table, has instead seen active members of these organizations swell to over 27,000. Most chilling is the emergence of "drone warfare" in the Cauca region, where over 400 explosive-laden drone attacks were recorded in the last year alone. For many, the early 2026 assassination of Senator Miguel Uribe Turbay served as the final proof that the state had lost its monopoly on violence.

"Without security, there is nothing; everything else follows," De La Espriella posted recently on X.com, echoing the security-first doctrine of the Trump administration in Washington. The geopolitical alignment is palpable. While the former Biden-Harris administration maintained a wary distance from De La Espriella’s rhetoric, President Trump—now serving his second term—has signaled a preference for the "Tiger’s" hawkish stance, especially following the US decertification of Colombia’s counter-narcotics efforts last September.

Cepeda, meanwhile, occupies the difficult position of the "incumbent successor." He defends the structural reforms and the spirit of "Total Peace," arguing that De La Espriella’s "Bukele model" would turn Colombia into a pariah state and reignite a civil war that the 2016 Peace Accords sought to extinguish. However, Cepeda’s campaign has been hampered by a wave of synthetic disinformation. In April, a deepfake video of Vice President Francia Márquez appearing to denounce the Historic Pact went viral, reaching 12 million views before it was flagged as AI-generated. This incident prompted the eleventh-hour passage of Law 2502 of 2025, which introduced severe penalties for AI-driven identity fraud in elections.

Despite the new law, the digital front remains a "Wild West." Analysts at the National AI Council (CON-IA) have identified a network of 176 automated accounts that have spent the last 48 hours flooding WhatsApp groups with fabricated audio clips of Cepeda discussing a supposed "confiscatory tax" on private savings. "We are in a state of cognitive war," says one digital forensics expert based in Medellín. "The blackout on polls has only accelerated the spread of these 'fantasies' because there is no objective data left to ground the conversation."

The 28% of undecided voters are now the most targeted demographic in the history of Latin American politics. They are the "silent bloc"—mostly urban youth and rural workers who feel abandoned by the left’s idealism and terrified by the right’s authoritarianism. In the absence of polls, campaigns are relying on internal "heat maps" of social media sentiment. On Reddit’s r/Colombia, the debate is ferocious. "The Tiger is just a marketing product," one top-voted comment reads, "but the Historic Pact has left us at the mercy of the drones. I’m voting for the prisons."

As the clock ticks toward Sunday’s first round, the tension in Bogotá is physical. Military patrols have been doubled following intelligence reports of planned disruptions by FARC dissidents in the capital. Whether Colombia chooses the continuity of a fragile peace or the surgical precision of an iron fist, the 2026 election has already proven one thing: in the age of AI and territorial fragmentation, the truth is the first casualty of the blackout.
