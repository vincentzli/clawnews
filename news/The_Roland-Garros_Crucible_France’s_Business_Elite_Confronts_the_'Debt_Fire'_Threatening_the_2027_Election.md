# The Roland-Garros Crucible: France’s Business Elite Confronts the 'Debt Fire' Threatening the 2027 Election

The red clay of Stade Roland-Garros, usually the stage for grueling tennis matches, was transformed on August 27, 2026, into a different kind of arena. Under the late summer Paris sun, the annual summer forum of the Mouvement des Entreprises de France (MEDEF), known as La REF, became the setting for the first major debate of the 2027 French presidential campaign. Seven declared or probable presidential candidates took the stage before a crowd of thousands of anxious business leaders, entrepreneurs, and executives. The official theme of the forum was "Le Courage" (Courage), but the real topic on everyone’s mind was France’s ballooning public debt, which has now soared past 116% of GDP, representing a ticking time bomb at the heart of Europe’s second-largest economy.

At the center of the storm is Jean-Luc Mélenchon, the radical left-wing candidate representing La France Insoumise (LFI). Currently polling second in first-round projections for the April 2027 election, Mélenchon is positioned as a strong contender to qualify for the second-round runoff against the far-right candidate Marine Le Pen. His rise has raised concerns among business leaders and centrist politicians, primarily due to his radical fiscal proposals. During his campaign, Mélenchon summarized his approach to the nation’s debt with a characteristic provocation: "All we have to do is take the 18% held by the Bank of France and chuck it in the fire."

This proposal—to cancel or freeze the portion of French public debt held by the national central bank as part of the Eurosystem—has roiled French politics. In Mélenchon’s view, because the Banque de France is a public institution, this debt is effectively a "debt we owe to ourselves." Proponents, including prominent left-wing intellectual and investment banker Matthieu Pigasse, argue that erasing these liabilities from the central bank’s balance sheet would be a harmless accounting entry, instantly shaving off billions of euros from France’s headline debt figures and freeing up vital fiscal space to fund public services and ecological transitions.

However, the French business community and moderate political rivals view the proposal as a grave threat to France's financial stability. During the three-hour debate at Roland-Garros, the clash over this "debt fire" reached a fever pitch. Former Prime Minister Édouard Philippe, now leading the centre-right Horizons party, launched a scathing attack on Mélenchon's plan, warning that such a radical step would "result in France being shut out of the banking system" as international investors lost all faith in the country's creditworthiness. 

On online forums like Reddit, defenders of Mélenchon’s approach point to Japan's massive debt load, arguing that debt held domestically is not a structural threat. One commentator argued that if the state effectively owes the money to its own central bank, it is a ledger entry rather than a debt to foreign creditors. However, detractors on the platform quickly counter that France lacks Japan’s monetary sovereignty. As another user noted, France no longer has its own currency, and under Eurozone rules, unilaterally burning debt is equivalent to a default, which would trigger a catastrophic rating downgrade.

The centrist government, led by Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu, has also gone on the offensive. Lecornu condemned the plan as "fraud in its purest form," warning that it would destroy investor confidence and send interest rates skyrocketing. Economy Minister Roland Lescure echoed these sentiments, labeling the proposal "pure fraud" and pointing to the violation of European Union treaties that strictly forbid central banks from directly financing national governments. Critics note that under Article 123 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU), monetary financing is illegal, and any attempt to unilaterally cancel debt held by the Eurosystem would trigger a constitutional crisis with the European Central Bank and potentially force France out of the Eurozone.

As the political landscape shifts ahead of the autumn campaign, the debate is widening. On Sunday, August 30, 2026, Socialist Party leader Olivier Faure entered the race, confirming he will run in the centre-left primary against Raphaël Glucksmann. Meanwhile, former Prime Minister Dominique de Villepin has indicated a potential bid, declaring his intent to bypass the primary system and seek the necessary 500 endorsements from elected officials to run as an independent candidate with center-right roots.

Even Marine Le Pen, the leader of the National Rally (RN) who has historically championed populist economic policies, used the MEDEF forum to strike a more moderate, business-friendly chord. Le Pen sought to reassure the audience that she would honor France's public debt, proposing instead a €125 billion cost-cutting plan to put public finances back on track. This stance contrasts sharply with Mélenchon's unilateral approach, highlighting the deep ideological divisions on the left and right.

MEDEF President Patrick Martin made it clear that while the business community is prepared to work with whichever political majority emerges from the 2027 election, it is deeply alarmed by the fiscal proposals of the extremes. "Responsible growth must be our priority," Martin told the forum, warning that France cannot tax or write off its way out of a structural competitiveness crisis. The warnings were echoed by European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, who delivered a speech at the forum emphasizing that Europe's "economic certainties" are vanishing and that the continent must undergo a massive industrial transformation rather than succumb to fiscal illusions.

With France's interest payments now rivaling the national education budget, the debate over how to handle the country's sovereign liabilities is no longer an academic exercise. It is a central battleground that will define the future of the French state and the stability of the Eurozone.
