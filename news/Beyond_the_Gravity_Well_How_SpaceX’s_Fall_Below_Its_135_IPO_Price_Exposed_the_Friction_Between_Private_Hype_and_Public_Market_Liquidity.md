# Beyond the Gravity Well: How SpaceX’s Fall Below Its $135 IPO Price Exposed the Friction Between Private Hype and Public Market Liquidity

When SpaceX priced its initial public offering on June 12, 2026, at $135 per share, lead underwriters Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley—who pocketed roughly $100 million each in underwriting fees—declared it a structural watershed for capital markets. Raising $75 billion in net proceeds at an initial market valuation of $1.77 trillion, ticker symbol SPCX was framed not merely as an aerospace debut, but as the benchmark asset for next-generation orbital infrastructure and AI compute integration following its acquisition of xAI. For seventy-two hours, public markets appeared to validate the narrative: by June 16, retail buying pressure pushed SPCX to an intraday high of $225.64 per share, briefly driving the company’s market capitalization above $2.95 trillion.

That triumph proved ephemeral. By July 18, 2026, SPCX has broken below its offering price to trade at $124.50 per share, marking a 44.8% drawdown from its mid-June peak and erasing over $1.32 trillion in market capitalization. The immediate catalyst arrived on July 16, 2026, at SpaceX’s Starbase facility in Boca Chica, Texas, when the 13th test flight of the Starship vehicle suffered an automatic engine abort at T-0 seconds due to a igniter sequence failure in two Raptor 3 engines. Though SpaceX target-dated a rescheduled launch for July 20, 2026, the operational hiccup triggered a cascading selloff across both retail portfolios and institutional desks. 

Underneath the launch pad drama lies a deeper structural conflict: the friction between private-market valuation mechanics and public-market liquidity. In private funding rounds, SpaceX had been marked up based on low float velocity and long-term TAM (Total Addressable Market) assumptions. But in public trading, where daily turnover demands immediate fundamental support, investors quickly calculated that SPCX’s initial $1.77 trillion price tag represented a price-to-sales multiple of nearly 118x on projected 2026 revenues of $15 billion—a premium that left zero margin for operational friction.

The price breakdown has ignited fierce debates across financial social media, exposing severe drawdowns among retail traders who chased the debut. On r/wallstreetbets, where call options volume spiked to record levels during the mid-June rally, the sentiment has rapidly pivoted from euphoria to capitulation. One heavily upvoted post by user u/RocketBagholder summarized the damage: "Bought 500 shares at $210 after the hype, thinking $300 was guaranteed before lockup. Now I’m down $42,750 and holding bags below IPO price while my $140 August calls are down 92%." Another trader on r/stocks noted: "Private market valuations are purely visual art until public order books demand real cash flow. A 118x P/S ratio in a high-interest-rate environment under the Trump administration’s current trade policy was always a trap for retail."

Short-sellers have capitalized aggressively on the momentum. Exchange data indicates that institutional short interest in SPCX surged from 6.2% of float in late June to 29.1% by mid-July, representing over $6.2 billion in aggregate short positioning. Bearish funds have exploited the tight public float—which stands at just 555 million Class A shares, or roughly 5% of total outstanding shares—to drive down prices on negative headlines. Fintwit commentary has reflected this institutional battle. Prominent market analyst @MacroCap Strategy posted on X: "SPCX breaking $135 is the ultimate reality check for mega-cap mega-valuation debuts. The math was broken from Day 1: a $75B capital raise into a 5% float created an artificial supply squeeze. Now short interest is 29% and the August 6 lockup unlock is looming."

That lockup expiration represents the primary overhang haunting institutional trading desks. Following SpaceX’s second-quarter earnings report, scheduled for August 6, 2026, the first post-IPO lockup period will expire, unlocking an estimated 1.1 billion Class A shares previously restricted to early venture investors and employees. With over $135 billion in potential supply hitting a market where daily trading volume averages just 32 million shares, fears of dilutive dumping have intensified. As Bloomberg terminal commentary noted earlier this week: "The supply-demand imbalance facing SPCX in August could dwarfen previous tech unlock drawdowns unless management delivers flawless execution on Starship payload cadence."

Despite the selling pressure, contrarian buyers are attempting to construct a floor. Cathie Wood’s ARK Innovation ETF (ARKK) disclosed a purchase of 1.2 million shares on July 17 at an average price of $125.80, citing Starlink’s 68% year-over-year subscriber growth and expected orbital satellite constellation margins exceeding 70%. Yet with Federal Reserve Chairman Jerome Powell keeping benchmark interest rates at 4.25%-4.50% and macro liquidity constrained, institutional investors appear unwilling to underwrite multi-trillion-dollar valuation multiples without tangible earnings support. Whether SPCX recovers above its $135 IPO price following the July 20 Starship launch attempt or descends further into its August lockup cliff remains the most consequential debate on Wall Street today.
