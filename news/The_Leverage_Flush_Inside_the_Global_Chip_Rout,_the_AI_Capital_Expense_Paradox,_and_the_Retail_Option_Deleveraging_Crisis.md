# **The Leverage Flush: Inside the Global Chip Rout, the AI Capital Expense Paradox, and the Retail Option Deleveraging Crisis**

####

The global technology trade, long heralded as a one-way bet on the infinite scaling of artificial intelligence, suffered its most violent fracture of the decade on Tuesday, July 28, 2026. At the epicenter of the damage was Micron Technology Inc. (NASDAQ: [MU](file:///Users/vzl/.gemini/antigravity-cli/scratch/mu_data.txt)), which plummeted 8.85% in a single session to close at $820.53, down from Monday's close of $900.20. The drop marked a brutal milestone: a 34.6% plunge from its June 25 all-time high of $1,255.00. The collateral damage extended across the Pacific, as Seoul’s KOSPI index closed down 10.84%, dragged to the earth by its two corporate titans. Samsung Electronics Co. plummeted 13.39% to 220,000 KRW, while SK Hynix Inc. collapsed 14.65% to close at 1.55 million KRW. Meanwhile, the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) shed 4.5% on the day, placing it on track for a 29% decline in July—its worst month since the dot-com collapse of 2002.

Beneath the raw price action lies a cascading deleveraging event fueled by retail option traders and leveraged derivative instruments. In South Korea, retail accounts using high-leverage Contracts for Difference (CFDs) and 3x leveraged exchange-traded products faced margin calls that triggered automated sell-offs. The contagion spread directly to U.S. option chains. On Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets, where speculative call buying has acted as a key source of retail gamma for chipmakers, the mood shifted from euphoric dip-buying to sheer panic. One retail trader, observing the carnage in the South Korean overnight session, posted: "My 450k in memory calls are in the hands of the country that has had nonstop circuit breakers for the past month please pray for me." Only weeks earlier, on July 14, the prevailing sentiment was typified by users asserting that "MU is on sale and it'll rip to $1500 in the next few weeks." By late July, the humor turned gallows-esque: "Micron only at -2% so basically green," commented a user on July 10 as the stock began its structural descent.

The primary anxiety crushing the sector is the viability of the AI capital expenditure model. Investors are growing increasingly skeptical of the multi-billion-dollar infrastructure spends of the "hyperscalers"—Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, and Amazon—ahead of their crucial earnings reports. This skepticism was brought to a boil by leaks regarding Nvidia Corp.’s (NASDAQ: NVDA) advanced negotiations to provide a massive financial backstop to OpenAI. According to market reports, Nvidia, whose shares settled at $196.51 after a volatile session, is in talks to guarantee up to $250 billion in lease and debt financing to help OpenAI lease a 10-gigawatt data center campus in Piketon, Ohio. Developed by SoftBank’s SB Energy on a decommissioned uranium-enrichment site, the total project investment, including hardware, could top $500 billion, with Nvidia reportedly offering another $350 billion in direct chip financing.

For institutional investors, these credit guarantees carry the distinct odor of "vendor financing" from the late 1990s, when telecommunications giants like Cisco Systems and Lucent Technologies financed their own customers' equipment purchases, leading to a catastrophic wave of defaults. If Nvidia must backstop the credit of its primary customer to justify its chip demand, the "circular economy" of the AI hardware trade may be closer to its limit than bulls acknowledge. The anxiety has been further stoked by corporate insiders. Micron President and CEO Sanjay Mehrotra sold 31,285 shares on July 24 under a Rule 10b5-1 trading plan adopted on January 30, 2026. The sales, executed at weighted-average prices between $906.48 and $965.85, netted approximately $29.2 million. While scheduled months in advance, the optics of the chief executive divesting millions in equity as retail options holders faced margin calls did little to soothe nerves.

Simultaneously, the structural narrative of a supply-constrained High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) "supercycle" extending into 2028 is facing competitive headwinds. The initial public offering of Chinese memory manufacturer ChangXin Memory Technologies (CXMT) has raised fears of an impending oversupply of standard DRAM, which could bleed into the premium HBM space. Compounding this, reports emerged that a Chinese state-backed consortium has commenced mass production of domestic Deep Ultraviolet (DUV) lithography machines. This technological leap threatens to break the monopoly of Western toolmakers and accelerate China's memory self-sufficiency, challenging the pricing power of Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix.

Faced with this triple threat—a retail leverage unwind, vendor-financing fears, and a rising Chinese competitive challenge—investors are retreating. The market is no longer pricing in infinite demand; it is demanding proof of monetization. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Federal Reserve Chair Jerome Powell are watching these dynamics closely as the Fed prepares for its upcoming policy meeting. The era of "growth at any cost" has officially collided with the reality of credit limits and margin calls, leaving retail option books in tatters and leaving the broader market to debate whether this is a temporary rotation or the structural end of the silicon boom.
