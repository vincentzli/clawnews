# Drones Over Diapers: How AeroVironment’s $1.5 Billion Backlog Exposed Corporate America’s Great Consumer Divide

When the closing bells rang on Wednesday, September 9, 2026, corporate earnings reports offered two irreconcilable visions of the American economy. In one, capital was flowing with near-limitless velocity into autonomous warfare and state-sponsored defense technologies. In the other, households across middle America were running out of cash, counting pennies at gasoline pumps, and shelving discretionary purchases.

The split was laid bare in the quarterly filings of four disparate corporations: autonomous drone manufacturer AeroVironment Inc., sporting goods chain Academy Sports + Outdoors Inc., specialty retailer Signet Jewelers Ltd., and Midwestern convenience staple Casey’s General Stores Inc. While defense contractors trade on sovereign geopolitical anxiety, traditional retailers are executing financial acrobatics to mask a deteriorating consumer core.

In Simi Valley, California, AeroVironment reported fiscal 2027 first-quarter revenue of $480.5 million—a 6% year-over-year increase—and non-GAAP adjusted earnings per share of $0.59, crushing Wall Street consensus estimates of $0.24. Even as integration expenses tied to its $4.1 billion all-stock buyout of BlueHalo in May 2025 drove a GAAP net loss of $5.1 million, investors looked exclusively at the order pipeline. Funded backlog reached a record $1.5 billion, a 37% jump year-over-year and 23% sequentially. Factoring in unfunded awards, total backlog stood at $2.8 billion, bolstered by a 1.4 book-to-bill ratio on quarterly bookings of $683 million. The operational catalyst was a landmark $464.8 million contract from the U.S. Army for its LOCUST X3 directed-energy counter-drone laser system under the E-HEL program, accompanied by a $50 million international direct commercial sale.

AeroVironment shares rallied 9.6% on Thursday to close near $153.50, taking its enterprise value to roughly $7.5 billion. On Wall Street, JPMorgan analyst Seth Seifman lifted his price target from $200 to $210, maintaining an Overweight rating. Across social trading platforms, retail sentiment reached a fever pitch. "AVAV is the only pure-play drone moat converting conflict directly into backlog," noted one widely shared post on StockTwits, while on Reddit’s r/stocks, an investor argued that "autonomous loitering munitions aren't subject to consumer discretion; Uncle Sam’s checkbook never bounces."

Yet across the consumer landscape, Uncle Sam’s citizens are clearly exhausting their borrowing capacity.

At Academy Sports + Outdoors, net sales rose 3.0% to $1.65 billion for the second quarter, but comparable-store sales dropped 0.4%. While adjusted earnings of $2.31 per share beat estimates of $2.09, prompting a 14% relief rally, chief executive Steve Lawrence offered an unvarnished post-mortem of household balance sheets. Foot traffic from families earning less than $50,000 per year fell at a high-single-digit rate, balanced only by high-single-digit traffic gains among households earning more than $100,000. Lower-income shoppers, Lawrence noted, restricted their spending almost exclusively to four promotional holidays: Memorial Day, Father's Day, the Fourth of July, and back-to-school. Academy’s gross margin expansion to 40.4% was not driven by robust pricing power, but by a 510-basis-point windfall from tariff refunds and aggressive freight re-negotiations.

The bifurcated consumer was equally visible at Signet Jewelers. The company reported adjusted EPS of $2.19, topping the $1.74 consensus, while comparable sales rose 2.2% on net sales of $1.53 billion. Shares surged nearly 24%, cheered by a raised fiscal 2027 EPS target of $10.45 to $12.15. Yet beneath the headline beat overseen by chief executive J.K. Symancyk—who took the helm in November 2024 following the retirement of Virginia Drosos—demand told an exclusionary tale. Unit sales for fine jewelry priced above $2,000 grew at high single digits, while accessible fashion jewelry under $500 languished. Signet's primary liquidity engine came not from organic store traffic, but from a restructured credit agreement with Bread Financial through 2035, securing $80 million in upfront cash for the third quarter and an estimated $1 billion in long-term operating income, which funded a $125 million accelerated share repurchase program.

Where financial engineering failed to cushion valuation, the market showed no mercy. Casey’s General Stores reported diluted EPS of $7.37, up 27.7%, on $5.68 billion in revenue. In-store prepared food sales grew 4.8%, driven by double-digit volume growth in whole pizzas as families substituted sit-down casual dining for convenience-store takeout. Yet Casey's stock cratered 14.2% on Wednesday, shedding $104.46 to close at $629.03. Same-store fuel gallons slipped 0.3%, exposing the reality that operating profit was almost wholly dependent on an unsustainable fuel margin of 47.8 cents per gallon—up 6.8 cents from the prior year. Analysts at KeyBanc promptly slashed their price target from $970 to $780, while UBS cut its target to $720, concluding that a premium 28-times earnings multiple could not withstand volume declines in non-discretionary commuting.

"Casey's just reminded everyone what happens when a 30x multiple runs into negative unit volume," observed one macro trader on X.com. "You can only charge 48 cents of fuel margin for so long before the driver stops driving."

This corporate divergence mirrors official macroeconomic distress. Data from the Bureau of Economic Analysis pegged the U.S. personal saving rate at just 3.0% in July 2026, down from 3.7% a year prior and well below historical norms. Total household debt hit $18.8 trillion in the second quarter, according to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, with credit card delinquency rates at commercial banks persisting at 2.92%—their highest sustained levels since 2011.

With Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh—sworn in this past May—maintaining a cautious approach toward monetary easing, and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent steering federal fiscal policy under President Donald Trump and Vice President JD Vance, the tension between sovereign defense appropriations and consumer solvency has reached an inflection point.

The open question for institutional investors is whether autonomous defense multiples can remain insulated from the domestic economy. AeroVironment commands an enterprise value near $7.5 billion, trading at roughly 3.5 times forward revenue and a negative trailing GAAP price-to-earnings multiple following heavy amortization and integration costs from BlueHalo. Bulls argue that sovereign defense spending is non-cyclical, protected by multi-year procurement authorizations under the National Defense Authorization Act.

However, defense hardware is ultimately funded by tax revenues and sovereign debt issuance. If the bottom 60% of consumers continue to retrench, corporate tax receipts from retail and discretionary sectors will erode. A government confronting an $18.8 trillion household debt burden and climbing Treasury yields cannot subsidize defense technology multiples indefinitely. When consumer discretionary cash flows evaporate, even the most formidable military backlogs cannot permanently defy gravity.
