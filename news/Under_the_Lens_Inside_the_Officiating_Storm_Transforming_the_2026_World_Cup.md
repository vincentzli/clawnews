# Under the Lens: Inside the Officiating Storm Transforming the 2026 World Cup

The promise of the 2026 FIFA World Cup was one of grand scale and historic inclusivity: 48 teams, 104 matches, and a month-long celebration of football stretched across three North American nations. Yet, as the tournament moves deep into its knockout stages, the dominant narrative is not the expansion of the game’s borders, but the tightening of its rules. A series of highly controversial refereeing decisions in the Round of 32 has ignited a global debate over the integrity of officiating, the overreach of Video Assistant Referee (VAR) technology, and the new, hyper-regulated guidelines introduced by FIFA’s Chief Refereeing Officer, Pierluigi Collina.

From Levi’s Stadium in Santa Clara to the Atlanta Stadium and Seattle’s Lumen Field, the drama of the pitch is increasingly being overshadowed by the quiet deliberations of the VAR booth. What was designed to eliminate "clear and obvious errors" has instead created an environment of intense scrutiny where natural contact is replayed in slow-motion and reinterpreted through a microscopic lens, altering the course of the tournament’s biggest contenders.

The storm reached a peak in Santa Clara on July 1, during the United States Men’s National Team’s 2-0 victory over Bosnia and Herzegovina. U.S. forward Folarin Balogun had been the hero of the first half, scoring a clinical opening goal to put the host nation ahead. But in the 64th minute, the match turned on its head. While challenging for a loose ball, Balogun lost his footing and made contact with the ankle of Bosnian defender Tarik Muharemović. The contact occurred as Balogun attempted to plant his foot after losing his balance, leaving Muharemović on the turf. 

Brazilian referee Raphael Claus initially signaled a simple foul, but he was quickly summoned to the pitchside monitor by the VAR. After viewing repeated, slowed-down replays of the point of contact, Claus returned to the field and brandished a straight red card. The decision forced the USMNT to play the final 26 minutes with ten men and automatically ruled Balogun out of the highly anticipated Round of 16 clash against Belgium.

In the post-match press conference, USMNT head coach Mauricio Pochettino did not hide his anger. "It is never a red card," Pochettino said, his voice tense. "It was a completely accidental collision, a normal action in football. If you watch it in real-time, it is a contact sport. But when you freeze the frame, every challenge looks like a crime." Pochettino's frustration is compounded by the fact that U.S. Soccer has no mechanism to appeal the decision. Under FIFA regulations, referee decisions on the field are final, leaving the Americans without their starting striker for the knockout rounds.

Former Select Group referee and television analyst Andy Davies echoed Pochettino’s sentiments, pointing to the structural flaws in the VAR review process. Davies argued that recommending a review based on slow-motion and static frames violates the spirit of the protocol. "By showing the referee only the point of impact rather than the full speed of the run, the VAR is guiding the official toward a predetermined conclusion," Davies noted. "It strips away the context of momentum and intent."

While the Americans managed to defend their lead and secure a second goal through Malik Tillman, other teams were not as fortunate. In Seattle, the clash between Belgium and Senegal descended into absolute chaos. Senegal had built a commanding 2-0 lead through Habib Diarra and Ismaïla Sarr, seemingly cruising toward the Round of 16. But Belgium mounted a furious late comeback, with Romelu Lukaku scoring in the 86th minute and Youri Tielemans equalizing in the 89th to force extra time.

At 2-2, the match entered the final seconds of the second period of extra time. In the 125th minute, with a penalty shootout looming, Tielemans went down in the box under a challenge from Senegal's Lamine Camara. The contact was highly controversial, with replays showing minimal contact that many pundits argued did not warrant a foul. Honduran referee Said Martinez did not whistle for a foul initially, but he was instructed by the VAR to review the incident. After a lengthy delay at the monitor, Martinez pointed to the spot. Tielemans stepped up and converted the penalty at 124 minutes and 44 seconds—officially the latest goal in World Cup history—to win the match 3-2.

The decision triggered immediate outrage. On Reddit's r/soccer and r/worldcup, post-match threads exploded with fans accusing the officials of "robbing" Senegal, leading to heavily moderated threads and heated debates. The pundits on ITV Sport were equally scathing. Former Manchester United captain Roy Keane called the penalty "a bit harsh" and blasted Martinez for his hesitation. "I want to see some conviction from the referee," Keane said. "If you need to spend three minutes looking at ten different angles of a soft touch, it is not a clear and obvious error. Senegal were hard done by, though they also found a way to lose after being 2-0 up." Gary Neville agreed, stating flatly that it was not a foul. In contrast, Senegal coach Pape Thiaw refused to criticize the officiating directly, muttering only that "football can be cruel" during a brief post-match interview.

These incidents are not isolated occurrences but rather the result of a deliberate shift in FIFA's refereeing philosophy. Ahead of the 2026 tournament, Collina introduced a sweep of expanded VAR guidelines. For the first time, VAR is authorized to review second yellow cards, corner kick awards that lead to goals, and attacking obstructions. 

The consequences of these rules were felt most acutely by Germany. In their Round of 32 match against Paraguay on June 29, Germany was eliminated in a penalty shootout after a 1-1 draw. But the defining moment came in the 105th minute of extra time, when Jonathan Tah’s headed goal was disallowed. A VAR review determined that Waldemar Anton had obstructed Paraguay goalkeeper Orlando Gill. Germany head coach Julian Nagelsmann was furious, calling the decision a "scandal" and arguing that Anton was merely holding his ground. Collina later defended the call, stating that referees were specifically instructed to penalize attacking players who block opponents without playing the ball. 

The expanded 48-team format has also drawn fire from critics who argue it has diluted the quality of officiating. With more matches than ever before, FIFA has had to draw referees from a wider pool of confederations. While officials like Adham Mohammad of Jordan managed a relatively quiet game during England’s 2-1 comeback win over the Democratic Republic of the Congo in Atlanta, matches in Santa Clara and Seattle have highlighted the immense pressure on refereeing crews. The sheer volume of games, combined with the complexity of the new VAR protocols, has created a system that feels over-engineered and inconsistent.

As the tournament moves toward the Round of 16, FIFA faces a crisis of confidence. The technology designed to bring clarity to the sport has instead introduced a new level of subjective controversy, leaving teams like Senegal and the United States to ponder what might have been had the game been left to the men on the pitch rather than the screens in the booth.
