# The Invisible Primary of 2028: How the 2026 Midterms Became a Proxy War for a Post-Trump Era

The 2028 U.S. presidential election represents the first time since 1884 that the American electorate will face consecutive open races without an incumbent president on the ballot. With President Donald Trump term-limited, the political landscape has descended into a quiet but ferocious scramble for succession. As the November 2026 midterm elections approach, potential contenders in both parties are engaged in a high-stakes proxy war, using down-ballot campaigns to lay the groundwork for their national networks.

For the Republicans, the contest is characterized by ideological distinctions highlighted by political observers. Vice President JD Vance, who has deferred any official decision on a 2028 bid until after the midterms, represents the populist "New Right," characterized by protectionist economic policies and a restrained, non-interventionist foreign policy. Opposing him in speculation is Secretary of State Marco Rubio, representing the traditional, hawkish conservative establishment.

Early polling shows the two administration heavyweights in a statistical dead heat. A May 2026 Emerson College poll of likely Republican primary voters showed Vance leading Rubio by a single percentage point, 36% to 35%, with Ron DeSantis and Nikki Haley trailing far behind at 5% each. This represented a dramatic tightening from February 2026, when Vance held a commanding 52% to Rubio’s 20%. By July 2026, the Emerson poll showed Vance at 39% and Rubio at 38%, underscoring a persistent gridlock within the party's base. The polling data reveals a distinct demographic divide: Rubio leads Vance among voters over 50 (41% to 35% in the May poll), while Vance maintains a strong lead among voters under 50 (37% to 26%).

The rivalry has forced both men to navigate different political and legal realities. As Vice President, Vance is exempt from Hatch Act restrictions, allowing him to travel the country headlining fundraisers and endorsing down-ballot allies, such as Republican candidates like Barry Moore in Alabama and incumbent Susan Collins in Maine. He has been a primary fundraising draw for the Republican National Committee, leveraging his office to build loyalty among the state party chairs who will control the 2028 primary process.

Meanwhile, Secretary Rubio is constrained by the strictures of the Hatch Act, which prohibits federal employees from engaging in partisan political activity. This legal boundary has already sparked controversy. In October 2025, House Democratic leaders, including Representative Gregory Meeks and Senator Jeanne Shaheen, demanded an investigation into the State Department, alleging that Rubio used official communications and the department’s website for partisan messaging during a government shutdown. Deprived of the ability to campaign openly, Rubio has relied on his high diplomatic profile—particularly his handling of escalating conflicts in the Middle East—to maintain his national standing, while his political allies build a quiet donor network behind the scenes.

This proxy conflict is also unfolding in the special elections to fill the Senate seats vacated by Vance and Rubio. In Ohio, Governor Mike DeWine appointed Lieutenant Governor Jon Husted to Vance’s former seat, and Husted is now running in the November 2026 special election. In Florida, Governor Ron DeSantis appointed Attorney General Ashley Moody to Rubio’s seat, where she is now running in the November 2026 special election. The outcome of these races will be viewed as a direct test of the Vance and Rubio brands in their home states.

On the Democratic side, the field is equally fluid but lacks a clear heir apparent. Former Vice President Kamala Harris holds a nominal lead in early surveys, but her support remains soft. In the July 2026 Big Data Poll of 1,338 likely Democratic voters, Harris led the field at 29.7%, followed by California Governor Gavin Newsom at 14.3%. Progressive Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez polled at 8.6%, with former Secretary of Transportation Pete Buttigieg at 7.4% and Governor Josh Shapiro at 6.5%.

Despite her lead, Harris faces skepticism. Political commentators on platforms like Reddit's r/politics argue that her support is driven primarily by name recognition rather than a consolidated base. One commenter noted that the early polls represent "mostly name recognition at this point," and another user wrote that Newsom represents the "worst excesses" of California politics, questioning his appeal in the Rust Belt. To combat this perception, Harris launched the "Kamala HQ" digital platform in February 2026 to coordinate grassroots activities. She has also begun a selective national tour, addressing the Nevada Democratic Party in May and the National Urban League in Nashville in August, warning donors that "2028 won't matter if we don't win 2026."

Governor Newsom, who is term-limited in California and cannot seek re-election in 2026, has been much more aggressive. Using his Campaign for Democracy PAC, Newsom has raised millions of dollars to support Democratic candidates in swing districts, traveling to Nevada, Michigan, and beyond. Newsom has indicated he will "seriously consider" a 2028 run after the midterms, positioning himself as a national leader capable of taking the fight to red states.

Other Democratic contenders are using the midterms to expand their footprints. Former Secretary of Transportation Pete Buttigieg has visited over 30 states since the beginning of the midterm cycle, speaking at the Mackinac Policy Conference in Michigan and headlining the Iowa Democratic Party's Liberty and Justice Celebration. By campaigning in traditionally conservative or rural areas, Buttigieg is attempting to build a unique coalition of moderate and independent voters.

In Pennsylvania, Governor Josh Shapiro is actively running for reelection to a second term against Republican State Treasurer Stacy Garrity in the November 2026 midterms. To expand his profile, Shapiro focused on a national book tour for his new memoir, *Where We Keep the Light*, published in January 2026. Shapiro has also launched the "Rise Up Pennsylvania" initiative to help state Democrats flip four Republican-held congressional seats, demonstrating his fundraising prowess and down-ballot coattails.

Meanwhile, Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez has built a war chest of over $15 million in her reelection campaign for New York’s 14th district. She has used her financial and political capital to support progressive candidates nationwide, including rallies in Michigan, establishing herself as the undisputed leader of the party's left wing.

As the 2026 midterms enter their final stretch, the candidates are not just campaigning for their parties—they are campaigning for themselves. The results of November 3, 2026, will not only decide the balance of power in Congress but will also redraw the map for the 2028 race.
