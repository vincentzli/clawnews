# The $36 Billion Turf War: How Albany’s Crackdown on Kalshi Threatens the Future of Prediction Markets

##

On the afternoon of July 31, 2026, a legal dispute was initiated in Manhattan. New York Governor Kathy Hochul and Attorney General Letitia James filed a lawsuit in the Supreme Court of the State of New York, alleging that prediction market platform KalshiEX, LLC was operating an unlicensed, illegal gambling business. Within eight hours, Kalshi’s legal team filed a notice of removal, transferring the state-court action to the U.S. District Court for the Southern District of New York. 

This jurisdictional move marked the opening of a legal battle that will define the boundaries of the digital derivatives market. At its core, the litigation represents a direct conflict between state-level police powers over gambling and the federal preemption authority of the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC). The financial remedies sought by the state are unprecedented: New York is seeking a court order to halt Kalshi's operations in the state, the disgorgement of all profits from New York-based transactions, restitution for users, treble damages, and statutory civil penalties of up to $100,000 for each individual offering of unlicensed sports wagering. Given the platform's high transaction volumes, analysts estimate the cumulative financial penalties could reach as high as $36 billion. 

The litigation follows a significant increase in prediction market activity during the summer of 2026. In June 2026, the global prediction market sector recorded a revised record of $46.95 billion in notional trading volume. Kalshi led the domestic market, capturing approximately $33 billion of that total—representing roughly 70% of the industry's volume. The primary driver of this trading activity was the 2026 FIFA World Cup. Kalshi’s World Cup-related contracts generated over $27 billion in trading volume, driven by match-winner markets and tournament props. To facilitate the settlement of these contracts, Kalshi entered into a partnership with Genius Sports to utilize official, real-time sports data feeds.

Unlike traditional sportsbooks where bettors wager against the house at fixed odds, Kalshi operates a peer-to-peer exchange where users trade binary "Yes/No" contracts. The price of a contract fluctuates between 1 cent and 99 cents based on market demand and probability, and traders can sell their positions before the underlying event concludes to secure profits or limit losses. 

New York state authorities, however, reject this distinction. "No matter what they call themselves, prediction markets like Kalshi are gambling platforms, plain and simple," Attorney General Letitia James said in a statement. "By ignoring our laws, Kalshi is running an illegal operation and harming New Yorkers in the process."

The state’s complaint focuses on New York’s consumer protection laws and minimum age limits. New York law restricts mobile sports wagering to individuals aged 21 and older. Kalshi, operating as a federally regulated exchange, permits individuals aged 18 to 20 to open accounts and trade contracts. New York officials contend that by allowing 18-to-20-year-olds to trade sports-related contracts, Kalshi is facilitating underage gambling. 

The conflict also carries significant fiscal implications. New York imposes a 51% tax rate on the gross gaming revenues of mobile sports wagering operators—the highest in the United States. This tax framework generated over $1.3 billion in tax revenue for the state in 2025, and $109.5 million in July 2026 alone. These tax proceeds are directed to public education, youth sports programs, and problem gambling treatment services. Because Kalshi operates under federal commodities regulations rather than a state gaming license, it does not pay the 51% state sports betting tax. 

"Kalshi has chosen to ignore New York's gaming laws, which exist to protect consumers, prevent problematic gambling, deliver funding for critical public services, and ensure that every company plays by the same rules," Governor Kathy Hochul said. "This choice has consequences."

Kalshi’s defense is centered on the doctrine of federal preemption. In 2020, the CFTC registered Kalshi as a Designated Contract Market (DCM), authorizing it to offer event contracts under the Commodity Exchange Act (CEA). Kalshi argues that because the CEA grants the CFTC exclusive jurisdiction over federally registered derivatives exchanges, state gaming laws cannot be used to regulate or halt its operations.

During an appearance on CNBC’s *Squawk Box* on August 3, 2026, Kalshi CEO Tarek Mansour characterized the lawsuit as an effort by legacy industries to stifle competition. "I think the more interesting thing that's at play here is that you have an industry, the prediction market industry, that is disruptive, that is growing fast, consumers are adopting it, and it's threatening a legacy incumbent industry that is unhappy about that," Mansour said. He compared the platform's regulatory challenges to those previously faced by ride-sharing and home-sharing companies. "The playbook is very simple. It's: Litigate. Then you try to legislate. And then finally, when you realize that consumer demand is not going to go away, you try to compete and innovate. That's the cycle that we're going through right now. It's played out with taxis and Uber. It's played out with hotels and Airbnb." Mansour added that if New York successfully shuts down the platform, "they're gonna have a lot of pissed off New Yorkers."

The CFTC has actively defended its regulatory jurisdiction. Under Chairman Michael S. Selig, the federal agency has filed lawsuits against several states—including Arizona, Connecticut, Illinois, and Wisconsin—to prevent them from applying state gambling laws to federally registered prediction market exchanges. "To any state that seeks to nullify federal law and seize authority over these markets, I say again: we will see you in court," Selig stated. The agency maintains that a fragmented patchwork of state rules would undermine market integrity and compromise consumer protections.

However, federal courts have been cautious about blocking state-level actions. The New York lawsuit was preceded by a legal setback for Kalshi on July 7, 2026, when U.S. District Judge Analisa Torres denied Kalshi's motion for a preliminary injunction against the New York State Gaming Commission. Judge Torres rejected Kalshi's argument that the CEA preempted state gambling laws, allowing the commission to maintain a cease-and-desist order originally issued in October 2025. Kalshi appealed that ruling to the Second Circuit on July 8.

The legal battle escalated on August 4, 2026, when U.S. District Judge Jed S. Rakoff denied the CFTC’s emergency request for a temporary restraining order (TRO) to halt New York’s state-level lawsuit. Judge Rakoff ruled that the CFTC had failed to demonstrate a "high likelihood of success on the merits" or proof of "irreparable harm" that would justify federal intervention at that stage. The denial was issued "without prejudice," allowing the CFTC to renew its request before U.S. District Judge Victor Marrero by August 7, 2026.

As both parties submit their renewed filings, the litigation in the Southern District of New York remains a critical test case. The outcome will determine whether individual states can enforce local gaming laws and tax structures against federally regulated derivatives exchanges, shaping the regulatory landscape for the wider prediction market industry.
