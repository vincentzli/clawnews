# **Pennies in Front of Falcon 9s: The High Opportunity Cost of Selling Covered Calls on the Space Economy**

####

On the retail options forums of Reddit and the fast-moving stock feeds of StockTwits, a bitter tactical debate has broken out over how to capture the commercialization of outer space. With capital expenditures ballooning and satellite constellations finally ascending into orbit, shares of high-beta growth stocks like AST SpaceMobile Inc. (NASDAQ: ASTS) and Rocket Lab USA Inc. (NASDAQ: RKLB) have undergone wild, earnings-driven price swings. For the retail trading community, which spent years nursing paper losses on these pre-revenue and early-revenue moonshots, the dramatic rallies of the past two years presented a tactical fork in the road: do you buy and hold the equity, or do you sell out-of-the-money covered calls to harvest the sky-high implied volatility?

The argument for selling covered calls is rooted in the quest for immediate yield. In an environment where the Federal Reserve, under Chair Kevin Warsh—who took the oath of office on May 22, 2026—kept interest rates unchanged at its July 2026 meeting, the effective federal funds rate remains pegged at 3.63%. With Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent steering a fiscal path focused on debt stability and the target interest rate range sitting at 3.50% to 3.75%, the prospect of collecting double-digit annualized premiums is highly tempting. Because ASTS and RKLB frequently exhibit implied volatility levels exceeding 100%, option writers can easily collect 4% to 8% in monthly premiums by selling calls that are 20% or 25% out-of-the-money. To some, this feels like an investment hack. As one retail trader put it: "Most are called away at this point and I'm thrilled. After it spent so much time between 2 and 3, I'm more than happy to take all the wins."

Yet, a rigorous look at the performance data reveals a darker mathematical reality for the option-selling crowd. In highly volatile, catalyst-driven secular bull runs, covered calls act as a wealth-limiting cage, decimating share counts and capping the very upside that draw investors to high-beta stocks in the first place. When a stock experiences a vertical breakout, the premium collected is a rounding error compared to the value of the shares forfeited.

Consider a quantitative model comparing two investors, Trader A and Trader B, starting on April 1, 2024. On this date, AST SpaceMobile traded at a split-adjusted $2.00 per share, while Rocket Lab stood at $3.76. 

Trader A is a pure buy-and-hold investor. He purchases 10,000 shares of ASTS for a total outlay of $20,000. He also purchases 10,000 shares of RKLB for $37,600. Trader A does nothing but hold his positions through the volatility of the subsequent 28 months. As of mid-August 2026, with ASTS trading at $70.00 and RKLB closing at $80.65 on August 15, Trader A’s portfolio is worth a staggering $1,506,500 ($700,000 in ASTS and $806,500 in RKLB). His total net profit is $1,448,900—a return of 2,515%.

Trader B, by contrast, wants to monetize the volatility of his holdings. He also buys 10,000 shares of ASTS at $2.00 and 10,000 shares of RKLB at $3.76, committing the same initial capital. However, he commits to writing monthly covered calls that are 25% out-of-the-money, collecting an average premium of 8% of the stock price per month.

In April 2024, with ASTS at $2.00, Trader B writes May 2024 $2.50 calls, collecting a premium of $0.15 per share ($1,500 total). Then, the catalyst hits. On May 15, 2024, AST SpaceMobile announces a definitive commercial agreement with AT&T to build a space-based cellular broadband network. The next day, the stock surges 44% on massive volume of 36 million shares. By the May options expiration, ASTS is trading at $5.00. Trader B's shares are called away at the $2.50 strike price. He receives $25,000 from the share sale and keeps the $1,500 premium. His total capital is now $26,500.

But ASTS is still running. Determined to continue the strategy, Trader B buys back in at the market price of $5.00. Crucially, his capital can now only purchase 5,300 shares. His ownership in the company has been slashed by nearly 47% in a single month. In June 2024, he writes July $6.25 calls for a premium of $0.35 per share, yielding $1,855 in total premiums. By late July, fueled by progress on its commercial partnerships, the stock climbs to $12.00. Again, he is assigned. His 5,300 shares are sold at $6.25, yielding $33,125, plus the $1,855 in premiums, totaling $34,980.

To get back into the trade, he must buy shares at $12.00. He can now only afford 2,915 shares. When ASTS surges to its late-August 2024 peak of $33.40, his upside is capped once more. By the time ASTS reaches its all-time high of $133.86 on May 26, 2026, and eventually settles to $70.00 in August 2026, Trader B's share count has been completely decimated. Even if he collected thousands of dollars in cumulative premiums, the compounding effect of buying back in at higher prices means his ASTS position is worth less than $90,000. He left over $610,000 of profit on the table.

A similar erosion occurs with Rocket Lab. In April 2024, Trader B sells May $4.70 calls on RKLB for a premium of $0.25 per share. In June 2024, RKLB rises to $4.80, and by September, it surges to $9.73. Each time the stock breaks out, Trader B is forced to sell at his strike, only to buy back in at higher levels with a depleted share count. When Rocket Lab reaches its all-time high of $151.00 on May 26, 2026, and settles at $80.65 in mid-August 2026, Trader B’s RKLB holdings are worth a mere fraction of Trader A’s $806,500 position.

This math explains the palpable frustration on retail forums. One Reddit user calculated the opportunity cost: "I shouldn't calculate this, but I did; if I had not sold these covered calls, I would be up an additional 60-91k depending on when I sold my shares." Another trader compared the emotional toll of having shares called away to a physical theft: "Feels like someone is driving your car away." For those who tried to manage the position when the stock neared their strikes, the anxiety was constant: "My $90 covered calls expiring today are saved. It was looking pretty shaky earlier this week and I had mentally accepted my shares would get taken away."

The systemic flaw of the covered call strategy in growth sectors is its structural asymmetry: you assume 100% of the downside risk but cap 100% of the upside. If ASTS or RKLB had crashed to zero, Trader B would have suffered a near-total loss, mitigated only by his minor premium income. But when the companies succeeded operationally, he was locked out of the compounding gains.

And the operational progress has been real. On August 5, 2026, AST SpaceMobile successfully launched its BlueBird 11, 12, and 13 satellites aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket, bringing its operational fleet to 13 spacecraft in orbit. Despite reporting a net loss of $0.44 per share in its Q2 2026 earnings on August 10, the company’s capital expenditure is building out a physical moat. Meanwhile, Rocket Lab reported record Q2 2026 revenue of $234.07 million, beating estimates of $231.62 million, alongside a historic $2.36 billion backlog—up 137% year-over-year—and a major deal to acquire Iridium Communications Inc. 

For retail investors who suffered through years of dilution and skepticism, these operational milestones represent the transition from speculative concepts to space infrastructure giants. But for those who sought to skim yield along the way, the lessons are clear. In the space race, trying to collect pennies in front of a Falcon 9 rocket is a guaranteed way to get left on the launchpad.
