# The 70-Percent Deadlock: Inside the Stalled Gaza "Phase II" Accord and Netanyahu’s Pre-Election Security Corridor

One year after the comprehensive ceasefire agreement took effect on October 10, 2025, the four-lane paved strip of asphalt slicing across the midsection of the Gaza Strip has hardened from a temporary military staging post into a fortified permanent border. Lined with concrete berms, automated surveillance towers, and armored checkpoints, the Netzarim Corridor stands as physical proof that while the active war has theoretically paused, the conflict has calcified into an armed, territorial partition.

When the 20-point peace framework sponsored by President Donald Trump’s administration was enacted in the autumn of 2025, it was heralded by international mediators as a definitive roadmap out of catastrophe. Phase I was largely executed according to schedule: through grueling winter negotiations, the exchange of all living Israeli hostages and deceased remains in exchange for Palestinian prisoners was finalized by early 2026. On January 14, 2026, U.S. Special Envoy Steve Witkoff formally declared the transition to "Phase II"—a phase structured around the demilitarization of militant groups, the complete withdrawal of the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) from urban zones, the handover of civil administration to a technocratic Palestinian committee, and the deployment of a 20,000-strong International Stabilization Force (ISF).

Instead, nine months after Witkoff’s announcement, Phase II has ground to a complete structural deadlock. Far from withdrawing, the IDF retains operational control over roughly 70 percent of the Gaza Strip through an expansive network of fortified zones: the Netzarim Corridor bisecting the north and south, the Philadelphi Corridor along the Egyptian boundary, an expanded perimeter buffer along the eastern border, and the military boundary line designated as the "Yellow Line." Within the remaining 30 percent—primarily overcrowded pockets in al-Mawasi, Deir al-Balah, and fragments of Khan Younis—over two million displaced Palestinians remain trapped in suspended ruin. United Nations assessments indicate that over 80 percent of Gaza's municipal infrastructure remains pulverized, more than 70 percent of sewage pumping stations are inoperable, and 70 percent of agricultural land remains cordoned off by military access restrictions.

Although large-scale aerial bombardments have ceased, the ceasefire exists in name only. Over the past twelve months, low-intensity retaliatory airstrikes, artillery duels, and perimeter engagements have killed over 1,400 Palestinians, according to United Nations and humanitarian reporting. The ceasefire framework has degenerated into a classic war of attrition governed by reciprocal vetoes: Israel insists that full, verifiable disarmament by Hamas is an immutable prerequisite for troop withdrawals, while Hamas leadership refuses to surrender a single weapon while Israeli armor remains stationed inside the strip and airstrikes persist.

This strategic paralysis has marooned the international institutions created under UN Security Council Resolution 2803. The Board of Peace (BoP)—chaired by President Trump, advised by Jared Kushner, and managed day-to-day by High Representative Nickolay Mladenov—was designed to oversee Gaza’s reconstruction and supervise the National Committee for the Administration of the Gaza Strip (NCAG). The NCAG, composed of 15 Palestinian technocrats led by former Palestinian Authority official and civil engineer Ali Shaath, was tasked with running municipal services, hospitals, and reconstruction. Yet nine months after its appointment, Shaath and his 15-member committee remain confined to Cairo hotels, barred by Israeli security vetoes from establishing a presence inside Gaza.

Simultaneously, the deployment of the International Stabilization Force has completely stalled. While diplomatic pledges were tentatively offered by nations such as Morocco, Indonesia, Albania, Kosovo, and Kazakhstan, not a single international battalion has entered Gaza. Regional Arab states have refused to supply personnel for what defense analysts describe as an open-ended counterinsurgency mission that lacks a defined exit strategy or a binding pathway toward a sovereign Palestinian state. Even a modest, pilot-phase deployment of 200 ISF troops to a humanitarian corridor in Rafah—tentatively agreed to by Israeli authorities in July 2026—remains frozen, caught in bureaucratic deadlock and disputed rules of engagement.

In Jerusalem, the deadlock in Gaza is inseparable from domestic electoral arithmetic. On October 27, 2026, Israelis will head to the polls for the 26th Knesset election, marking the first time since 1988 that an Israeli parliament has completed its full statutory four-year term without an early dissolution. For Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, the election is a high-stakes referendum on his leadership, wartime legacy, and survival against a united opposition. Recent polling consistently shows Netanyahu’s Likud-led coalition falling short of a 61-seat majority, trailing an opposition bloc spearheaded by former IDF Chief of Staff Gadi Eisenkot’s "Yashar" party and the centrist alliance of Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid under the "Beyachad" banner.

Netanyahu’s electoral viability rests entirely on preserving his alliance with ultranationalist coalition partners: National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich. Both ministers have repeatedly threatened to bring down the government and dissolve the Knesset if the IDF yields control over the Philadelphi or Netzarim corridors, or if the Cairo-based NCAG is granted entry. By maintaining military dominance over 70 percent of Gaza, Netanyahu has preserved his parliamentary majority while presenting himself to conservative voters as the sole leader capable of resisting foreign pressure.

This posture has triggered fierce accusations of political weaponization from the opposition. Eisenkot has repeatedly assailed Netanyahu’s security claims, arguing that Israel’s "strategic position will not rise and fall over the Philadelphi Corridor" and alleging that corridor control is being used as a cynical pretext to derail Phase II and safeguard a brittle coalition. On platforms like X and within political forums on Reddit, discussions in communities such as r/worldnews and r/neoliberal have reflected this deepening skepticism. Commentators frequently characterize the corridor infrastructure as a "political shield disguised as a security buffer," with analysts noting that Netanyahu has managed to freeze the conflict in place until the ballots are cast.

The long-term geopolitical costs of this domestic calculation were laid bare at the United Nations General Assembly. UN Secretary-General António Guterres issued a sharp warning against what he characterized as the systematic partition and "creeping annexation" of Gaza. Guterres warned that the permanent militarization of corridors and the severance of Palestinian territory deals an irreversible "deathblow" to the geographical contiguity required for a viable two-state solution.

As the October 27 election approaches, the October 2025 ceasefire agreement stands suspended between rhetoric and geography. Phase I succeeded in bringing hostages home, but Phase II has become an instrument of political entrenchment. With 1,400 dead in a year of supposed peace and 70 percent of Gaza carved into military corridors, the architecture of stabilization has produced an indefinite, fortified occupation.
