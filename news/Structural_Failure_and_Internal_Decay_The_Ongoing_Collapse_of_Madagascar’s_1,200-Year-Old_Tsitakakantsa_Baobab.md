# Structural Failure and Internal Decay: The Ongoing Collapse of Madagascar’s 1,200-Year-Old Tsitakakantsa Baobab

In southwest Madagascar, the Tsitakakantsa baobab—officially recognized as the largest baobab tree on the planet—has entered an irreversible biological and structural decline following the impact of Tropical Storm Jude in early 2025. Located near the remote village of Andombiry in the Morombe district, this specimen of *Adansonia grandidieri* measures 28.98 meters (95 feet) in circumference and stands 25 meters tall. Its scale is reflected in its local name, which translates to: "if you sing on one side of the trunk, the song cannot be heard on the other." 

As of mid-2026, the tree is in the terminal stages of its lifecycle. This collapse has severely impacted the local Andombiry community, who revere the tree as a sacred deity, while prompting the international botanical community to track a documented trend of structural failures among Africa’s millennial "keystone" trees.

The catalyst for this decline was Tropical Storm Jude, a weather system that struck the Atsimo-Andrefana region in early 2025. According to on-the-ground assessments by baobab specialist Cyrille Cornu, extreme rainfall and winds from the storm caused profound physical trauma to the tree. The storm filled a massive cavity at the summit of the baobab with an exceptional volume of water. Because baobabs function structurally like dense, fibrous sponges, this localized water reservoir initiated a massive rotting process within the heartwood.

By October 2025, researchers and local observers documented a foul-smelling, highly viscous black liquid leaking from fissures at the base of the trunk. Aerial drone footage revealed a necrotic hole at the crown. The decisive structural shift occurred in late 2025 when the tree shed a primary branch. In ancient baobabs, the loss of a major limb is the structural signature of imminent fragmentation. Compromised by internal rot and the failure of its fibrous core, the tree is collapsing under its own gravity. Field biologists project the tree will completely fragment within the next two to three years.

For the people of Andombiry, this biological event presents a localized spiritual crisis. In regional cosmology, Tsitakakantsa operates as a living ancestor, a "guardian of the horizon," and a focal point for worship. The community regularly performs rituals at its base, offering coins, poultry, and zebu sacrifices to petition for health and rainfall. The tree assumed this primary spiritual role only after the previous regional giant, Tsitakakoike, underwent a similar collapse in 2019 following Cyclone Ava. Within a decade, the community is managing the loss of a second cultural anchor, prompting internal shifts regarding local spiritual succession.

The collapse of these millennial trees has driven intense discourse regarding the culpability of shifting climate patterns. When aerial photographs of the compromised trunk circulated in early 2026, public debate polarized rapidly. As one X user pointed out during the viral spread, "We are watching centuries of history dissolve into mulch while debating if it's natural or climate collapse. It's both." On the Reddit forum r/collapse, users focused on recruitment rates: "The real tragedy isn't just an old tree dying; it's the lack of new saplings surviving the current droughts to replace it." 

However, scientific analysis points to a highly specific mechanism of death. Researchers from the African Baobab Alliance note that these trees are "icons of endurance." The death of Tsitakakantsa is fundamentally a mechanical and biological failure induced by old age and exacerbated by anomalous storm conditions, rather than a direct consequence of drought-induced climate change alone. 

Data indicates the most pressing threat to *Adansonia grandidieri* in Madagascar is habitat destruction and a lack of new sapling recruitment. Madagascar has lost an estimated 44% of its natural forest cover since the 1950s due to agricultural practices. The broader ecological metric is not solely the death of a 1,200-year-old specimen, but the reality that the surrounding degraded landscape currently lacks the necessary conditions for a succeeding generation of baobabs to establish root systems and reach maturity.
