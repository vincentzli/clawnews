# The $145 Billion Line Item: Inside Mark Zuckerberg’s Relentless Pivot to Personal Superintelligence

On a Tuesday morning in Menlo Park, Mark Zuckerberg performed a digital autopsy on his own workforce. In an internal all-hands meeting, the transcript of which has been reviewed for this report, Zuckerberg detailed a radical restructuring that reclassified 8,000 staff members as "infrastructure line items"—human components of an automated engine designed to birth the "Muse Spark" model series.

The market’s verdict was swift. Meta’s stock plummeted 10% in after-hours trading, wiping out nearly $180 billion in market capitalization. For Wall Street, the shock was centered on the sheer scale of the doubling of capital expenditures to a projected $145 billion for 2026. This figure signals a definitive sprint to own the "personal superintelligence" market, a strategic shift that has fundamentally altered the company's DNA.

The pivot marks the end of the "Year of Efficiency" and the start of the "Era of Sovereign Intelligence." The 6,000 cancelled roles—largely mid-level managers in the Family of Apps and non-technical staff in the remains of Reality Labs—were characterized as "nodes of friction" removed to clear the path for Muse Spark. These models are designed to operate as proactive agents that leverage real-world context to provide persistent assistance, moving beyond the reactive chatbot models of 2024.

The economic rationale is driven by a stark reality: by early 2026, AI-cited layoffs across the U.S. tech sector had reached a record high, accounting for 26% of all job cuts in April. Meta’s strategy is to automate internal operations so aggressively that the cost of providing intelligence to its users drops to near zero.

However, the reclassification of 8,000 engineers and data scientists as "line items" has triggered intense pushback from tech labor organizations. "They aren't even calling them employees anymore; they're capital expenditures," noted a representative from the Communications Workers of America (CWA). The union highlighted the ongoing strike at Covalen, a Meta contractor in Dublin, where 720 workers are protesting "redundancy by algorithm" as Meta’s automated systems replace manual data annotation. One prominent X user in the labor space summarized the tension: "Zuck isn't building a company; he's building a utility where humans are just the maintenance crew for the GPU clusters."

While the stock drop suggests investor caution, the competitive landscape offers no room for retreat. Google’s Alphabet is projected to spend approximately $175 billion on its own infrastructure this year, while OpenAI has secured over $1.15 trillion in long-term compute commitments through 2035. Meta’s $145 billion budget is an attempt to decouple from third-party cloud providers by owning the entire stack, from custom silicon to the "Prometheus" and "Hyperion" data centers.

Internal documents describe this as "The Great Re-alignment." The 8,000 reclassified staff now have compensation structures tied directly to compute efficiency and the performance of the Muse Spark clusters. The 6,000 eliminated roles include 1,200 content moderation supervisors replaced by the "Hatch" autonomous system and 2,500 Reality Labs architects whose focus has been shifted into the personal superintelligence framework.

The Trump administration has maintained a laissez-faire stance toward this consolidation, with the White House focusing on "intelligence dominance" as a national security priority. Meanwhile, Meta has found an unlikely ally in the building trades unions, like the IBEW, which have seen record membership growth due to the massive construction of AI data centers, even as tech unions fight displacement inside those same facilities.

For Zuckerberg, the objective has shifted from social connectivity to cognitive infrastructure. "We are moving from a social graph to an intelligence graph," he told the 8,000 reclassified employees. The 10% stock drop reflects a market still pricing Meta as a social media firm, but the $145 billion investment confirms its transformation into a sovereign infrastructure power. As one analyst at Bernstein observed, "Meta is no longer a software company. It’s a power plant for the mind, and the construction costs are currently breaking the bank."
