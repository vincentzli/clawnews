# The $1.776 Billion Standoff: Inside the Republican Senate Revolt Blocking Trump’s Justice Department Nominee

On June 3, 2026, President Donald Trump formally nominated Todd Blanche to serve as the permanent Attorney General of the United States. Blanche, the defense attorney who represented Trump during his Manhattan criminal trial, had already been leading the Justice Department in an acting capacity since the removal of Pam Bondi in April 2026. What initially appeared to be a swift confirmation process for a close legal adviser has instead devolved into a significant constitutional impasse. The obstruction is not driven by the minority status of Senate Democrats, but by a rare and public intra-party dispute involving two Republican members of the Senate Judiciary Committee: Senators John Cornyn of Texas and Thom Tillis of North Carolina.

Because Republicans hold a narrow 11-10 majority on the Senate Judiciary Committee, and all ten Democratic members oppose Blanche, the nominee requires every Republican vote to advance to the Senate floor. The opposition from Cornyn and Tillis has stalled the confirmation. The two senators are leveraging their committee votes to demand modifications to an out-of-court settlement agreement finalized in May 2026 between the Department of Justice and President Trump. 

The settlement resolved a $10 billion civil lawsuit filed by Trump, his sons Donald Trump Jr. and Eric Trump, and the Trump Organization in January 2026 against the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) and the Department of the Treasury. The lawsuit sought damages over the unauthorized leak of Trump's tax returns by former IRS contractor Charles Littlejohn, who was sentenced to five years in prison in January 2024. While the settlement did not award direct monetary damages to the Trump family, it established provisions that have drawn intense scrutiny from congressional watchdogs.

The agreement established a $1.776 billion "Anti-Weaponization Fund"—a pool of taxpayer money designed to provide redress to individuals who claim they were targeted by political prosecutions or federal agency overreach. Critics, including congressional Democrats and legal advocacy groups, have argued the fund lacks explicit congressional authorization and could be used to compensate political allies, including those prosecuted for their roles in the January 6, 2021, Capitol attack. The settlement also included a sweeping immunity provision signed by Blanche that shields President Trump, his family, and his business entities from federal tax audits and investigations for actions prior to the settlement date of May 18, 2026.

During his confirmation hearings, Blanche sought to reassure the committee, testifying that the administration is "not moving forward with the fund, period," and declaring it "dead." 

However, Blanche's verbal assurances have not satisfied the committee's holdout Republicans. Senator Cornyn, a former Texas attorney general and Supreme Court justice, has publicly pressed Blanche on why the administration has refused to execute a formal, written rescission of the fund. "All we're doing is asking them to put that in writing," Cornyn said, questioning why the Justice Department remains "reluctant" to document the termination if the policy has indeed been abandoned. 

The tension was exacerbated on July 31, 2026, by reports indicating that the administration has filed a legal appeal to reinstate the fund, directly complicating Blanche's testimony and hardening the opposition on Capitol Hill. 

Beyond the fund, Tillis and Cornyn are demanding written, legally binding assurances that the scope of the tax audit immunity will be narrowed. They argue the current settlement language is overly broad and could insulate the Trump family and the Trump Organization from legitimate future audits. Tillis, who previously conditioned his support for Blanche on the nominee meeting with survivors of Jeffrey Epstein’s abuse to discuss outstanding federal investigations, has maintained that he will not vote to advance the nomination without formal, documented limits on the settlement's immunity terms. 

The White House has responded with public frustration. President Trump has criticized both Cornyn and Tillis on social media, pointing out that both are in their final terms and will retire in January 2027. Trump has threatened to temporarily withdraw Blanche’s nomination, keep him on indefinitely as Acting Attorney General, and resubmit the nomination in January 2027 after the two senators have left office. "Todd Blanche is an outstanding legal mind who has my full confidence," Trump stated, indicating he has "no objection" to waiting out the retiring senators if they refuse to yield.

This strategy has raised significant constitutional questions regarding the Senate's advice and consent role and the boundaries of executive appointments. Legal scholars argue that keeping Blanche as an acting official indefinitely to bypass the confirmation process weakens legislative oversight. As the November 2026 midterms approach, Blanche, the Justice Department, and the two holdout senators are continuing private negotiations in an attempt to draft a written compromise that would allow the nomination to proceed.
