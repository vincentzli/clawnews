# The Caracas Realignment: How Washington Captured Maduro, Normalized His Deputy, and Carved Up the Orinoco

On the evening of September 22, 2026, inside the gilded interior of the Lotte New York Palace hotel in Midtown Manhattan, Venezuelan Acting President Delcy Rodríguez joined President Donald Trump for a closed-door meeting on the sidelines of a reception hosted for world leaders during the 81st United Nations General Assembly. Flanked by Secretary of State Marco Rubio, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, and White House Chief of Staff Susie Wiles, Rodríguez smiled as cameras documented a high-level engagement that would have been unthinkable a year prior. Just five miles south across the East River, behind the perimeter of the Metropolitan Detention Center in Brooklyn, her former superior, Nicolás Maduro, sat in a federal cell awaiting trial in the Southern District of New York on narco-terrorism and cocaine trafficking conspiracy charges.

The contrast highlighted an unprecedented geopolitical pivot. Eight months after the military operation that unseated Maduro, his former vice president has transitioned from a stalwart defender of the Bolivarian revolution to Washington's central interlocutor in Caracas. In New York, Rodríguez publicly thanked the Trump administration for reopening diplomatic channels, cementing an arrangement that prioritizes energy production, international debt restructuring, and regional stability over the democratic transfer of power demanded by Venezuela’s political opposition.

The strategic chain of events began on September 1, 2025, when the Pentagon launched Operation Southern Spear. Led by U.S. Southern Command, the campaign concentrated naval strike forces and aerial surveillance assets throughout the Caribbean basin, establishing an aggressive maritime cordon around Venezuelan oil transit routes following the designation of the *Cartel de los Soles* as a Foreign Terrorist Organization. That pressure campaign culminated at 2:00 a.m. local time on January 3, 2026, when U.S. Special Operations Forces commenced Operation Absolute Resolve. Precision airstrikes and electronic countermeasures involving more than 150 aircraft dismantled Venezuelan radar and air defense infrastructure, while assault teams from Delta Force and the 160th Special Operations Aviation Regiment executed an airborne raid on Maduro’s Caracas residence, taking him and his wife, Cilia Flores, into custody.

The strike set off an intense constitutional battle on Capitol Hill. The Trump administration defended the assault as an extraterritorial law enforcement operation executed under Article II executive authorities to apprehend federal fugitives, circumventing the notification mandates of the War Powers Resolution of 1973. On January 8, the Senate voted 52 to 47 to assert war powers oversight, led by Senator Tim Kaine, who argued that deploying military forces to topple a foreign leader without congressional authorization marked a perilous expansion of the imperial presidency. Justice Department legal memoranda countered that the narrow operational duration did not meet the constitutional threshold of sustained hostilities. Meanwhile, in lower Manhattan, U.S. District Judge Alvin K. Hellerstein scheduled Maduro’s federal trial for June 1, 2027, with the court setting an October 8, 2026 hearing to weigh pretrial release arguments for Flores while defense attorneys prepare formal challenges based on sovereign immunity.

Rather than installing the opposition coalition led by Nobel Peace Prize laureate María Corina Machado and Edmundo González Urrutia, Washington chose to work with the institutional machinery left behind. Following Maduro’s capture, Venezuela’s Supreme Court designated Delcy Rodríguez as interim president, while Machado remained barred from returning to the country despite seven verified border crossing attempts. Washington moved swiftly to stabilize the relationship: on April 1, 2026, the U.S. Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) formally removed Rodríguez from its Specially Designated Nationals list, rescinding sanctions imposed on her in 2018. Reporting by *The New York Times* revealed that Secretary of State Marco Rubio assumed a guiding role in bilateral governance, maintaining continuous contact with Rodríguez via encrypted messaging platforms to review cabinet appointments, energy directives, and institutional transition benchmarks.

The economic core of the bilateral thaw is the re-allocation of the Orinoco Belt’s heavy crude reserves. Following an August 28 announcement by President Trump outlining a comprehensive program to rehabilitate Venezuela’s energy infrastructure, U.S. oil majors moved aggressively. On September 2, Chevron announced a $7 billion, five-year capital expansion plan, securing amended commercial and fiscal terms for its *Petroindependencia* joint venture that incorporate the Carabobo-1 and Carabobo-2-South-A tracts, with a target of more than doubling production to 600,000 barrels per day. Weeks later, Continental Resources entered into an MOU with state oil company PDVSA to develop the 126,000-acre Ayacucho 2 block—an asset estimated to hold 30 billion barrels of extra-heavy oil—under a structure assigning the driller a 100% working interest. Simultaneously, the Department of Defense’s Office of Strategic Capital facilitated an equity transaction involving North American Blue Energy Partners, granting the venture concessions across 17 fields containing approximately 65 billion barrels of oil reserves.

The developments triggered significant activity across global equity markets and retail trading platforms like r/wallstreetbets, where participants highlighted the "Venezuela trade" and framed Chevron’s historical assets as an unrivaled competitive moat against competing multinationals. Across Latin America, however, the U.S. operation deepened regional divisions. Brazilian President Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva characterized the unilateral capture of a sitting president as an "extremely dangerous precedent" and an "affront to Venezuela’s sovereignty," while Colombian President Gustavo Petro condemned the action as foreign aggression and deployed 30,000 troops along the Colombian-Venezuelan border. In contrast, Argentine President Javier Milei praised the intervention, declaring that "freedom advances." In Havana, the warming relationship caused severe diplomatic friction: Cuban Minister of Economy and Planning Joaquín Alonso Vázquez publicly labeled Rodríguez a "traitor" in a social media post that was subsequently deleted, amid an immediate reduction in Venezuelan crude shipments to Cuba.

Within Venezuela, the pragmatic entente between Washington and the interim administration has fueled widespread popular disillusionment. Addressing the UN General Assembly on September 23, Rodríguez pledged that presidential balloting would eventually take place, though she refused to commit to a date, asserting that Venezuelans would select the "right moment." Transition insiders confirm that planned restructurings of the National Electoral Council and the Supreme Court make an election improbable before late 2027. According to a nationwide survey by Meganálisis conducted between September 15 and 19 involving 1,627 adult respondents, 91% of Venezuelans disapproved of the Trump-Rodríguez New York meeting, 94% reported feeling unrepresented by her delegation at the UN, and approximately 80% rejected the recent oil agreements signed with American firms. In hypothetical electoral matchups, Machado holds 61% voter preference, compared to roughly 3% for Rodríguez. While Washington has secured access to the world’s largest oil deposits and neutralized an adversarial regime, millions of Venezuelans find their democratic aspirations once again subordinated to international realpolitik.
