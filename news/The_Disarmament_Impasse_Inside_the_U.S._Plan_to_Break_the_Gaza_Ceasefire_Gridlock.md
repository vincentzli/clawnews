# The Disarmament Impasse: Inside the U.S. Plan to Break the Gaza Ceasefire Gridlock

JERUSALEM — On the afternoon of August 17, 2026, U.S. special envoy Jared Kushner held a four-hour meeting with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu in Jerusalem to address the diplomatic gridlock surrounding the implementation of the Gaza ceasefire. Kushner was accompanied by former British Prime Minister Tony Blair and diplomat Nickolay Mladenov, both representing the U.S.-led Board of Peace. 

The Jerusalem meeting followed a session in Egypt on August 16, 2026, where Kushner met with Hamas political chief Khalil al-Hayya. During the Egypt talks, al-Hayya expressed a conditional willingness to demilitarize Gaza if Israel halted its military campaign and withdrew its forces. However, during the subsequent August 17 session, Netanyahu maintained that Israel would not withdraw its forces or permit reconstruction until Hamas was fully and verifiably disarmed. Netanyahu also reaffirmed his opposition to the establishment of a Palestinian state.

This sequencing dispute remains the primary obstacle to executing the 15-point peace roadmap signed on October 9, 2025, which went into effect on October 10, 2025. The impasse is centered on the "Yellow Line," a demarcation boundary established under the 2025 agreement to separate areas under Israeli military control from Palestinian-administered zones. Originally, the line placed 53% of the Gaza Strip under Israeli control. Since then, the Israeli military has moved the boundary markers westward, expanding its control to approximately 64% of the territory as of August 2026. 

This shifting boundary has resulted in regular military skirmishes along the Yellow Line. Israel states that its operations in these sectors are required to destroy tunnel networks and maintain security, while international observers point to the displacement of the population crowded west of the line. The concentration of displaced persons has also exacerbated public health and sanitation issues in the remaining Palestinian-administered areas.

During the talks, the U.S. delegation shifted its stance to prioritize the immediate disarmament of Hamas. Kushner proposed that Major General Jasper Jeffers III, who was appointed by President Donald Trump on January 16, 2026, to command the International Stabilization Force (ISF), oversee the disarmament process and the handover of weapons. Jeffers, who commanded U.S. Special Operations Command Central (SOCCENT) until August 2026 and previously monitored the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire, is designated to lead a stabilization force of up to 20,000 personnel contributed by nations including Albania, Indonesia, Kazakhstan, Kosovo, and Morocco. 

To facilitate the proposal, the U.S. delegation and the Israeli government agreed to establish two working groups: one dedicated to the disarmament and demilitarization of Gaza, and another to address sanitation, clean water, and public health. Kushner urged Israel to accept "small steps" to test Hamas's compliance, advising Netanyahu that if the group failed to disarm during a trial phase, Israel would possess stronger international backing to resume military operations to finish the job.

Netanyahu faces significant domestic pressure as Israel prepares for legislative elections for the 26th Knesset on October 27, 2026. The election is historically notable as the 25th Knesset is the first legislature since 1988 to complete its full four-year term without early dissolution. Netanyahu’s Likud party is currently locked in a close race, facing competition from the opposition Yashar party led by Gadi Eisenkot and various right-wing factions. Netanyahu has campaigned heavily against smaller conservative parties, warning that splitting the right-wing vote could lead to an opposition victory. Maintaining a firm stance on Gaza's disarmament is seen as central to his campaign strategy.

Simultaneously, the Board of Peace (BoP) faces intense public scrutiny. Established in January 2026 and authorized by UN Security Council Resolution 2803, the BoP was chartered to oversee Gaza’s reconstruction and governance. However, the organization's financial structure has drawn criticism. Under the charter, which designates Donald Trump as its long-term chairman, member states are required to contribute $1 billion to secure a permanent seat on the executive board. 

This funding model has led to criticism on platforms such as Reddit and X, where users have characterized the board as a "grift" or a "slush fund." These concerns grew after U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio announced the transition of the BoP from a public international body into an international non-governmental organization (INGO), a designation that limits public auditing. In congressional hearings, Rep. Jamie Raskin questioned the organization's transparency, highlighting the use of the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office to register trademarks for the entity while promised reconstruction funds remained unfulfilled.

On forums like r/moderatepolitics and r/Economics, users expressed skepticism regarding the proposed oversight mechanisms. Critics pointed out the contradiction of attempting a voluntary disarmament process while the physical boundaries of the Yellow Line continue to shift westward. Others noted that the transition of the Board of Peace to an INGO reduces diplomatic accountability. With the October elections approaching in Israel and the humanitarian situation along the Yellow Line unresolved, the proposed working groups and peacekeeping deployments face significant operational challenges.
