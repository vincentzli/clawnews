On August 1, 2026, the boundary between generative pattern matching and formal, machine-checkable reasoning collapsed. OpenAI quietly announced that an internal version of its next-generation reasoning model, **Astra**, had generated verified solutions to ten of the most intractable open problems in mathematics and theoretical computer science. 

Unlike the speculative AI research papers of the past, this was not an exercise in peer-reviewed prose. Instead, OpenAI published a 249-page collection of manuscripts alongside complete, machine-checkable **Lean 4** certificates on GitHub. By compiling these proofs directly against Lean’s standard mathematical library (`mathlib`), OpenAI bypassed the biggest roadblock to AI-generated science: the hallucination problem. The math is either correct or the compiler throws a type checking error. There is no middle ground.

The results are monumental. Astra successfully resolved the existence of a **non-sofic group**—a group theory enigma open since Mikhail Gromov introduced the concept in 1999—and constructed a counterexample disproving **Connes's rigidity conjecture** on von Neumann algebras (open since 1980). This is the story of how AI-driven formal verification entered the frontier, the raw compute economics of mathematical discovery, and the brewing civil war between the Silicon Valley labs and the global mathematical establishment.

### The Mathematics: Inside the Ten Astra Proofs

To appreciate the scale of Astra's achievement, one must look at the specific mathematical structures generated by the model. The ten problems resolved span group theory, high-dimensional geometry, quantum complexity, and cryptography.

#### 1. The Explicit Construction of a Non-Sofic Group
Since 1999, group theorists have wondered whether every countable discrete group is "sofic"—meaning its multiplication table can be asymptotically approximated by finite permutation groups. Astra settled the question by constructing an explicit counterexample: **the elementary subgroup of $9 \times 9$ matrices over the binary Leavitt algebra $L_{\mathbb{F}_2}(1,2)$**.

To prove non-soficity, Astra’s proof architecture synthesized **property-(T) expanders** and **Thompson’s Group $V$**. It demonstrated that any attempt to approximate the group's local multiplication rules in finite permutation "laboratories" must fail to satisfy the group's axioms within a specified Hamming distance. By utilizing median normalization and component alignment, Astra proved that the local relations are so rigid that any finite imitation must eventually collapse, establishing that not all infinite structures can be approximated by finite ones.

#### 2. Disproving Connes’s Rigidity Conjecture
Posed by Alain Connes in 1980, the rigidity conjecture proposed that for certain highly rigid groups—specifically infinite conjugacy class (ICC) property (T) groups—the group von Neumann algebra $L(\Gamma)$ acts as a unique "fingerprint" that completely determines the group $\Gamma$. 

Astra disproved this by constructing infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic property (T) groups that generate isomorphic group von Neumann algebras. This proves that two distinct algebraic structures can cast the identical "analytic shadow," resolving a 46-year-old question in operator algebras.

#### 3. Sphere Packing and Coding Theory Bounds
Astra established a tighter upper bound on sphere-packing density in high dimensions, representing the first major analytical improvement to this bound since 1978, pushing it down toward the Cohn–Elkies threshold. In tandem, the model derived exponentially improved bounds on the maximum size of binary codes at a prescribed minimum distance, alongside matching results for high-dimensional spherical codes.

#### 4. Arithmetic Circuit Complexity and Cryptography
In theoretical computer science, Astra derived new lower bounds for computing the permanent using arithmetic circuits, proving an arithmetic-formula lower bound of order $n^4 / \log n$. It also proved an exponential parallel repetition theorem for general two-player quantum games. For lattice cryptography, Astra established polynomial-factor hardness of approximation for the Closest Vector Problem (CVP), a foundational result for the security parameters of post-quantum cryptographic schemes. Finally, the model resolved three specific open questions from Paul Erdős’s list (problems 146, 180, and 183).

### The Lean 4 Verification Workflow

Historically, the mathematical community has been highly skeptical of AI-generated proofs, which are often riddled with subtle logical leaps or outright hallucinations. OpenAI bypassed this by requiring Astra to output its reasoning directly in **Lean 4**, a functional programming language and proof assistant.

The workflow is highly structured:
1. **CoT Blueprint**: The model generates a natural-language chain-of-thought blueprint of the proof.
2. **Formalization**: The model attempts to translate the step-by-step mathematical assertions into Lean 4 code.
3. **Interactive Debugging Loop**: The model interacts with the Lean compiler. If the compiler rejects a tactic (e.g., failing to close a goal or indicating a type mismatch), the model reads the compiler error and regenerates the code.
4. **Human-in-the-Loop Refinement**: Human mathematicians step in to help define custom lemmas or import complex mathematical definitions from `mathlib` when the model gets stuck.

Lean 4 serves as a "trustless verification layer." A mathematical proof is no longer an essay to be peer-reviewed by exhausted humans over six months; it is a software package. If the Lean compiler outputs no errors and accepts the code without the `sorry` identifier (which marks unproved assumptions), the proof is mathematically sound.

### Compute Cost Analysis: The $2,000 Breakthrough

OpenAI revealed that the total compute cost to generate these ten solutions was approximately **$2,000** at GPT-5.6 Sol API rates. 

To put this in perspective, let’s look at the pricing for the GPT-5.6 Sol model (released in early 2026):
* **Input Tokens**: $5.00 per 1 million tokens
* **Output Tokens**: $30.00 per 1 million tokens

If we assume a 3:1 ratio of input to output tokens during the search process, a $2,000 budget translates to roughly **178 million total tokens** (~133 million input tokens and ~44 million output tokens). This represents a massive agentic search. Astra was not merely predicting the next word; it was executing a massive tree-of-thought search, backtracking when Lean compilation failed, and running thousands of parallel logical trajectories. 

For $2,000—less than the monthly stipend of a single graduate student—OpenAI bought the resolution of ten decade-old mathematical mysteries.

### The Silicon Valley Replication War: Anthropic Strikes Back

The announcement of Astra's proofs immediately ignited a competitive firestorm. Within 24 hours, Levent Alpöge, a prominent mathematician and researcher at Anthropic, posted on X:

> "so after 24h i have half of them with fable i didn't see much discussion of prompting in the announcement but this is a similar setup as with my e.g. unit distance announcement: totally autonomous, generic prompt, no internet (+ paranoia to ensure no information leaked)."

Alpöge's claim that Anthropic's publicly available **Claude Fable 5** model could replicate 50% of Astra’s results overnight reframed the entire narrative. If an existing commercial model can replicate the proofs with a "generic prompt," is Astra truly a proprietary reasoning breakthrough, or is the breakthrough simply the human selection of AI-amenable problems?

This replication war follows Alpöge's landmark achievement in July 2026, where he used Claude Fable 5 to find a counterexample to the 87-year-old **Jacobian conjecture** for dimensions $n \ge 3$. While watching the FIFA World Cup final, Alpöge prompted Fable to construct a compact, 216-character polynomial map from $\mathbb{C}^3 \to \mathbb{C}^3$:
$$\left((1+xy)^3 z + y^2 (1+xy) (4+3xy),\ y + 3 x (1+xy)^2 z + 3 x y^2 (4+3xy),\ 2 x - 3 x^2 y - x^3 z\right)$$
This map yields a constant Jacobian determinant of $-2$, yet fails global invertibility (mapping three distinct inputs to the same output), disproving the conjecture for all dimensions three and up. 

The fact that Alpöge could extract such profound results from Fable suggests that the gap between OpenAI’s unreleased internal models and Anthropic’s shipping APIs is much narrower than OpenAI’s PR implies.

### Sociological Tensions and the Leiden Declaration

The rapid encroachment of AI into pure mathematics has caused profound anxiety within the academic community. On June 2, 2026, a group of international mathematicians released the **Leiden Declaration on Artificial Intelligence and Mathematics**, which was quickly endorsed by the International Mathematical Union (IMU).

The Leiden Declaration warns of several existential threats:
1. **Proprietary Control**: The risk that corporate AI labs will dictate the direction of mathematical research, focusing only on problems that show off their models' capabilities.
2. **Dilution of Peer Review**: The threat of human mathematicians being overwhelmed by a deluge of AI-generated papers.
3. **Loss of Autonomy**: The concern that mathematics will shift from an pursuit of human understanding to a software-engineering task of compiling Lean code.

Mathematicians are deeply divided. Fields Medalist Timothy Gowers, who has spent years working on these exact problems, expressed his unease regarding the Astra results: 
> "very strange and not particularly pleasant... [it is] unsettling to see an AI solve problems I have personally been working on."

In contrast, Terence Tao has taken a more adaptive stance, urging the community to view AI not as a replacement, but as a collaborative tool:
> "We must re-examine our values and working methods... and learn to use these tools to enhance human mathematical inquiry."

### The Verdict

We have entered the epoch of verified AI reasoning. By anchoring neural networks to formal proof compilers like Lean 4, the industry has solved the hallucination problem in the formal sciences. But as the replication war between Astra and Fable proves, the capability is already commoditized. The race is no longer just about building a model that can think—it is about deciding what we want it to think about.
