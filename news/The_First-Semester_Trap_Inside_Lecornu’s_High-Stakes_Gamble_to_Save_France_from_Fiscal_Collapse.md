# The First-Semester Trap: Inside Lecornu’s High-Stakes Gamble to Save France from Fiscal Collapse

PARIS — “Je ne sais pas dans quelle langue il faut que je le dise ! Je ne me mêle pas de la campagne présidentielle.” (I don’t know in what language I must say it! I am not getting involved in the presidential campaign.) With those words, published in an interview with *Le Parisien* on August 29, 2026, French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu attempted to decouple his minority government from the electoral storm of April 2027. By ruling out his own candidacy, Lecornu sought to isolate the budget process from presidential campaign politics, positioning his cabinet as a “gouvernement de mission” (government of mission) rather than a “gouvernement en campagne” (government in campaign). However, this administrative neutrality faces an immediate challenge from a severe fiscal crisis and a looming constitutional showdown that could destabilize the euro zone’s second-largest economy.

Faced with a highly fractured National Assembly and a deadline of October 6, 2026, to submit the 2027 budget bill, Lecornu is attempting a precarious legislative maneuver. He has pitched the upcoming bill as a “budget de premier semestre” (first-semester budget), structured with “mesures réversibles” (reversible measures). Political analysts and opposition lawmakers view this strategy as an acknowledgment that the centrist coalition appointed by President Emmanuel Macron in September 2025 lacks the parliamentary strength to pass a comprehensive, long-term fiscal plan without facing a successful censure motion. The goal of a reversible budget is to keep basic services running while allowing any legislative majority emerging from the spring 2027 elections to modify or rescind the measures.

The numbers underlying the crisis are unforgiving. France’s budget deficit for 2025 hovered at 5.1% of GDP, and projections for 2026 show no improvement, remaining stuck between 5.1% and 5.2%. Meanwhile, gross public debt is on track to hit 118% of GDP in 2026 and march toward 120% in 2027. In June 2026, Lecornu sent a strict “lettre de cadrage” (framing letter) to his ministers, demanding they pare back their spending demands after initial ministerial requests exceeded available limits by €30 billion. To close this gap, the 2027 budget will include contentious cuts to social spending, tighter rules on employee sick leave (“arrêts maladie”), and reduced state reimbursements for certain medications. Under the government's proposal, state expenditures will grow slower than inflation, while local government spending is projected to track inflation closely.

Sovereign debt markets have responded to this gridlock with mounting alarm. The spread between 10-year French OAT (Obligation Assimilable du Trésor) bonds and German Bunds—a key barometer of risk—has widened to between 85 and 90 basis points, a level reflecting a severe political uncertainty premium. For the first time in recent history, France’s borrowing costs are converging with those of Italy, prompting warnings from rating agencies and Brussels, which has placed Paris under an excessive deficit procedure. 

The Prime Minister's current predicament is the latest chapter in a turbulent legislative history. Following the collapse of François Bayrou’s government on September 8, 2025, after a failed confidence vote, Lecornu was appointed prime minister on September 9. His initial cabinet announcement on October 6, 2025, triggered immediate opposition, leading to his prompt resignation and reappointment by Macron on October 10. In his general policy statement on October 14, 2025, Lecornu sought to build consensus by proposing to suspend the implementation of the 2023 pension reform until the 2027 presidential election, a concession that temporarily secured the neutrality of the Socialist Party (PS) during subsequent no-confidence votes. 

This arrangement fractured in January 2026, when the government invoked Article 49.3 of the Constitution to pass the remaining parts of the 2026 budget without a parliamentary vote. The decision prompted immediate no-confidence motions from the opposition. The government survived these challenges when the censure motion introduced by La France Insoumise (LFI) failed, receiving 269 votes in favor—20 short of the 289 votes required for a majority—while the National Rally (RN) motion drew 142 votes. To prevent the Socialists from backing the censure, the government made concessions, including €1 university lunches for students and increased commitments to social housing. 

But as the 2027 presidential race begins in earnest, the opposition is no longer in a mood for compromises. Marine Le Pen, the leading candidate for the far-right RN, has weaponized the budget crisis, proposing a €125 billion cost-cutting program. Her plan relies on slashing France’s net contribution to the European Union budget and cutting welfare benefits for non-citizens, alongside a promise to lower the retirement age to 60 for certain workers. On the left, Jean-Luc Mélenchon of LFI has put forward a radical economic platform, calling on the European Central Bank to cancel the French sovereign debt it holds—a proposal he frames as neutralizing "debt we owe ourselves"—while demanding aggressive corporate tax increases. 

Olivier Faure, the first secretary of the Socialist Party, has warned that the era of concessions is over, stating that there is "no suspense" regarding the 2027 budget. Faure declared that the PS will vote to censure the government if Lecornu attempts to pass the bill through backdoor negotiations with the far right or if it entrenches further austerity. Similarly, LFI has stated it will automatically censure the government if the bill contains the proposed spending cuts. Jordan Bardella, president of the RN, has likewise refused to rule out a censure "by principle."

If the National Assembly rejects the budget or censures the cabinet, Lecornu will face an existential constitutional dilemma. The government could invoke Article 49.3 once more, but doing so would almost certainly unite the left and far-right opposition in a fatal censure vote, instantly dissolving the cabinet. 

Should the government fall or the budget fail to pass within the 70-day constitutional limit mandated by Article 47, the executive branch must resort to emergency measures. Under Article 47, if Parliament fails to reach a decision within 70 days, the government can theoretically implement the budget provisions by executive Ordinance. Alternatively, the government could petition Parliament for a "special rollover law" (loi spéciale or loi de roulement) to continue collecting taxes, coupled with a presidential decree to make emergency funds available at 2026 levels. However, using these stopgap powers to bypass a hostile parliament in an election year would represent an unprecedented and legally precarious expansion of presidential power, likely triggering a full-scale constitutional crisis.

Lecornu has publicly declared himself "ready to be censured," warning that if the opposition brings down his cabinet, he will regain his "liberté" (freedom) to speak out. But for international investors and European partners, a government collapse in Paris is less about political freedom and more about systemic risk. With the 2027 election looming, the budget of the first semester may turn out to be a trap from which neither the government nor the French state can easily escape.
