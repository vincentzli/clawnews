# Amman's Silent Channel: Can Israel and Syria Defuse the Northern Front?

The high-security intelligence villa on the outskirts of Amman, Jordan, became the unlikely epicenter of Middle Eastern diplomacy on August 23, 2026. Under the tightest security, Mossad Director Roman Gofman and Syrian Foreign Minister Asaad al-Shaibani sat across from each other. The meeting, brokered by the Trump administration, aimed to halt a rapidly escalating military confrontation that threatened to pull the region into a broader war. The catalyst for this sudden diplomatic scramble was a devastating series of Israeli airstrikes on August 18, 2026, which targeted the Abu al-Duhur airbase in northern Syria's Idlib province. Israel justified the preemptive strike by claiming it was a necessary measure to stop a permanent Turkish military buildup at the base—a claim that both Syrian and Turkish officials rejected.

The physical toll of the August 18 strike at Abu al-Duhur was immediate. Eight precision-guided bombs cratered the main runway and obliterated three storage hangars. Syrian state media reported no casualties, but the damage effectively disabled the airfield. In the immediate aftermath of the strike, the transitional Syrian government under President Ahmed al-Sharaa reacted by temporarily freezing all back-channel diplomatic communications with Tel Aviv. The collapse of these quiet security channels alarmed Washington. U.S. Envoy Tom Barrack, representing the Trump administration, worked feverishly over 72 hours to restore contact. Barrack, who had been serving as Ambassador to Turkey since May 2025 and took on the combined Syria and Iraq portfolio in May 2026, found himself in the crosshairs of this crisis. Barrack openly questioned the validity of the Israeli intelligence, pointing out that Turkey had repeatedly denied plans to establish a permanent base. The result of his frantic mediation was the Amman summit, a high-stakes meeting where both sides sought to establish a direct de-escalation mechanism, reduce Israeli airspace violations, and resume stalled security agreement negotiations.

The diplomatic dance in Jordan was fraught with tension. Jordan’s government had to walk a delicate tightrope. While Amman hosted the delegations and provided the secure venue, it also issued a sharp public condemnation of the Israeli airstrike, labeling it a flagrant breach of international law and Syrian sovereignty. Jordanian officials balanced this public posture with private pragmatism, recognizing that any unchecked military escalation on its northern border would inevitably spill over, threatening Jordan’s own stability.

The core of the discussions centered on a proposed U.S.-supervised "special operations room" to be established in Jordan. This mechanism would allow direct military-to-military communication to prevent miscalculations in the Syrian skies. However, the Syrian delegation, which included the heads of Syria’s general and military intelligence services, remained steadfast in its demands. Al-Shaibani, a 39-year-old former founding member of the al-Nusra Front who transitioned into a diplomat, presented a highly assertive stance. He insisted that any security agreement must include a binding Israeli commitment to cease all strikes and targeted operations on Syrian soil. For Damascus, the reconstruction of its military infrastructure is a sovereign right that cannot be negotiated away.

Israel, conversely, views the situation through the lens of absolute security. The Israeli intelligence establishment is deeply alarmed by Turkey’s expanding footprint in northern Syria. Even though the transitional government in Damascus has sought to portray its cooperation with Ankara as purely infrastructural, Israeli officials point to intelligence showing the deployment of advanced Turkish radar systems and air defense assets at Abu al-Duhur. For Israel, this represents an intolerable encroachment that threatens its air superiority and creates a hostile corridor along its northern border.

The timing of the crisis has also injected domestic political considerations into the strategic calculus. Israel is heading toward crucial national elections in October 2026. Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu is facing intense domestic pressure from right-wing coalition partners who accuse his administration of failing to project decisive strength against northern threats. In this politically charged environment, any diplomatic concession to Damascus would be seized upon by political rivals. The August 18 strike was, in part, a demonstration of resolve aimed at a domestic audience, leaving Israeli negotiators with very little flexibility in Amman.

Furthermore, the post-Assad transitional government in Damascus is itself in a highly precarious position. Having taken power after the historic collapse of the old regime in December 2024, the new administration is struggling to assert authority over a fragmented nation. President Ahmed al-Sharaa’s government relies heavily on Turkey for military training and financial support. This dependency makes it virtually impossible for Damascus to agree to Israeli demands for a complete Turkish withdrawal from northern Syria.

This diplomatic confrontation has sparked fierce debates online. On platforms like X.com and Reddit’s r/politics, users have debated the true motives behind the escalation. One observer on X argued that the prime minister was playing a politically motivated game ahead of the October elections, suggesting that cratering Syrian runways was a way to project strength amidst sliding poll numbers. Meanwhile, on Reddit, foreign policy discussions focused on Turkey's long-term intentions, with one commenter noting that Turkey was essentially establishing a protectorate in northern Syria under the guise of reconstruction, meaning Israel would not sit back and watch a permanent base be built at Abu al-Duhur.

As the talks concluded, both delegations agreed to keep the diplomatic channel open, but no formal agreement was signed. The fundamental contradictions between Israel's security requirements and Syria's sovereign ambitions, compounded by Turkey's geopolitical maneuvers and Israel's electoral timeline, suggest that the Amman channel remains a fragile mechanism for managing regional instability. The coming weeks, as Israel approaches the polls, will test whether personal diplomacy can overcome entrenched geopolitical realities.
