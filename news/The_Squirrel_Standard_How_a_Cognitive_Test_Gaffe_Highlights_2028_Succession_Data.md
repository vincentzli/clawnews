# The "Squirrel" Standard: How a Cognitive Test Gaffe Highlights 2028 Succession Data

On May 4, 2026, during an address at the White House Small Business Summit, President Donald Trump veered off his economic script to defend his mental acuity, accelerating early maneuvers in the 2028 presidential race. Boasting about "acing" the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) three times, the President recounted his experience with the clinical screening tool. "The first question is very easy," Trump told the audience. "You have a lion, a bear, an alligator, and a, what's another good..? A squirrel. Which is the squirrel?" He proceeded to claim that while the animal identification section is simple, the test grows "very tough" by the end, asserting that "nobody's going to get all 30 questions correct" except for him, before demanding all future presidential and vice-presidential candidates be "forced to take a Cognitive Examination prior to entering the Race."

The medical reaction was immediate. Dr. Ziad Nasreddine, the creator of the MoCA, issued a prompt clinical clarification. He emphasized that the assessment is not an intelligence quotient test but rather a baseline measure designed to detect mild cognitive impairment or early signs of dementia. Achieving a perfect score, Nasreddine noted, simply indicates an absence of obvious neurological impairment—a threshold met by approximately 10% of people in the President's age group. Across social media platforms like X and Reddit's r/politics, critics mocked the concept of a sitting executive bragging about his capacity to identify a squirrel. Conversely, the President's defenders framed the moment differently; conservative users pointed to his willingness to take the test and publish the results as a standard of health transparency that his predecessor, former President Joe Biden, allegedly lacked.

These public reactions intersect with a documented shift in voter sentiment regarding age and leadership. According to a recent CBS News poll, 71% of Americans now favor maximum age limits for elected officials—a policy that holds higher support among senior citizens than younger adults. Corroborating this trend, a Pew Research poll found that only 3% of Americans believe the ideal age for a president is in their 70s or older, with nearly 80% favoring federal age caps. While the median age of the U.S. population sits at 38.9, the median age in the Senate remains 63.3.

Within the Republican Party, the race to secure the 2028 nomination is actively dividing along lines of continuity versus executive alternatives. Vice President J.D. Vance enters the cycle as the statistical frontrunner, currently leading the GOP field with 38% to 53% support in early polling. Vance is running on a "Third Term" platform. Operating as the RNC finance chairman, he is consolidating resources from traditional GOP megadonors and Silicon Valley billionaires. However, polling indicates nearly half of independent voters prefer "someone else." 

Florida Governor Ron DeSantis is attempting to capture those independent voters by positioning himself as a disciplined, policy-driven alternative. DeSantis is pitching the "Florida Model"—highlighting the elimination of property taxes, aggressive redistricting, and his state's unique "AI Bill of Rights." Despite this approach, DeSantis is currently polling in the single digits, capturing 2% to 9% of the primary vote. His path is structurally challenged by a crowded "Sunshine State lane," as Secretary of State Marco Rubio currently commands 13% to 17% support, outperforming DeSantis among senior citizens and native Floridian voters.

Across the aisle, the Democratic Party is witnessing a "Shadow Campaign" engineered to present a younger contrast. California Governor Gavin Newsom has leveraged his Campaign for Democracy PAC to raise millions for Democrats in red states and swing districts. Newsom's strategy involves high-profile international travel to China and Israel to build executive credentials. Major Democratic donors in Hollywood and Silicon Valley are reportedly directing early resources to his operation. 

Simultaneously, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro is cementing his status as the pragmatic, swing-state alternative. Banking on his high approval ratings in a critical battleground state, Shapiro is drawing substantial interest from donors focused on rebuilding the "Blue Wall" in the Midwest. Both Newsom and Shapiro are explicitly using the 2026 midterms as a testing ground—campaigning and fundraising for down-ballot candidates to accumulate political capital and test national messaging against the current administration's record. 

The intersection of the President's MoCA comments and current polling data quantifies a volatile electoral landscape. As 71% of voters demand an end to aging leadership paradigms, the 2028 contenders are actively weaponizing this gerontocracy fatigue, transforming clinical gaffes into strategic openings in the race for the next presidential cycle.
