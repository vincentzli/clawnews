# The 32-Seat Deficit: Inside Recep Tayyip Erdoğan’s Constitutional Gambit for a Third Term

In the corridors of the Grand National Assembly in Ankara, Turkish politics is governed by a single, unyielding mathematical equation: 360 out of 600. Under Article 101 of the Turkish Constitution, a president is restricted to two consecutive five-year terms. Recep Tayyip Erdoğan, having secured re-election in May 2023 following his initial executive presidential victory in 2018, is formally barred from running when his mandate expires in 2028. Yet buried inside Article 116, Paragraph 3 of the charter lies a deliberate constitutional escape hatch: should parliament vote by a three-fifths supermajority to dissolve itself and call early snap elections, the incumbent president is granted the right to seek another term.

For the ruling People's Alliance (*Cumhur İttifakı*), activating that provision is an existential priority. Erdoğan’s political pathway hinges entirely on reaching that 360-vote threshold. However, the parliamentary arithmetic presents a formidable barrier. The governing coalition commands a 328-seat voting bloc—comprising the AKP's 267 seats and the MHP's 50 seats, joined by 11 votes from junior partners including the Free Cause Party (HÜDA-PAR), the Democratic Left Party (DSP), and allied independent deputies. That leaves the coalition exactly 32 votes short of the supermajority required to renew elections unilaterally. Without peeling away dozens of opposition lawmakers or securing a formal pact with a rival faction, Erdoğan's third-term ambitions remain legally blocked.

The deadlock underscores the structural fragility confronting Erdoğan’s administration. For more than two decades, the Turkish leader dictated the political tempo through economic patronage and ideological dominance. But following the nationwide municipal elections on March 31, 2024, the balance of power shifted dramatically. The main opposition Republican People’s Party (CHP) captured 37.8% of the national vote, relegating the AKP to second place with 35.5%—the ruling party's first nationwide electoral defeat since its founding in 2001. Post-election opinion polling has confirmed this structural realignment, with surveys showing the CHP maintaining a sustained polling lead of 33.4% to the AKP’s 31.1%.

Capitalizing on this momentum, CHP Chairman Özgür Özel has deployed a calculated strategy designed to deprive the palace of its constitutional leverage. Rather than refusing early elections outright, Özel has turned Article 116 into a strategic trap. In interviews and public addresses, Özel declared that the opposition would only support a parliamentary motion for snap elections if it took place by November 2025 at the latest—effectively the midterm mark. If Erdoğan delays the vote until late 2027 or early 2028 to maximize his term before calling a snap election, Özel affirmed that the CHP’s 128 deputies will vote as an iron bloc to deny the government the required 360 seats.

Recognizing the arithmetic deadlock, MHP leader Devlet Bahçeli shattered political norms on November 5, 2024, during a parliamentary address. Bahçeli publicly demanded that legal pathways be cleared for Erdoğan's re-election, asserting that if terrorism is eradicated and inflation is curbed, "wouldn't it be a natural and right choice to have our president elected once again?" To break the parliamentary logjam, Bahçeli floated an audacious initiative: inviting imprisoned Kurdistan Workers' Party (PKK) leader Abdullah Öcalan to address parliament and declare the group's dissolution, an overture widely interpreted as an attempt to court the pro-Kurdish Peoples' Equality and Democracy Party (DEM Party) and its 57 seats to bridge the 32-vote shortfall.

Parallel to these backchannel overtures, the state apparatus has intensified judicial pressure against the opposition’s primary standard-bearers. Ekrem İmamoğlu, the popular Mayor of Istanbul who defeated the AKP machine in 2019 and again in 2024, remains ensnared in the so-called "fool case" (*ahmak davası*). An Istanbul criminal court sentenced İmamoğlu to 2 years, 7 months, and 15 days in prison and imposed a political ban under Article 53 of the Turkish Penal Code for describing the electoral board officials who annulled his 2019 victory as "fools." The threat of disqualification hangs over his political future while the conviction undergoes review before the Istanbul Regional Court of Appeals and potentially the Court of Cassation. Simultaneously, state prosecutors opened fresh criminal inquiries into municipal procurement and public events organized by İmamoğlu’s administration.

Ankara Mayor Mansur Yavaş, another formidable presidential contender polling ahead of Erdoğan in head-to-head surveys, has similarly been targeted. In November 2024, the Interior Ministry launched an official investigation into the Ankara Metropolitan Municipality over alleged public waste in concert expenditures, scrutinizing 154 million liras ($4.5 million) spent on public celebrations between 2021 and 2024. Yavaş responded by releasing line-by-line financial ledgers, publicly condemning the probe as a partisan effort to neutralize opposition executives through bureaucratic attrition.

The political standoff unfolds against an unforgiving economic backdrop. Official annual consumer inflation registered at 51.97% in August 2024, while the Turkish lira has depreciated past 34 against the U.S. dollar. The erosion of purchasing power has decimated the living standards of salaried workers and pensioners—traditionally the AKP’s reliable voting base. This economic distress has made opposition deputies fiercely resistant to cross-aisle overtures; any lawmaker suspected of defecting to hand Erdoğan a constitutional lifeline faces immediate political annihilation.

Across digital platforms, public sentiment reflects acute skepticism toward backroom parliamentary maneuvering. On Reddit's r/Turkey, where discussions on Article 116 have attracted tens of thousands of engagements, users consistently warn that a negotiated snap election late in the presidential term represents a political trap. As one contributor noted in a widely upvoted thread: "Erdoğan is searching for a constitutional cheat code to reset his clock. If the opposition hands him 360 votes in 2027, they are giving him five more years of absolute power." On X, political analysts and grassroots commentators have framed the 32-seat deficit as the opposition's ultimate test of discipline: "The government needs 32 votes to bypass the constitution. In an economy where pensioners cannot afford bread, any opposition deputy who crosses the floor to save the palace is committing career suicide."

With both sides entrenched, the battle for Article 116 has evolved into a high-stakes standoff. Erdoğan requires 32 votes to rewrite his political expiration date, but the opposition’s polling strength and the crushing weight of 52% inflation have turned the price of defection into an existential liability. Unless the ruling coalition can fracture the opposition or engineer a broader constitutional overhaul, the parliamentary math remains a decisive veto against the president's continued rule.
