# Regulators Alert, Wall Street Stakes: The Paradox of Hyperliquid’s Unstoppable Rise

The Monetary Authority of Singapore (MAS) added Hyperliquid, the breakout star of the decentralized perpetuals market, to its Investor Alert List (IAL) on June 26, 2026. The IAL is a public directory maintained by the Singaporean regulator to warn retail investors about entities that are not licensed locally but might be wrongly perceived as regulated. Within hours of the listing, Hyperliquid’s community responded on social media, clarifying that the listing was not an enforcement action or a finding of fraud. Instead, the platform pointed to its fundamental architecture: it operates as a decentralized, self-custodial on-chain protocol that has never claimed to be licensed or regulated by Singaporean authorities. In a statement posted on X, the protocol emphasized its status as "permissionless infrastructure" where users maintain self-custody and transactions settle on-chain, noting that operations remained unchanged.

The regulatory warning in Singapore highlights a growing friction between sovereign oversight and permissionless networks. The IAL is a consumer-protection tool that flags entities that, based on information available to the MAS, may be wrongly perceived as being licensed, authorized, or regulated. Over the years, MAS has added several major crypto exchanges to the list, including Binance, Bybit, KuCoin, and Bitget, typically to warn local consumers that they operate outside the regulatory perimeter and lack MAS protections. In Singapore's active cryptocurrency community, users on Reddit's r/sgcrypto and X discussed how they navigate the platform using self-custody wallets and regulated fiat on-ramps. Many pointed out that because the protocol operates entirely on-chain, local regulators face a technical wall when trying to restrict access. Rather than serving as an outright ban, the listing acts as a consumer advisory, indicating that local users who choose to trade on the L1 do so without statutory regulatory recourse. One user on r/sgcrypto commented that "since it's decentralized and on-chain, MAS can't really block it, they can only warn you that you're on your own if things go south."

Yet, while Singaporean regulators wave yellow flags, Wall Street is aggressively moving in. The institutional validation of Hyperliquid is unprecedented for a pure-play DeFi protocol. On June 16, 2026, the native HYPE token hit an all-time high of approximately $76.81, propelled by an $11.5 million short squeeze and a protocol mechanism that buys back 90% to 97% of revenues. The token's meteoric rise was also fueled by increased trading volume from new offerings such as SpaceX perpetual contracts and prediction markets. Although the price corrected to the $62–$66 range in late June, the financial machinery backing it shows no signs of slowing down.

In mid-May 2026, Bitwise Asset Management launched the Bitwise Hyperliquid Staking ETF (BHYP), a regulated investment vehicle that holds and actively stakes HYPE tokens. Bitwise utilizes its proprietary Onchain Solutions infrastructure to capture daily staking rewards, allowing institutional clients to gain direct exposure to the protocol’s yield. Hunter Horsley, CEO of Bitwise, has remarked on the growing demand among institutional allocators for yield-generating digital assets that operate independently of traditional banking systems.

Following Bitwise's lead, Grayscale Investments announced its own plans in late June for a Hyperliquid Staking ETF (HYPG), aiming to capture a share of the institutional appetite for high-performance DeFi. Peter Mintzberg, the chief executive of Grayscale, stated that the firm intends to offer the most cost-efficient access to Hyperliquid, outlining a competitive management fee of 0.29%. The juxtaposition of two of the world's largest digital asset managers structuring regulated investment products around a protocol flagged on Singapore’s Investor Alert List reveals the deep dichotomy in modern crypto regulation.

For the retail market, the incentive to trade on Hyperliquid has only intensified. In June 2026, the protocol launched a highly anticipated points program on Hypertrade, its dedicated decentralized exchange aggregator. Hypertrade scans the native Hyperliquid L1, routing swaps through Spot, Hyperswap, and Prjx to minimize slippage. The points program rewards users for swapping, providing v3 liquidity, and onboarding new users through referrals, with the points designed to convert directly into HYPE tokens. This has created a massive feedback loop: trading volume on the aggregator surges, driving more network fees back to HYPE stakers, which in turn makes the Bitwise and Grayscale ETFs more attractive to institutional buyers.

As the boundary between decentralized protocols and traditional finance blurs, the MAS alert is a reminder of the limits of regulatory reach. Users in Singapore continue to access the platform by bridging USDC via self-custody wallets, relying on local regulated on-ramps to convert Singapore dollars into stablecoins before transferring them onto the Hyperliquid L1 chain. Because the user maintains control of their private keys and the contracts are executed by code, the transactions remain outside the traditional custodial framework. The coming months will test whether sovereign regulators can adapt to a world where financial primitives exist as immutable software, or if the regulatory perimeter will simply redirect users into the self-custodial shadows.
