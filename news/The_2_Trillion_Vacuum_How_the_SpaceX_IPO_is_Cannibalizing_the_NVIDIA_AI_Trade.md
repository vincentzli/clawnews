# The $2 Trillion Vacuum: How the SpaceX IPO is Cannibalizing the NVIDIA AI Trade

On the trading floors of Lower Manhattan and in the hyper-caffeinated forums of r/stocks, the conversation has shifted from the "Magnificent Seven" to a singular, gravity-defying event: the "SpaceX Siphon." On May 20, 2026, SpaceX officially filed its S-1 prospectus with the SEC, targeting a valuation between $1.75 trillion and $2 trillion for its June 12 debut on the Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX. It is a moment of unprecedented scale, but for the broader market, it is behaving less like a new opportunity and more like a liquidity black hole, specifically at the expense of the reigning king of the AI era, NVIDIA (NVDA).

The "liquidity drain" theory, once a fringe concern on StockTwits, has become the dominant institutional narrative. The math is brutal and zero-sum. With a target raise of $75 billion—the largest in global history, eclipsing Saudi Aramco’s $29.4 billion—institutional desks are being forced to find cash in an environment where capital is no longer cheap. Under the new Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh, who succeeded Jerome Powell on May 22, the "higher for longer" mantra has kept the 10-year Treasury yield hovering at 4.65%, making massive equity reallocations a painful necessity rather than a surplus-cash exercise.

"Where do you find $75 billion in a week?" asks one widely circulated post on r/stocks. "You don't find it in your couch cushions. You sell your winners. You sell NVDA." This sentiment is backed by the hard mechanics of passive indexing. Because SpaceX is expected to qualify for "fast-track" inclusion into the Nasdaq-100 and S&P 500 within 15 trading days of its debut, passive ETFs must legally prepare to buy roughly $40 billion in SPCX shares to match its projected index weight. To maintain balance, they must sell the existing heavyweights. NVIDIA, currently sporting a $5.2 trillion market cap, stands to see the largest absolute dollar outflow, estimated by analysts to reach $12 billion in a single rebalancing window.

The tension is exacerbated by the fact that SpaceX is no longer just a rocket company; it is a direct competitor for "frontier tech" dollars. Following its February 2026 merger with xAI, the company revealed a "Three-Pillar" business model that strikes at the heart of NVIDIA’s dominance. While Starlink remains the cash cow—generating $11.4 billion in 2025 revenue—the S-1 highlighted a new "AI Infrastructure" segment. This includes a massive $1.25 billion-per-month compute-services agreement with Anthropic, utilizing SpaceX’s planned "Orbital Data Centers." 

"The trade is simple," noted a prominent Fintwit analyst. "NVIDIA sells the shovels for the ground-based AI gold rush. SpaceX is building the gold mine in low-Earth orbit. If you’re an institutional PM, you’re trimming the 32x trailing P/E on NVDA to get into the 100x sales 'Elon Premium' of SPCX."

However, the $2 trillion valuation is built on a foundation of aggressive assumptions and significant risk. The S-1 revealed that while SpaceX achieved an Adjusted EBITDA of $6.6 billion in 2025, it posted a staggering GAAP net loss of $4.94 billion. Capital expenditures are running at an annual clip of $40 billion as the company scales Starship V3 and its AI constellation. "This is the ultimate exit liquidity play," warned one skeptic on r/wallstreetbets. "They’re dumping $5B in annual losses onto the public at a $2T cap because the federal gravy train is slowing down."

That "gravy train" is indeed under threat. The political backdrop in May 2026 is one of fractured alliances. Despite Elon Musk’s early-term role in the Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE), his public fallout with President Trump in late 2025 led to the dissolution of the entity. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has signaled a more disciplined approach to federal outlays, and the White House has directed NASA and the Pentagon to review SpaceX’s $22 billion in existing contracts. 

In this climate, the SpaceX IPO is more than a listing; it is a survival maneuver. By earmarking a record 30% of the float for retail investors, Musk is bypassing the cooling institutional sentiment and tapping into the "cult of personality" that once fueled Tesla. But the cost of this maneuver may be a sustained "liquidity winter" for the rest of the tech sector. As the June 12 listing approaches, the market is holding its breath, watching as the $2 trillion vacuum begins to pull the oxygen out of the AI trade that has defined the last three years.
