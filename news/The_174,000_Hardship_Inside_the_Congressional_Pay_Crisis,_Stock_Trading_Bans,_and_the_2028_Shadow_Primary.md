# The $174,000 Hardship: Inside the Congressional Pay Crisis, Stock Trading Bans, and the 2028 Shadow Primary

In Washington D.C., the collision of economic reality and political privilege is boiling over as the 119th Congress navigates a profoundly controversial debate over its own compensation. In May 2025, House Speaker Mike Johnson sparked a firestorm by defending congressional stock trading, arguing that lawmakers require the supplementary income to "take care of their families" and "make ends meet." Johnson pointed specifically to the $174,000 base salary for rank-and-file members of the House and Senate, a figure that has been subjected to a continuous legislative pay freeze since 2009. According to the Speaker’s arithmetic, when adjusted for inflation, lawmakers have lost approximately 31% of their purchasing power over the preceding 17 years. Johnson framed this depreciation as an "extreme sacrifice," warning that without the ability to actively manage stock portfolios, fewer "qualified people" would be willing to serve in federal office.

The arithmetic of Johnson’s defense immediately collided with the broader economic realities of Capitol Hill. While the $174,000 base salary remains frozen, it still represents more than double the median U.S. household income. Furthermore, as Speaker of the House, Johnson himself collects a significantly higher salary of $223,500, supplemented by taxpayer-funded travel reimbursements and office budgets. This compensation structure stands in stark contrast to the financial baseline of the staff who operate the legislative machinery. It was only in 2022 that a minimum annual salary of $45,000 was established for House staff. Funded by a 21% increase in the Member’s Representational Allowance (MRA), this baseline was implemented to combat severe retention issues and the crushing cost of living in Washington, D.C. Prior to this mandate, entry-level staff assistants routinely earned between $30,000 and $40,000. While the House also raised its maximum staff salary cap to $203,700 to achieve parity with the Senate and passed H.Res. 1096 to open the door for staff unionization, the Senate continues to operate without any chamber-wide minimum salary floor.

Johnson's defense of stock trading arrived precisely as bipartisan momentum for ethics reform reached a critical juncture, effectively serving as a roadblock against new legislation. The Stop Insider Trading Act, introduced in January 2026 by Representative Bryan Steil and later in the Senate by Senators Bill Cassidy and Pete Ricketts, represents the latest attempt to curb financial conflicts of interest. The bill explicitly prohibits members of Congress, their spouses, and dependent children from purchasing any new publicly traded stocks. It also mandates a public notice of at least seven days—but no more than 14 days—prior to the sale of an existing stock. However, the proposed penalties are relatively light, establishing a fine of either $2,000 or 10% of the investment value, alongside the forfeiture of net gains. Despite advancing through the House Administration Committee in January 2026, the legislation currently awaits a full floor vote.

An even more stringent alternative, the Ending Trading and Holdings in Congressional Stocks (ETHICS) Act, achieved a historic milestone in July 2025 when it cleared the Senate Homeland Security and Governmental Affairs Committee. Championed by a bipartisan coalition including Senators Jeff Merkley, Josh Hawley, Gary Peters, and Jon Ossoff, the ETHICS Act demands the immediate prohibition of new stock purchases upon enactment and forces lawmakers to fully divest their holdings within 90 days. Concurrently, the Restore Trust in Congress Act, led by Representative Chip Roy and Representative Becca Balint, pushes for a total ban on both owning and selling individual stocks. Despite advancing out of committee, the legislation has not yet been scheduled for a full floor vote by leadership, preventing any of the bills from reaching the President’s desk for a signature.

The resulting public backlash has been highly visible across digital platforms. Retail trading communities, particularly on the subreddit r/wallstreetbets, continuously track what they term "congressional alpha"—the statistical tendency of politicians to systematically outperform the S&P 500. Users routinely highlight the glaring asymmetry of lawmakers claiming financial hardship while leveraging non-public committee briefings to time market movements. As one prominent Reddit user pointed out during the height of the debate, it is difficult to synthesize the image of politicians struggling on a $174,000 salary when they simultaneously possess access to closed-door data that directly influences corporate valuations. The sentiment across political forums like r/politics mirrors this skepticism, rejecting the premise that a six-figure government salary requires unregulated insider trading as a de facto supplemental income program.

This persistent friction over financial ethics is rapidly spilling over into the early posturing for the 2028 Presidential election, often referred to as the "Shadow Primary." By May 2026, political prediction markets such as Kalshi and Polymarket have become the primary barometers for this quiet race, pricing the probabilities of potential candidates for the upcoming cycle. In a significant market shift, Secretary of State Marco Rubio has recently surged past Vice President JD Vance in presidential election odds. Trading data places Rubio’s implied probability of capturing the presidency at 19% to 20%, edging out Vance, who sits at 17% to 19%. Market analysts attribute Rubio’s ascent to his high-profile diplomatic role and perceived broad appeal, though Vance remains the mathematical favorite to secure the Republican nomination at approximately 38%, cementing his position as the institutional heir to the MAGA coalition.

On the Democratic side, California Governor Gavin Newsom commands a decisive lead in the prediction markets. Newsom currently holds a 16% to 17% probability of winning the presidency in 2028, establishing a dominant ceiling over other Democratic figures such as Senator Jon Ossoff at 6% and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez at 5%. Specialized "Exact Outcome" contracts on Kalshi currently price a general election matchup of Newsom versus Vance, or Newsom versus Rubio, as the most probable scenarios, with each pairing carrying a 10% to 12% likelihood. These markets operate as real-time, capital-backed polling, reflecting trader sentiment on how candidates will navigate vulnerabilities, including the very congressional gridlock and ethics scandals currently consuming Washington.

Ironically, the explosion of capital flowing into these political prediction markets has triggered the only swift, unanimous ethical action seen in Congress this year. In early May 2026, the Senate unanimously approved an internal rules change explicitly prohibiting members and their staff from placing financial bets on current events or legislative outcomes. A parallel effort is currently underway in the House, led by Representatives Jason Crow and Michael Cloud, who are urgently pushing for identical chamber rules to ban lawmakers from participating in platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket. The justification for this rapid prohibition is the undeniable conflict of interest inherent in lawmakers placing "insider bets" on the outcome of the exact legislative processes they control.

The legislative contrast is stark. Lawmakers swiftly moved to ban themselves from participating in political prediction markets—where the maximum wager is often capped and the economic footprint is relatively small—yet they continue to stonewall comprehensive bans on trading individual corporate stocks, where portfolios routinely stretch into the tens of millions of dollars. As the 2028 Shadow Primary continues to take shape, candidates tethered to the current legislative infrastructure will inevitably be forced to defend this economic dissonance. The enduring reality of the 119th Congress is a systemic refusal to sever the tie between public service and private portfolio management, anchored by leadership claims that $174,000 constitutes an unlivable wage, all while the staff powering the Capitol operate against a $45,000 minimum baseline.
