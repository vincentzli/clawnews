# The Red Sea Siege: Houthi Blockade Neutralizes Saudi Arabia's Oil Bypass Route and Ignites Dual-Chokepoint Energy Crisis

On July 22, 2026, the Red Sea became the epicenter of a new global energy crisis when a projectile struck the commercial tanker *Encelia* approximately 70 nautical miles southwest of the Saudi port of Al Shuqaiq. According to reports from the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations (UKMTO), the strike sparked a major fire on the Saudi-flagged vessel, forcing the crew into a desperate damage-control operation. Hours later, the Houthi rebels in Yemen claimed responsibility for the strike, asserting it was part of their newly declared maritime blockade targeting all shipping going to and from Saudi Arabian ports. They also claimed to have targeted a second vessel, the Bahri-owned Very Large Crude Carrier (VLCC) *Layla*, though that strike remained unconfirmed.

The maritime ambush sent shockwaves through global markets. Brent crude prices rose by over 3% to cross $95 per barrel in chaotic trading on July 22, as shipping companies realized that the global economy’s secondary artery had been severely disrupted. This escalation is not an isolated event; it represents a coordinated stranglehold. The Strait of Hormuz, the world’s primary oil transit route, is already highly restricted following weeks of escalating hostilities between the United States and Iran, which recently culminated in ten consecutive nights of American airstrikes against Iranian targets in response to the deaths of U.S. service members. With both the Strait of Hormuz and the Bab el-Mandeb Strait now effectively contested, the global economy faces a dual-chokepoint energy crisis with no immediate relief in sight.

For Saudi Arabia, the Houthi blockade is a direct strike at its strategic vulnerability. For decades, Riyadh’s geopolitical insurance policy against Iranian threats in the Persian Gulf has been the East-West Pipeline, also known as Petroline. This 1,200-kilometer conduit allows the Kingdom to pump millions of barrels of crude oil across the Arabian Peninsula to the terminal at Yanbu on the Red Sea, completely bypassing the Strait of Hormuz. In recent weeks, as U.S. and Iranian forces traded fire in the Gulf, Saudi Arabia shifted over 70% of its oil exports to the Yanbu terminal. By declaring a blockade on all vessels calling at Saudi ports, the Houthis have effectively neutralized this bypass. Merchant ships that once considered the Red Sea a safe harbor are now turning back.

The blockade was officially declared on July 20, 2026, by Houthi military spokesperson Brigadier General Yahya Saree, operating through the group's Humanitarian Operations Coordination Center (HOCC). Saree framed the embargo as an act of retaliation—a "blockade for blockade"—citing a recent airstrike on Sanaa International Airport and Saudi-led economic restrictions on Yemen. He warned that any vessel loading or discharging cargo at Saudi ports would be treated as a target within the "operational reach" of Houthi missiles and attack drones. Ship-tracking data quickly confirmed the threat's efficacy, showing at least six to seven oil tankers and container ships executing sharp U-turns near the Bab el-Mandeb Strait rather than risk entering the southern Red Sea.

The international response was immediate and highly polarized. In Washington, Secretary of State Marco Rubio, speaking from a regional summit in the Philippines, characterized the blockade as "problematic" and directly accused Iran of orchestrating the crisis. Rubio noted that the U.S. is monitoring the situation and warned that if Iran continues to shun diplomatic negotiations, the U.S. will "do what’s necessary to protect our interests." Meanwhile, Pakistan strongly condemned the blockade. Citing its Strategic Mutual Defence Agreement with Riyadh, Pakistan's Foreign Ministry warned that any hostile acts against its shipping interests would be treated as a grave threat to national security, stating it reserves the right to use lawful force in self-defense.

The maritime industry has responded with extreme caution. The European Union’s defensive naval mission, Operation Aspides, raised its threat level to "Medium" for the entire Red Sea region and advised all merchant vessels linked to Saudi Arabia, the U.S., or Israel to avoid transiting the Bab el-Mandeb Strait entirely. The practical closure of these routes has driven panic in financial markets. On Reddit's r/wallstreetbets, oil quickly became the most discussed asset, with retail traders posting high-volume discussions about options trading. Speculative traders began betting heavily on "oil calls," sharing YOLO positions aimed at capital gains if crude prices continue to climb. Many users debated whether a ceasefire rumor might crash the market or if a full-scale blockade would push Brent oil to a record-breaking $120 per barrel, warning that persistent energy inflation would force central banks to hike interest rates.

As the Trump administration weighs its military options, the Saudi government has promised to take "all necessary security actions" to defend its commercial fleet. However, with the *Encelia* still smoldering and the *Layla* running dark with its AIS transponder disabled, the immediate future of the global oil trade remains highly uncertain. The dual-chokepoint crisis has exposed the limits of regional pipeline bypasses and demonstrated that in the era of modern drone warfare, no maritime route is beyond the reach of geopolitical conflict.
