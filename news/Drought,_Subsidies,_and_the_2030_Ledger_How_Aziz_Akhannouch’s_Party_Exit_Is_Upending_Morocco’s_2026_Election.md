# Drought, Subsidies, and the 2030 Ledger: How Aziz Akhannouch’s Party Exit Is Upending Morocco’s 2026 Election

RABAT — On the coastal plains of Benslimane, thirty miles northeast of Casablanca, heavy earth-moving equipment operates around the clock to level ground for the Grand Stade Hassan II. Designed with a planned capacity of 115,000 spectators and budgeted at 5 billion Moroccan dirhams ($500 million), the stadium is the centerpiece of Morocco’s co-hosting arrangement for the 2030 FIFA World Cup alongside Spain and Portugal. Yet eighty miles southwest, in the arid heartland of the Settat province, the Al Massira reservoir—Morocco’s second-largest water reservoir, with a capacity of 2.65 billion cubic meters—holds barely 1.4% of its volume, exposing vast swathes of desiccated, cracked earth.

This stark contrast between massive capital commitments to prestige sports infrastructure and the immediate strains of an environmental and cost-of-living crisis defines the political terrain leading into Morocco’s September 23, 2026 legislative elections. Voters will cast ballots for all 395 seats in the House of Representatives in the shadow of a profound political realignment: Prime Minister Aziz Akhannouch’s unexpected February 2026 departure from the leadership of the National Rally of Independents (RNI).

Akhannouch’s withdrawal was finalized on February 7, 2026, during an extraordinary party congress at the Mohammed VI Exhibition Park in El Jadida. After a decade leading the center-right, business-aligned formation, the billionaire oil executive announced he would not pursue another term, citing a personal commitment to two-term leadership. Mohamed Chaouki, an entrepreneur, founder of asset management firm AD Capital, member of parliament for Boulemane, and chairman of the parliamentary Committee on Public Finance Oversight, was elected unopposed as the RNI’s new president, securing 1,910 out of 1,933 cast ballots.

While Akhannouch remained in office to guide the government through the remainder of the legislative term, his surrender of the party presidency alters the post-election calculus. Article 47 of Morocco’s 2011 Constitution stipulates that the King appoints the Head of Government "from within the political party arriving ahead in the election of the members of the House of Representatives." Although the legal text permits any party member to be designated, royal and constitutional convention established since 2011—governing the appointments of Abdelilah Benkirane, Saad Dine El Otmani, and Akhannouch himself—has consistently conferred the premiership upon the sitting leader of the plurality party. By relinquishing the RNI presidency, Akhannouch has effectively stepped outside the institutional pathway to a second term, leaving Chaouki as the party’s presumptive prime ministerial nominee if the RNI secures another victory.

The leadership transition followed five years marked by historic climatological disruption and persistent price pressures. Morocco is now grappling with its sixth consecutive year of historic drought, the longest protracted dry spell since national monitoring began in the 1980s. Across the kingdom, national dam storage rates have hovered around 28.5%, forcing the Ministry of Equipment and Water, headed by Istiqlal Party Secretary-General Nizar Baraka, to implement emergency water preservation measures. These include periodic supply cutoffs in municipal pipes, weekly forced closures of traditional public bathhouses (hammams), and the rapid construction of emergency water transfer pipelines connecting the Sebou and Bouregreg river basins to prevent total supply failure in Casablanca and Rabat.

The persistent drought severely disrupted domestic food production. Agriculture accounts for roughly 30% of national employment, and successive rain deficits caused cereal yields to plunge by nearly 40% year-on-year. Core food inflation sustained a rate exceeding 6.5%, driven by price surges in basic pantry staples, including olive oil, which climbed above 100 dirhams ($10) per liter, and fresh produce.

The government’s response to soaring meat costs became a focal point of voter discontent. With the domestic livestock herd contracting by an estimated 38% due to parched pastures and expensive feed, the administration launched an emergency import subsidy framework ahead of the annual Eid al-Adha holiday. The state disbursed a direct subsidy of 500 dirhams ($50) per imported sheep to licensed livestock brokers importing animals from Spain, Portugal, and Romania, alongside sweeping waivers on customs duties and value-added tax. Addressing parliament, Akhannouch defended the policy as an indispensable intervention to protect the national breeding stock, noting that the measure successfully brought in nearly 800,000 head of livestock.

Rather than lowering consumer costs, the policy collided with market speculation. In souks across Casablanca, Fes, and Marrakech, sacrificial animals traded at prices between 4,000 and 8,000 dirhams—representing between one and two-and-a-half months of income for workers earning Morocco’s guaranteed minimum wage of approximately 3,120 dirhams. Opposition lawmakers and trade union leaders condemned the initiative as a state-funded windfall for a small tier of commercial importers who pocketed hundreds of millions of dirhams while retail red meat prices climbed to 120 to 140 dirhams per kilogram. The public backlash prompted a late-term strategic shift under Minister of Agriculture Ahmed Bouari, who assumed office in the October 2024 cabinet reshuffle, replacing physical feed distribution with direct cash allowances channeled to over 352,000 registered smallholders.

The livestock subsidies and inflation controversies have provided political ammunition for the opposition, led by former Prime Minister Abdelilah Benkirane of the Islamist Justice and Development Party (PJD). Following the PJD’s steep decline in 2021, when it plummeted from 125 seats to just 13, Benkirane has utilized populist rhetoric to mount a political comeback. On the campaign trail, Benkirane has centered his attacks on Akhannouch's personal wealth and corporate holdings, focusing on fuel distributor Afriquia, a subsidiary of the Akhannouch family's Akwa Group, amid complaints over refined petroleum distribution margins.

Benkirane explicitly tied the livestock controversy to the government’s rollout of the Unified Social Register (RSU), pointing out that while commercial importers received 500 dirhams per animal, certain vulnerable citizens, including low-income widows, saw their monthly state allocations compressed to an equivalent 500 dirhams under revised subsidy formulas. "Five hundred dirhams per sheep for the import barons," Benkirane declared at a party gathering in Sale, "while the most vulnerable families are told that 500 dirhams per month is enough to survive." On social media platforms, clips of his speeches have circulated widely, accompanied by renewed online expressions criticizing living costs and calling into question the executive's ties to commercial monopolies.

The governing coalition itself approaches the September 23 election under severe friction. The Authenticity and Modernity Party (PAM), which took second place in 2021 with 87 seats, has faced reputational fallout stemming from the "Escobar du Sahara" legal proceedings, which led to the arrest and prosecution of high-profile former party figures, including former Oriental regional council president Abdenbi Bioui and former parliamentarian Said Naciri, over allegations of international drug smuggling and illicit financial operations. PAM’s collective leadership, coordinated by Minister of National Territory Planning, Land Planning, Housing and Urban Policy Fatima Ezzahra El Mansouri, has struggled to contain the political damage in regional strongholds.

Concurrently, the Istiqlal Party, which won 81 seats in 2021, has sought to insulate its brand by highlighting the water ministry’s infrastructure agenda, including the fast-tracking of seawater desalination plants in Casablanca and Dakhla.

The election's outcome will ultimately turn on the divide between urban discontent and rural electoral mechanics. In large urban voting districts, low voter turnout and widespread cynicism among young citizens—nearly 36% of whom are not in employment, education, or training—could diminish the ruling parties' tallies. However, in rural and semi-rural constituencies, where the parliamentary majority is won, local patronage networks, tribal affiliations, and candidate-centric mobilization remain decisive, offering RNI and Istiqlal candidates a durable structural advantage.

With the 2030 World Cup requiring tens of billions of dirhams in public commitments—encompassing high-speed rail lines to Marrakech and Agadir, highway expansions, and stadium renovations—Rabat requires a cohesive legislative majority to pass upcoming austerity measures and structural tax reforms. Whether Mohamed Chaouki can steer the RNI to victory without Akhannouch at the top of the ticket will test whether the ruling party's administrative apparatus can withstand the economic and environmental headwinds confronting Moroccan households.
