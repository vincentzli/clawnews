# **Gaza’s Divided Peace: Inside the Diplomatic Gridlock, Shifting Lines, and Funding Disputes of the Board of Peace**

####

On the eastern edge of the Gaza Strip, a line of yellow-painted concrete blocks and freshly bulldozed earthen berms snakes through the agricultural fields of Beit Hanoun. This is the "Yellow Line," established under Phase I of the October 2025 "Comprehensive Plan to End the Gaza Conflict." In theory, it was designed to serve as the demarcation boundary for the phased withdrawal of the Israel Defense Forces (IDF) and the return of civilian administrative control to a newly formed, technocratic Palestinian authority. In practice, the line is a shifting, lethal frontier. 

By March 2026, the IDF had quietly introduced maps to international organizations displaying a new boundary: the "Orange Line." This updated line pushed the restricted military zone further westward, carving out an additional 11% of Gaza’s territory. Today, as the diplomatic process stalls, Israeli military forces occupy and coordinate operations across roughly 64% of the Gaza Strip, leaving Hamas and other armed factions confined to the remaining 36% behind these militarized buffer zones. 

This territorial division has become the backdrop for a profound diplomatic stalemate. The transition to Phase II of the U.S.-brokered peace plan, endorsed by United Nations Security Council Resolution 2803 in November 2025, has ground to a complete halt. At the center of the gridlock is a fundamental dispute over disarmament. The U.S.-backed Board of Peace (BoP)—a transitional international administrative body chaired by President Donald Trump—has insisted that Hamas undergo verified decommissioning of its heavy weapons, rockets, and light arms as a non-negotiable prerequisite for reconstruction and IDF withdrawal. Hamas has flatly rejected these terms, refusing to surrender its weapons unless Israel implements a total and permanent cessation of military operations, withdraws its forces entirely, and permits the unrestricted flow of humanitarian aid.

On the ground, the institutions tasked with managing this transition are struggling to function. The Palestinian National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), established in January 2026 as an apolitical governing body, has found itself structurally paralyzed. Led by Chief Commissioner Dr. Ali Shaath, a civil engineer with a PhD from Queen's University Belfast, the NCAG is caught between Israeli administrative restrictions and Hamas’s lingering civil authority. While Dr. Shaath’s cabinet of fifteen technocrats was meant to restore essential water, electricity, and health services, the committee has faced immense administrative delays from Israeli authorities at the crossings, while Hamas continues to collect local taxes and exert de facto control over civil servants in the areas behind the Yellow Line.

Simultaneously, the International Stabilization Force (ISF), authorized by the UN to monitor the ceasefire and train a reformed local police force, remains largely a paper army. While a force of 20,000 personnel was envisioned, actual deployment has been slow. Indonesia has agreed to act as deputy commander, and countries including Morocco, Albania, Greece, Egypt, Kosovo, and Kazakhstan have committed delegations or troops. However, contributing nations remain deeply hesitant to deploy their personnel into active urban environments without a clear status-of-forces agreement, fearing they will be drawn into active "peace enforcement" operations against Hamas factions rather than traditional peacekeeping.

To bypass this deadlock, Nikolay Mladenov, the High Representative for Gaza and Director-General of the Board of Peace, introduced a new 15-point roadmap in May 2026. Mladenov's proposal attempts to bridge the gap between Phase I and Phase II by linking key deliverables to a strict schedule of "reciprocity and verification." Under this framework, specific steps—such as opening border crossings and delivering fuel—are directly tied to parallel security milestones from both sides. Mladenov has toured Cairo, Jerusalem, and Ankara, warning that the lack of progress risks turning a temporary ceasefire into a permanent partition of the Strip. Factional leaders are currently reviewing Mladenov's proposed frameworks, with a joint delegation expected to travel to Cairo to deliver a formal response.

Yet, as diplomatic talks drag on, the financial and legal foundations of the Board of Peace have come under intense scrutiny. Although member states pledged up to $17 billion for reconstruction during the Board’s inaugural meeting in February 2026, the official World Bank trust fund established to receive these donations remains at exactly zero dollars. Instead, the Board of Peace directed donor nations to deposit their contributions into a private JPMorgan Chase account under the Board’s direct control. 

This financial detour has sparked allegations of a "pay-to-play" grift from international observers and political commentators. Critics point out that the JPMorgan Chase account lacks the independent oversight and public reporting mandates required by multilateral institutions like the World Bank. According to internal documents, the limited funds that have entered the private account—mainly from the United Arab Emirates and Morocco—have been utilized primarily to pay the administrative salaries of the NCAG and Mladenov's offices, while broader reconstruction funds remain frozen. 

Adding to the controversy is the prominent role of Jared Kushner, who serves as a key member of the Board of Peace’s seven-member executive board. At the World Economic Forum in Davos, Kushner unveiled a $30 billion "New Gaza" master plan, envisioning the Strip’s transformation into an industrial and tourism hub complete with skyscrapers, seaside resorts, and data centers. Critics point out that Kushner’s private equity firm, Affinity Partners, manages billions of dollars in sovereign wealth from Gulf states, raising deep questions from observers about whether the reconstruction of Gaza is being positioned as a commercial redevelopment opportunity for well-connected private entities.

These concerns have been exacerbated by draft documents circulated by the Board of Peace in June 2026. The drafts reveal that the Board is seeking sweeping legal immunity for its personnel, military affiliates, and private contractors, shielding them from arrest, detention, or legal proceedings within Gaza. Furthermore, the documents demand the right to acquire public land in Gaza "free of charge" for redevelopment purposes. Simultaneously, reports indicate that the Trump administration has begun exploring options to reclassify the Board of Peace from a "public international organization" to an "international nongovernmental organization" (INGO)—a maneuver that critics argue is designed to strip away public audit requirements and insulate the Board from international accountability.

Meanwhile, the daily reality in Gaza is one of rapid decay. Skirmishes along the Orange and Yellow lines occur almost daily, with Israeli forces conducting targeted strikes against suspected rocket launch sites and Hamas fighters launching sporadic sniper and mortar fire. The ceasefire is increasingly fragile, and humanitarian access is severely restricted by the shifting military lines. As the UN Security Council debates the geopolitical implications of the Board of Peace’s parallel authority, the civilians of Gaza remain trapped in a no-man's-land, caught between the concrete blocks of the IDF’s expanding buffer zones and the political maneuvers of a transitional board operating in the shadows of international finance.
