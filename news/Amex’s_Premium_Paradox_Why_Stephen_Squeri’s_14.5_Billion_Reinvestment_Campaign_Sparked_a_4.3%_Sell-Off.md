# Amex’s Premium Paradox: Why Stephen Squeri’s $14.5 Billion Reinvestment Campaign Sparked a 4.3% Sell-Off

On Friday, July 24, 2026, American Express Company (NYSE: AXP) presented a classic Wall Street conundrum: a clean bottom-line beat coupled with a sharp valuation correction. Shares of the New York-based credit giant plummeted 4.3% in the session, closing at $326.17 after gapping down from a previous close of $340.84. The market’s disappointment came despite American Express reporting second-quarter diluted earnings per share (EPS) of $4.53, handily outpacing the consensus analyst estimate of $4.40. Net income for the quarter rose 8% year-over-year to $3.11 billion, up from $2.88 billion in the prior-year period. Total revenue, net of interest expense, climbed 10% to $19.64 billion, yet it fell marginally short of the $19.69 billion Wall Street consensus. The headline numbers, however, masked the real battleground: a high-stakes strategic choice by Chairman and CEO Stephen Squeri and CFO Christophe Le Caillec to aggressively reinvest the company's operational outperformance rather than raising bottom-line guidance for the full year.

Management raised its full-year 2026 revenue growth guidance to 10%, an upgrade from the 9% to 10% range previously forecast. Yet, they kept full-year EPS guidance flat at $17.30 to $17.90. Instead of flowing the operational windfall down to the bottom line, American Express chose to pump it back into customer acquisition, technology, and marketing. "We can either drop the overperformance to the bottom line and buy back more shares or we can invest to grow the business further. We've chosen the latter," Squeri stated during the morning's earnings call. This choice was reflected in a 12% surge in total consolidated expenses to $14.5 billion, which outpaced the 10% revenue growth. Marketing expenses rose 6% year-over-year in the second quarter, and management announced plans to boost marketing spend by 10% in the second half of 2026 compared to the same period in 2025.

The strategy sparked immediate, polarized debates across financial communities. On Fintwit, bearish commentators focused on the negative operating leverage. Popular trading account @CapitalFlows posted: "AXP EPS beat but no guidance raise. Squeri decided to reinvest the outperformance instead. A classic beat and fade." Another user, @MacroCredit, noted: "Consolidated expenses growing at 12% while revenue grows at 10% is the definition of negative operating leverage. Amex is running faster just to stay in the same place." Conversely, on Reddit’s r/investing, long-term bulls defended the reinvestment. User u/PremiumCompounder wrote: "This is a long-term compounder at its finest. Squeri knows what he’s doing. Reinvesting today means stronger revenue next year. I am buying the dip." On r/stocks, user u/OptionSqueeze lamented the guidance freeze: "The market wanted a raise to the EPS guidance. When they kept it at $17.30-$17.90 despite the Q2 beat, the options got crushed. Total value trap today."

This internal tension between short-term margin expansion and long-term customer acquisition comes against a broader economic backdrop where premium consumer spending remains remarkably insulated. While lower-income cohorts struggle with high-interest rates and elevated Treasury yields under Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent’s fiscal regime, Amex’s core affluent cardmember base continues to spend. Card member spending (billed business) grew 9.4% on an FX-adjusted basis to $455.8 billion in the second quarter—the fastest pace of growth in three years—helping push total network volume to $516.8 billion. Travel and Entertainment (T&E) spending rose 10%, while retail spending jumped 13%. This robust spending suggests that the premium consumer has not yet succumbed to the high-rate environment maintained by Jerome Powell’s Federal Reserve.

Credit quality further underscored this premium resilience. Consolidated provisions for credit losses fell to $1.1 billion, down from $1.4 billion in the second quarter of 2025. This reduction was aided by a $191 million credit reserve release, in contrast to the reserve builds seen across regional banks and larger peers. The net write-off rate remained stable at 2.0%, well below pre-pandemic levels. The positive credit picture allowed Amex to release capital, which it then funneled into growth initiatives, including its proposed $700 million all-cash acquisition of European dining platform TheFork from Tripadvisor, designed to expand Resy’s global footprint by adding 75,000 bookable venues.

The reinvestment strategy is also designed to absorb the near-term structural headwinds of portfolio pruning. Throughout 2026, American Express has transitioned out of low-margin co-brand relationships. The transfer of the Lowe's small-business co-brand portfolio to Synchrony was completed in April 2026, and the Amazon small-business co-brand portfolio is scheduled to transfer to U.S. Bank on August 14, 2026. Management disclosed that these exits represent a temporary 1 percentage point headwind to billing growth and a 2.5 percentage point headwind to net interest income (NII) growth. By aggressively reinvesting in its core proprietary offerings—particularly the premium U.S. Platinum Card, which was recently refreshed—Amex is seeking to offset these losses. The focus remains on younger demographics: Millennials and Gen Z accounted for 65% of new consumer accounts acquired during the quarter. Squeri’s bet is clear: sacrifice short-term margin expansion to lock in premium lifetime value, even if it means weathering a 4.3% stock decline today.
