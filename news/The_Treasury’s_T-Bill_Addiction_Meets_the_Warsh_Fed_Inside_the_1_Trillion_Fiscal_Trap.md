# The Treasury’s T-Bill Addiction Meets the Warsh Fed: Inside the $1 Trillion Fiscal Trap

On July 21, 2026, U.S. Treasury yields rose across the board, with the benchmark 10-year Treasury yield hitting 4.64%. The spike represents a sharp reversal from the closing low of 3.96% recorded in late February and has reignited a fierce debate on Wall Street, Washington, and online forums like r/economics and r/investing. At the center of the storm is the federal government’s ballooning debt pile, which reached $39.58 trillion as of July 17, 2026, and a highly controversial financing strategy: the heavy reliance on short-term Treasury bills (T-bills) to fund the growing budget deficit.

The strategic shift toward the short end of the yield curve began under the previous administration and has persisted under Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, who was sworn in on January 28, 2025. In November 2020, the Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee (TBAC) recommended that the Treasury maintain the share of T-bills within a range of 15% to 20% of total outstanding marketable debt. However, by mid-2026, the actual share of T-bills had climbed to 21.12%, translating to $6.69 trillion of the $31.68 trillion in outstanding marketable U.S. debt. 

Critics have labeled this aggressive issuance of short-term debt "Activist Treasury Issuance" (ATI), a concept popularized by economists Nouriel Roubini and Stephen Miran. The core argument is that by flooding the market with short-term T-bills while reducing the issuance of longer-dated bonds, the Treasury has artificially depressed long-term yields. This behaves as a form of "stealth quantitative easing (QE)," keeping mortgage rates and corporate borrowing costs lower than they otherwise would be in a tight monetary environment. 

But this strategy has created a massive rollover risk. Because T-bills mature in one year or less, the Treasury is forced to constantly refinance trillions of dollars in debt. With the Federal Reserve keeping its benchmark rate elevated, this debt is being rolled over at historically high costs. The average interest rate on the total marketable U.S. debt has climbed to 3.411% as of June 2026. If yields remain elevated, the fiscal consequences are stark. 

According to the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), net interest costs on the national debt totaled $970 billion in fiscal year 2025. For fiscal year 2026, net interest payments are projected to surpass $1.0 trillion, consuming roughly 17.8% of the projected $5.6 trillion in federal revenues. This milestone means the U.S. government now spends more on servicing its debt than it does on national defense, which stood at $893 billion in fiscal year 2025. 

On Reddit’s r/economics, users are increasingly calling out the risks of this strategy. One popular post noted, "We are basically funding a long-term structural deficit with short-term credit card debt by leaning so heavily on T-bills. If rates don't fall fast, the rollover is going to eat the federal budget alive." Another user on r/investing warned, "The Fed will have to monetize it. There is no other way. The moment long-term yields spike high enough to cause a crisis, the Fed will pivot back to QE, inflation be damned."

This creates an acute policy conflict between the Treasury and the Federal Reserve, led by Kevin Warsh, who was sworn in as the 17th Fed Chair on May 22, 2026. While the Fed has sought to restrict demand to curb inflation, the Treasury’s issuance strategy has effectively acted as a backdoor liquidity injection. Now, the bond market is demanding a higher term premium. 

In a recent appearance on the *Master Investor Podcast*, JPMorgan Chase CEO Jamie Dimon warned that investors should avoid long-term U.S. Treasuries at current levels. Dimon pointed to the chronic deficits and the growing debt burden, arguing that "bond vigilantes"—investors who sell government bonds to demand higher yields in response to fiscal mismanagement—will eventually force interest rates higher. Dimon predicted that even if inflation returns to the Fed's 2% target, the 10-year yield should realistically remain in the 4.0% to 4.5% range.

Treasury Secretary Bessent has championed a "3-3-3" economic plan, aiming to reduce the budget deficit to 3% of GDP, achieve 3% real GDP growth, and increase domestic oil production by 3 million barrels per day by 2028. However, the CBO projects the fiscal year 2026 deficit to hit $1.853 trillion, or roughly 5.8% of GDP. To fund this gap, the Treasury is increasingly turning to private markets. Bessent has sought to tap new sources of demand, including proposing regulations that encourage stablecoin issuers—who hold significant reserves of T-bills—to expand their holdings.

The ultimate confrontation lies ahead. If bond vigilantes continue to dump long-term debt, driving the 10-year yield well past 4.64%, the Treasury’s interest expense will spiral further. Fed Chair Warsh will face an impossible choice: allow the government’s interest bill to crowd out federal spending and trigger a fiscal crisis, or step in with emergency buying, undermining the Fed's independence and reigniting inflation.
