# Strait of Hormuz Plunges Into Indefinite Warfare as Trump Reinstates Blockade and Tehran Threatens "Eye for an Eye" Infrastructure Retaliation

On the night of July 22, 2026, the skies over the southern coastline of Iran erupted in flames for the twelfth consecutive night. Precision-guided munitions launched from U.S. Navy F/A-18 Super Hornets and B-1B Lancer bombers struck coastal surveillance radars, missile silos, drone launch sites, and command infrastructure belonging to the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps (IRGC). According to statements from U.S. Central Command (CENTCOM), this sustained air campaign represents the most aggressive air operation in the Persian Gulf in decades, aimed at degrading Iran’s capability to choke off international shipping. Yet, despite the nightly bombardment, the conflict has entered a dangerous and highly volatile limbo, threatening to drag the global economy into a prolonged energy crisis.

The current escalation marks the absolute collapse of the brief diplomatic stabilization achieved in early summer. The ceasefire, brokered under the administration of President Donald Trump, had culminated in the signing of the Islamabad Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) on June 17, 2026, between President Trump and Iranian President Masoud Pezeshkian. This brief truce, which temporarily reopened the shipping lanes and halted hostilities, was short-lived. The agreement collapsed on June 20, 2026, when Iran abruptly declared the Strait of Hormuz closed once again, citing ongoing Israeli strikes in Lebanon as a violation of the agreement. By July 8, 2026, the truce was officially dead, replaced by open kinetic warfare as Iran resumed swarm attacks on commercial tankers and direct missile strikes on regional U.S. military installations.

In response to the collapse, President Donald Trump announced a dramatic shift in American deterrence strategy, labeled by analysts as the "Infrastructure Deterrence Doctrine." Trump issued an ultimatum warning that any further Iranian attack on commercial shipping would prompt the U.S. to target Iranian civil infrastructure directly, including bridges, power plants, and electrical grids in the heart of Tehran. Intelligence assessments indicate that while the airstrikes have degraded tactical assets, the overall campaign remains in an indefinite limbo, failing to establish long-term deterrence as Iran continues its asymmetrical operations.

Iran’s response was immediate and defiant. Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf announced on the social media platform X that "the equation of this war is clear: either all or none." Ghalibaf warned that if Iran is prevented from selling its own oil due to the U.S. naval blockade reinstated on July 14, no country in the region will be permitted to export crude, and that "no regional infrastructure will remain safe." Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi echoed these sentiments, writing on X that Iran's defense doctrine is governed by the principle of "an eye for an eye," warning that any strike on Iranian soil would result in immediate retaliatory strikes against U.S. bases in Kuwait, Bahrain, and Jordan, as well as the infrastructure of any allied nations hosting them.

This threat is not hollow. Over the last two weeks, the IRGC has launched intensive drone and ballistic missile salvos against U.S. military bases throughout the Middle East. The strikes have resulted in significant U.S. casualties, including a devastating strike on the Muwaffaq Salti Air Base and Al-Azraq Base in Jordan. In Bahrain, the Sheikh Isa Air Base and Salman Port facilities were hit, disrupting aircraft maintenance workshops and logistics. At Camp Arifjan in Kuwait, Marine Special Forces sheds and communications infrastructure at Ali Al-Salem Air Base were damaged in precision drone attacks. To date, at least 18 U.S. service members have been killed in this campaign, forcing the European Union Aviation Safety Agency (EASA) to issue emergency warnings advising commercial airlines to avoid the airspace over Jordan, Iraq, Kuwait, and Bahrain.

The military standoff has completely paralyzed the world's most critical energy chokepoint. The Strait of Hormuz, which typically handles 20 percent of the world’s petroleum liquids and 30 percent of global liquefied natural gas (LNG) transits, is effectively closed to commercial traffic. While the Pentagon maintains that the waterway remains legally open, data from Lloyd’s List Intelligence reveals a catastrophic decline in shipping activity. Non-Iran-linked vessel transits through the strait have plummeted by 90 percent year-on-year. During the week of July 13 to July 19, only 25 non-Iranian ships successfully transited the waterway, down from a weekly average of over 100. Inbound tankers have evaporated, with only two non-Iran-linked Very Large Crude Carriers (VLCCs) entering the Persian Gulf during that week. 

To survive this operational environment, the few shipowners willing to brave the passage have resorted to desperate measures. Nearly 70 percent of all commercial tankers transiting the area are now operating "dark," turning off their Automatic Identification System (AIS) transponders to evade Iranian tracking. Oman has suspended its standard Traffic Separation Scheme, implementing a temporary, highly restricted convoy system that has done little to restore market confidence.

As a result, global energy markets are in turmoil. Brent crude oil prices breached $96 a barrel—a six-week high—reversing the stability achieved during the brief June ceasefire and sparking panic across Wall Street. Traders on online forums like r/wallstreetbets have highlighted the disconnect between paper derivatives and the physical reality of a blocked strait. Some exporters have attempted to bypass the blockade by rerouting crude to the Yanbu terminal on the Red Sea, but that alternative has also been squeezed by parallel maritime threats from Houthi forces in Yemen. With U.S. naval blockades firmly in place and Iran digging in, the conflict remains locked in a dangerous stalemate with no clear diplomatic exit in sight.
