# The 22.5% Truce: Inside the Diplomatic Impasse and Corridor Politics Stalling Gaza’s Phase II Ceasefire

Across the scorched perimeter of southern Gaza, the tangible dividend of international diplomacy currently measures exactly 600 commercial and humanitarian relief trucks passing daily through the Kerem Shalom and Rafah cargo terminals. The convoys cross into territory bounded by what the Israeli Defense Forces (IDF) officially designate as the “Yellow Line”—a fortified demarcation demarcating the tactical pullback negotiated under Phase I of the ceasefire framework. While this initial stabilization paused active aerial bombardments, enabled preliminary exchanges of captives for Palestinian detainees, and allowed municipal crews to reconnect primary water transmission lines, negotiations aimed at executing Phase II have ground to an absolute halt.

The impasse reflects a structural deadlock between two irreconcilable political and military doctrines. In Jerusalem, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s security cabinet maintains that Phase II cannot commence without the total, verifiable demilitarization of Hamas, the destruction of its underground tunnel network, and sustained Israeli military control over two strategic corridors: the 14-kilometer Philadelphi Corridor running along the Egypt-Gaza border, and the 6.5-kilometer Netzarim Corridor that bifurcates northern Gaza from the south. Addressing domestic and international skepticism regarding the strategic value of the southern buffer zone, Netanyahu stated bluntly: “Three out of our four war objectives pass through one place—the Philadelphi Corridor. It is Hamas’s oxygen pipeline and smuggling route.” In public declarations, the prime minister affirmed that Israel will not withdraw its garrisons from either axis under current conditions.

In Cairo and Doha, Hamas’s negotiating delegation, led by newly appointed Political Bureau chief Khalil al-Hayya, has rejected disarmament out of hand. Hamas’s counter-position requires a legally binding, internationally guaranteed end to the war, a comprehensive IDF withdrawal behind the 1967 borders—including the immediate evacuation of both Philadelphi and Netzarim—and an irrevocable roadmap toward sovereign Palestinian statehood before any heavy armaments are surrendered or placed under third-party escrow. Outlining the group's baseline, al-Hayya declared: “We announce today that we have reached an agreement to end the war and aggression against our people, to begin implementing a permanent ceasefire, the withdrawal of occupation forces, the entry of aid, the opening of the Rafah crossing in both directions.” For Hamas, disarming while Israeli troops hold interior buffer zones represents unconditional capitulation; for Israel, an unconditioned withdrawal guarantees the political survival and rearmament of the militant group.

Faced with this binary contradiction, mediators from Egypt’s General Intelligence Directorate, Qatari Prime Minister Sheikh Mohammed bin Abdulrahman Al Thani, and officials representing U.S. President Donald Trump’s administration have attempted to construct an intermediary governing mechanism. Their blueprint focuses on standing up the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG), a 15-member non-partisan technocratic body tasked with civil administration, emergency healthcare, utilities, and cross-border aid distribution. Mediators agreed to appoint Dr. Ali Abdel Hamid Shaath, a Khan Younis-born civil engineer and former Palestinian Authority deputy minister who previously directed the Palestinian Industrial Estates and Free Zones Authority (PIEFZA), to head the committee. Endorsed jointly by Egypt, Qatar, and Turkey, the technocratic model was engineered to decouple municipal operations from Hamas’s political wing without requiring direct Israeli military government.

The technocratic mechanism, however, lacks operational security enforcement. A parallel diplomatic proposal to deploy an International Stabilization Force (ISF)—recruiting security detachments from regional Arab states including Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, and Jordan—has stalled due to rigorous legal and political preconditions set by Arab capitals. Arab governments have explicitly conditioned any military commitment on two non-negotiable requirements: a formal mandate authorized under Chapter VII of the United Nations Security Council, and an official invitation from President Mahmoud Abbas’s Palestinian Authority in Ramallah. 

Regional officials maintain that without these criteria, foreign Arab troops would be perceived domestically and locally as enforcing an Israeli military occupation. Saudi Foreign Minister Prince Faisal bin Farhan confirmed this position, noting that international troop deployments are viable only “under a UN mandate to support the Palestinian Authority after the war.” The United Arab Emirates similarly conditioned participation on an irreversible framework leading to a two-state solution. Because Israel rejects any post-war governance role for the Palestinian Authority in Gaza and opposes a binding UN mandate that would restrict IDF unilateral operational latitude, troop-contributing nations have refused to mobilize personnel.

These international frictions are further amplified by domestic political volatility inside Israel. In Tel Aviv, tens of thousands of demonstrators mobilized weekly by the Hostages and Missing Families Forum have surrounded government complexes, demanding an immediate Phase II deal to secure the recovery of the remaining 48 living hostages confirmed to be in captivity. Family representatives have warned that conditioning hostage exchanges on long-term territorial control over border corridors endangers the lives of those still surviving underground. 

Simultaneously, Netanyahu faces an acute coalition crisis. National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir of the Otzma Yehudit party and Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich of Religious Zionism command a combined 14 Knesset seats, representing the indispensable legislative bloc maintaining Netanyahu’s 64-seat majority. Both ministers have publicly threatened to dissolve the government if Phase II permanently ends the war without the absolute dismantling of Hamas. Following security cabinet briefings on mediator frameworks, Smotrich denounced pauses in military operations as a “grave mistake,” while Ben-Gvir reiterated that his party would resign and topple the governing coalition should the military withdraw from the Philadelphi axis or Netzarim corridor without eliminating Hamas's governing authority.

The combination of sequencing deadlocks, coalition vulnerability, and regional peacekeeping conditions has driven institutional expectations to historic lows. On the decentralized prediction market Polymarket, trading volume on the contract tracking whether an Israel-Hamas Phase II ceasefire will be finalized before December 31 dropped from a summer high of 78% down to 22.5%. Across social platforms and political forums, market analysts and regional observers have focused on the irreconcilable domestic constraints facing both leaderships. One prominent analyst on X observed that “the Philadelphi Corridor is no longer just a military buffer; it has become the domestic linchpin of the Israeli coalition, while disarmament without statehood is an existential impossibility for Hamas.” 

With the 600 daily trucks operating merely as a stabilization stopgap and the temporary provisions of Phase I rapidly exhausting their negotiated timelines, the diplomatic track remains pinned between competing redlines. Without a structural compromise on territorial corridors and international stabilization mandates, the transition to Phase II remains stalled beneath the threshold of political reality.
