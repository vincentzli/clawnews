# The $179 Billion Conservatorship Trap: Inside the Quiet Civil War Over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac’s Privatization

For nearly eighteen years, the Federal National Mortgage Association and the Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation—known universally as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac—have operated under the administrative purgatory of a federal conservatorship. What began as an emergency stabilization measure during the subprime mortgage meltdown of September 2008 has evolved into the longest-running government intervention in modern financial history. Together, the two Government-Sponsored Enterprises (GSEs) back roughly half of the $12 trillion U.S. residential mortgage market. Yet, as of mid-2026, the path toward returning these entities to private hands remains blocked by a complex web of regulatory capital rules, political maneuvering in Congress, and the structural overhang of the federal government’s equity warrants.

The latest battle over the future of the GSEs was joined on June 25, 2026, when Representative Scott Fitzgerald (R-WI) introduced H.R. 9460, titled the "Sustainable Homeownership Act." Part of a broader three-bill legislative package that includes the Working Families Home Construction Act and the Home Affordability Through Mortgage Simplification Act, Fitzgerald’s bill seeks to force the hand of both the regulator and the executive branch. Rather than allowing the Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) to unilaterally dictate the terms of an exit, H.R. 9460 proposes a statutory framework to dismantle the conservatorship, establishing strict congressional guardrails on the post-exit entities. Among its key provisions, the bill bans the use of "piggyback mortgages"—secondary loans taken out alongside a primary mortgage for the same home purchase, which performed disastrously in the 2008 crash— and ties future conforming loan limit increases to median household income rather than home-price appreciation.

The legislative push from Capitol Hill stands in sharp contrast to the slow-walking observed within the administration. When William "Bill" Pulte was confirmed as the Director of the FHFA in March 2025, market analysts and housing finance professionals anticipated an aggressive, rapid push toward privatization. Pulte did not hesitate to shake up the GSEs, naming himself chairman of the boards of both Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, initiating investigations into mortgage fraud, firing entrenched staff, and terminating special purpose credit programs. By late 2025, Pulte was publicly floating a plan to execute a limited initial public offering (IPO) of up to 5% of the companies' shares while they remained in conservatorship, testing the waters for a full market exit.

However, that momentum has stalled. President Donald Trump has publicly adopted a cautious stance, stating that while a GSE IPO is "on the table," there is "no rush" to execute it. This deliberate pace has been further compounded by Pulte's own expanding portfolio. In June 2026, President Trump appointed Pulte to serve as the Acting Director of National Intelligence (DNI), a dual-hatted role that requires him to oversee the nation's intelligence apparatus while simultaneously running the FHFA and chairing the boards of the GSEs. Market analysts and housing finance experts have expressed deep skepticism that Pulte can manage the demands of the intelligence community while orchestrating the largest financial restructuring in history. 

This administrative bottleneck has frustrated retail investors who have long traded the over-the-counter shares of Fannie Mae (FNMA) and Freddie Mac (FMCC). On retail trading forums like Reddit’s r/wallstreetbets, where the GSEs are frequently referred to as the "Toxic Twins," the sentiment is highly polarized. While some speculative bulls continue to hold out hope for a release, pointing to the administration's administrative powers as the keys to unlock billions in value, others are far more cynical, noting the severe dilution risk. Prominent investors like Bill Ackman have historically viewed the GSE transition as a "significant, asymmetric investment opportunity," yet the primary structural barrier to common shareholders remains the senior preferred stock purchase agreements (SPSPAs) signed with the U.S. Treasury during the financial crisis. Under these agreements, the Treasury holds common stock warrants to purchase 79.9% of each enterprise's common stock at a nominal exercise price of $0.00001 per share. If the Treasury exercises these warrants to recoup its taxpayer backing, the holdings of current common shareholders will be diluted to near-insignificance. 

Beyond the dilution risk lies the formidable challenge of meeting regulatory capital requirements. Under the FHFA's Enterprise Regulatory Capital Framework (ERCF), Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac must build hundreds of billions of dollars in capital reserves to protect against future defaults before they can safely exit conservatorship. Milliman estimates the single-family capital requirement alone stands at approximately $199 billion, while historical FHFA projections suggest the total combined risk-based requirements and buffers exceed $220 billion. As of the end of 2025, the combined net worth of the two GSEs reached approximately $179.4 billion. While this represents a significant increase from prior years, the GSEs are still short of the full regulatory capital required to be released, and the senior preferred stock held by the Treasury does not qualify as core capital due to its dividend structure. 

The debate over the government's role in the secondary mortgage market remains a central point of contention. Bipartisan voices in Congress, led by House Financial Services Committee Chairman French Hill (R-AR), have warned against unilateral administrative action. Chairman Hill has consistently maintained that any exit from conservatorship must be legislated by Congress rather than driven by executive fiat. Hill has argued that the GSEs are still undercapitalized and that Congress must first define their future mission, oversight, and corporate governance. In congressional hearings, Hill has highlighted the danger of "mission creep" and the risk to taxpayers, while also acknowledging the critical role the GSEs play in providing liquidity to the mortgage market, which helps maintain lower mortgage rates.

Without an explicit government guarantee, a fully privatized Fannie and Freddie would face significantly higher borrowing costs, which would inevitably be passed along to homebuyers in the form of higher mortgage interest rates. Industry groups like U.S. Mortgage Insurers (USMI) have supported Fitzgerald’s legislative efforts, arguing that private capital, such as private mortgage insurance, should absorb losses ahead of taxpayers. Yet, the core dilemma remains unresolved: how to withdraw the federal government from the mortgage market without triggering a spike in housing costs for American families. 

The FHFA's decision to increase the 2026 multifamily loan purchase caps to $88 billion per enterprise—a combined total of $176 billion, representing a 20.5% increase from 2025's caps of $73 billion per enterprise—underscores the scale of their ongoing market footprint. As Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac continue to expand their buying power under the direction of a dual-hatted FHFA chief, the tension between statutory reform and administrative inertia will only intensify. With H.R. 9460 now sitting before the House Financial Services Committee, the legislative and executive branches are locked in a standoff over who will ultimately dictate the end of the conservatorship, and under what terms.
