# The Post-START Vacuum: The Collapse of Nuclear Verification, the Triad’s Modernization Crisis, and Moscow’s Recalibrated Redlines

On February 5, 2026, the final legal bulwark governing the world’s two largest nuclear arsenals formally lapsed. The New Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty (New START), signed in Prague in 2010 and extended for five years in 2021, reached its statutory termination date without an extension, a successor framework, or an interim political accord. For the first time since the entry into force of the Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT I) and the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty in 1972, the United States and the Russian Federation are operating their strategic forces without legally binding caps or mandatory mutual monitoring mechanisms.

The collapse of the bilateral framework removes the structural ceilings that capped each nation at 1,550 deployed strategic warheads, 700 deployed delivery vehicles—intercontinental ballistic missiles (ICBMs), submarine-launched ballistic missiles (SLBMs), and heavy bombers—and 800 deployed and non-deployed launchers. Beyond the numerical ceilings, the expiration eliminates the verification infrastructure designed to provide strategic predictability. Between February 2011 and its gradual disintegration, the treaty supported 328 on-site inspections—encompassing Type One inspections of operational bases and Type Two inspections of maintenance and storage facilities—alongside semi-annual comprehensive data exchanges and more than 25,000 mandatory notifications tracking missile movements, silo maintenance, and test launches.

That verification machinery is now entirely defunct. The Bilateral Consultative Commission (BCC) in Geneva has been inoperative since Moscow suspended a scheduled session in November 2022. The semi-annual data exchanges ceased in 2023 following Russia’s formal suspension of the agreement and Washington’s reciprocal countermeasures, which halted telemetry sharing, revoked inspector accreditation, and discontinued location notifications. When Moscow proposed in late 2025 that both sides continue observing the 1,550-warhead ceiling on an informal, voluntary basis for one calendar year, the United States declined to formalize the arrangement.

Speaking in Beijing as the treaty expired, Russian Deputy Foreign Minister Sergei Ryabkov stated: "This is a new moment, a new reality – we are ready for it. The lack of an answer is also an answer." Ryabkov noted that Moscow would not "dramatize" the expiration, while reiterating that structured negotiations on strategic stability remain unfeasible as long as Washington maintains what the Kremlin characterizes as a "hostile course" aimed at Russia’s strategic defeat. In Washington, President Donald Trump reiterated his administration’s opposition to bilateral arrangements that exclude third-party arsenals, characterizing New START as an outdated framework and asserting that future nuclear diplomacy must incorporate the People’s Republic of China and address theater-range tactical nuclear warheads.

This absence of arms control constraints coincides with a formal lowering of Russia’s threshold for nuclear employment. On November 19, 2024, President Vladimir Putin signed Presidential Decree No. 991, approving the revised "Fundamentals of State Policy of the Russian Federation in the Area of Nuclear Deterrence," superseding the prior 2020 doctrine codified in Decree No. 355.

The revised text alters the threshold governing conventional conflict. Under the 2020 doctrine, nuclear retaliation against conventional aggression was restricted to scenarios where "the very existence of the state is under threat." Under Decree No. 991, Paragraph 18 broadens that mandate to encompass conventional aggression creating a "critical threat to the sovereignty and/or territorial integrity" of the Russian Federation or the Republic of Belarus as a constituent of the Union State. This legal standard replaces the objective criterion of existential state survival with a discretionary threshold based on threats to territorial integrity.

Decree No. 991 codifies two additional strategic parameters. Paragraph 11 establishes a joint-aggression doctrine: "Aggression against the Russian Federation and/or its allies by any non-nuclear state with the participation or support of a nuclear state is considered to be their joint attack." Designed to directly address Western ISR (intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance) support and standoff precision strikes by non-nuclear partners, the clause establishes a doctrinal predicate to treat non-nuclear conventional operations as direct actions by nuclear-armed sponsors.

Furthermore, Paragraph 19(b) formally lowers the operational trigger for launch-on-warning protocols. The doctrine authorizes nuclear weapons employment upon the "receipt of reliable data on a massive launch of aerospace attack weapons... and their crossing of the state border." The clause explicitly categorizes these aerospace systems as including "strategic and tactical aircraft, cruise missiles, unmanned aerial vehicles, hypersonic and other aircraft." In an operational theater where deep-strike drone salvos and cruise missiles are routinely deployed in conventional combat, codifying uncrewed aerial systems as a permissible trigger for strategic retaliation compresses command-and-control assessment cycles to minutes.

This convergence of lower doctrinal thresholds and an unmonitored nuclear operating environment has generated intense debate across the defense and arms control communities. On analytical platforms including Reddit’s r/geopolitics and r/armscontrol, defense analysts have pointed out the structural volatility of an unconstrained environment. "For the first time in fifty-four years, the world’s two largest nuclear arsenals have zero mutual verification guardrails," observed one widely circulated analytical assessment. "The acute risk is not an unprompted first strike, but the institutionalization of worst-case assumptions. Without daily telemetry, notifications, and base inspections, routine maintenance, dispersal drills, and sensor anomalies will be fed into predictive targeting algorithms. Both powers are flying blind into a multipolar dynamic." On X, nonproliferation specialists echoed these warnings, emphasizing that satellite remote sensing cannot replicate on-site warhead counting, forcing both defense establishments to calculate force balances based on maximum upload assumptions.

In Washington, this operational ambiguity coincides with acute fiscal and industrial strain across the modernization programs of the U.S. nuclear triad, a recapitalization effort projected by the Congressional Budget Office to require over $750 billion.

The land-based modernization effort, the LGM-35A Sentinel ICBM program contracted to Northrop Grumman, triggered a critical Nunn-McCurdy breach in January 2024 when its program acquisition unit cost grew by 81 percent over baseline projections. Pentagon reviews completed in July 2024 elevated the program's acquisition estimate from $77.7 billion to $140.9 billion. The cost escalation stems from the civil engineering requirements of overhauling 450 launch facilities, 600 missile alert complexes, and installing over 7,500 miles of hardened underground fiber-optic and utility cabling across five states. Although the Department of Defense certified the restructured Sentinel program as indispensable to national security, the first test flight has slipped past 2027, with initial operational capability deferred past 2031. To bridge the gap, the Air Force must execute extensive life-extension initiatives on its legacy Minuteman III missiles, which have been deployed since 1970.

Concurrently, the sea-based deterrent—which accounts for the majority of the nation’s deployed strategic warheads—faces manufacturing bottlenecks. The Navy’s Columbia-class submarine program, contracted to General Dynamics Electric Boat, is experiencing a twelve- to sixteen-month delay on the lead submarine, the *District of Columbia* (SSBN-826). The delivery window has drifted toward late 2028 or 2029 due to skilled shipyard labor deficits and manufacturing delays in major structural modules, placing schedule pressure on the maintenance cycles of operational Ohio-class hulls.

These delays have intensified strategic debates over force structure. In June 2024, Pranay Vaddi, then serving as Senior Director for Arms Control, Disarmament, and Nonproliferation at the National Security Council, signaled the policy reassessment forced by unconstrained foreign arsenals: "Absent a change in adversary arsenals, we may reach a point in the coming years where an increase from current deployed numbers is required. We need to be fully prepared to execute if the president makes that decision." 

The bipartisan Strategic Posture Commission has similarly recommended establishing "upload hedges," advising the Department of Defense to retain the capacity to re-MIRV land-based ICBMs with multiple warheads and increase warhead configurations on Trident II D5 submarine-launched missiles. Yet with structural delays in naval shipyards and missile silos, alongside capacity constraints at the National Nuclear Security Administration's plutonium pit manufacturing facilities, the United States confronts an unconstrained strategic environment marked by lowered adversary redlines, an absent treaty regime, and a modernization timeline stretched to its industrial limits.
