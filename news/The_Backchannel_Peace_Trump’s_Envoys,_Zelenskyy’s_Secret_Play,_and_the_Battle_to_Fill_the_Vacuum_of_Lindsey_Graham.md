# The Backchannel Peace: Trump’s Envoys, Zelenskyy’s Secret Play, and the Battle to Fill the Vacuum of Lindsey Graham

Standing beneath the soaring dome of the Capitol Rotunda on July 28, 2026, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy paid his final respects to the late U.S. Senator Lindsey Graham. Graham, who passed away on July 11 at the age of 71 due to an aortic dissection shortly after returning from Kyiv, had been Ukraine's most vocal champion in the Republican Party. His death left a massive political vacuum in Washington and South Carolina. But for Zelenskyy, the trip to Washington was not merely a solemn obligation; it was the backdrop for a critical, high-stakes meeting at the White House with President Donald Trump.

This closed-door encounter marked the second face-to-face meeting between Trump and Zelenskyy in July. The two leaders had met earlier in the month on the sidelines of the NATO summit in Ankara, Turkey, which occurred on July 7–8, 2026. This subsequent White House meeting, lasting slightly over an hour, focused on security, diplomacy, and the local manufacturing of Patriot missile interceptors. Zelenskyy sought to implement a pledge Trump made at Ankara: granting Ukraine the licenses to produce Patriot interceptors domestically. Behind the scenes, representatives from the Pentagon, Ukrainian defense officials, and industry giants Raytheon and Lockheed Martin are already coordinating technology transfers and supply chains. While Kyiv hopes to begin assembly by the end of 2026 to resolve critical air-defense shortages, defense analysts warn that establishing full-scale precision manufacturing under constant bombardment will likely take several years.

Simultaneously, the Trump administration's broader peace track is shifting. Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff, serving as the President's special peace envoys, are preparing their first official trip to Kyiv within the next two weeks. For months, Kushner and Witkoff have operated a Moscow-heavy channel, conducting multi-hour meetings with Russian President Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin and trilateral talks in the United Arab Emirates. This private, deal-driven diplomacy had faced severe criticism from congressional hawks who argued that the envoys were preparing a plan that functioned as a Russian wish list. In truth, the administration's reliance on backchannel negotiators has intensified concerns among lawmakers about the potential exclusion of congressional oversight and the preservation of Ukrainian territorial integrity. To counter this criticism and balance their mediation, Kushner and Witkoff held a phone call with Zelenskyy on July 22, setting the stage for their upcoming Kyiv trip where they will present a revised peace framework.

The centerpiece of this revised framework is a proposed "air ceasefire," which would halt mutual missile and drone strikes. While Kremlin spokesperson Dmitry Peskov dismissed reports of the proposal as "press speculation" and "premature," he confirmed that communication channels between U.S. negotiators and Moscow remain open. For Ukraine, the proposal comes at a moment of unique tactical leverage. Throughout mid-to-late July 2026, Ukraine executed a systematic, long-range drone campaign targeting logistics centers owned by Wildberries, Russia’s largest online retailer. Alleging that the platform facilitates the supply of dual-use goods like drone navigation gear and body armor to the Russian military, Ukrainian drones struck at least 10 major Wildberries facilities, including Elektrostal in the Moscow region, Shushary in St. Petersburg, Tambov, and Yekaterinburg. The strikes damaged roughly 10% of Wildberries' total warehouse capacity, destroyed billions of rubles in inventory, and caused nine deaths, forcing the state-controlled bank VTB to begin preparing a massive financial bailout. The economic disruption was so severe that Wildberries removed its "Everything for the SMO" category from its website.

Yet, even as Trump’s envoys push their deal-driven peace track, Congress is mounting a fierce counter-offensive. On July 28, the very day of Graham’s funeral, the U.S. Senate voted 86–12 in a procedural vote to advance S. 5025, the "Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia Act of 2026." Co-authored by Senator Richard Blumenthal and Graham's sister, Darline Graham, who was appointed to her brother's vacant seat on July 13 by South Carolina Governor Henry McMaster, the bill is a direct challenge to the administration’s backchannel diplomacy. The legislation mandates primary and secondary sanctions on Russian officials, oligarchs, and the "shadow fleet" exporting Russian oil. It also authorizes the President to impose tariffs of up to 100% on major global purchasers of Russian crude oil and natural gas, with exemptions only for countries importing less than 15% of their natural gas from Russia.

The bill has sparked intense domestic debate. Proponents like Blumenthal argue S. 5025 is the only way to "bankrupt Putin's war machine" and force a dignified peace. Conversely, the National Taxpayers Union (NTU) has urged a "NO" vote, warning that the bill cedes excessive Article I, Section 8 taxing authority to the executive branch, especially given the administration’s recent threats of tariffs on Canadian imports.

Meanwhile, the battle for Graham’s seat is spilling into South Carolina’s local politics. Darline Graham, a former public administrator who served as Commissioner of the South Carolina Commission for the Blind, faces a crowded field of nine opponents in the August 11, 2026 special Republican primary. The winner of the Republican nomination will face Democratic candidate Dr. Annie Andrews in the November 3 general election. As the primary approaches, the legislative fate of S. 5025 and the diplomatic mission of Witkoff and Kushner remain locked in a tense struggle, raising the stakes for whether Washington will deliver a negotiated peace or a fortified defense.
