# The Quota Reckoning: Al Green’s Border Deaths Impeachment Confronts Jim Jordan’s Wall and Democratic Midterm Jitters

When Representative Al Green of Texas stood before the House chamber on September 3, 2026, he delivered an address unencumbered by the calculated restraint gripping the Democratic campaign committees. Ten days prior, on August 24, the Houston lawmaker had officially introduced House Resolution 1486, formally drafting articles of impeachment against President Donald Trump. While party strategists scrambled to keep the national spotlight fixed on prescription costs and consumer inflation ahead of the November 2026 midterm elections, Green used his morning hour floor time to reject their political calculus. Holding the administration accountable for lethal federal enforcement operations, Green insisted, was no longer a tactical debate: it was “a matter of life and death.”

H.Res. 1486 marks the eighth time in his congressional tenure that Green has sought to impeach Trump, yet the 119th Congress measure represents a marked shift from past broadsides. The five-page resolution does not center on presidential rhetoric or executive orders in the abstract. Instead, it levels detailed charges of high crimes and misdemeanors rooted in the operational directives governing U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) and U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP). The resolution alleges that executive mandates instituted rigid, unconstitutional apprehension quotas that pressured regional field offices into reckless urban enforcement actions, transformed civil immigration units into “quota-driven, unaccountable paramilitary police forces,” and orchestrated systematic executive cover-ups following the fatal shootings of four civilians: Renée Good, Alex Pretti, Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, and Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero.

The factual chronology detailed within H.Res. 1486 traces an escalating series of fatal encounters spanning three geographic regions over seven months. On January 7, 2026, during a high-visibility operation designated “Operation Metro Surge” on Portland Avenue South in Minneapolis, ICE officer Jonathan Ross discharged three bullets through the front windshield of an automobile operated by Renée Nicole Macklin Good. Good, a 37-year-old poet and mother of three who had just dropped her six-year-old child off at an elementary school, died at the scene. Although Department of Homeland Security spokespersons quickly claimed that Ross had acted in justifiable self-defense against a vehicular assault, nearby residential security footage and witness mobile recordings contradicted the agency account, showing Good’s car maneuvering away from the agent rather than accelerating toward him.

Seventeen days later, on January 24, federal weapons discharged again on the streets of Minneapolis. Alex Pretti, a 37-year-old intensive care nurse employed at the Minneapolis Veterans Affairs Medical Center, stopped along a public walkway to film CBP personnel conducting a street-level interdiction. Bystander footage showed Pretti attempting to shield and assist an elderly woman who had been knocked to the concrete sidewalk by an advancing officer. CBP officers Raymundo Gutierrez and Jesus Ochoa deployed chemical pepper spray directly into Pretti’s face before firing multiple service rounds into his chest. 

By midsummer, lethal incidents under the quota directive spread south and northeast. On July 7 in Houston, an ICE tactical team intercepted 52-year-old Lorenzo Salgado Araujo, an independent masonry contractor and father of three U.S.-born children who had resided in Texas for 35 years. While transporting his work crew to a job site, Salgado Araujo was shot through the driver-side window by an ICE agent who alleged the contractor attempted to use his pickup truck as a weapon against federal transport vans. Physical evidence gathered by county investigators established that Salgado Araujo’s vehicle was in reverse gear when struck, and a search confirmed the absence of any contraband, weapons, or narcotics. Less than a week later, on July 13 in Biddeford, Maine, ICE officer David Brouillette fatally shot 25-year-old Colombian national Johan Sebastián Durán Guerrero during an off-target perimeter stop. Agency administrative logs later surfaced by congressional staff confirmed that Guerrero was not the individual named in the underlying warrant and had no prior criminal record in the United States.

H.Res. 1486 alleges that these civilian deaths were neither isolated tactical failures nor rogue actions, but the foreseeable result of white-paper directives issued from the Executive Office of the President establishing weekly removal targets. The resolution asserts that senior administration officials engaged in affirmative obstruction of justice by withholding internal use-of-force reviews, placing regional supervisory records under executive privilege claims, and declining to supply subpoenaed dispatch logs to the House Homeland Security Committee. Through these actions, the resolution argues, the President violated his constitutional oath under Article II, Section 1, Clause 8, while trampling Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable seizures and Fifth Amendment guarantees of substantive due process.

The legislative measure was referred upon introduction to the House Committee on the Judiciary, where Chairman Jim Jordan and the Republican majority—maintaining a 220-to-213 seat advantage—have enacted a strict procedural blockade. Jordan has declined to refer H.Res. 1486 to any subcommittee, schedule markup sessions, or authorize staff depositions, denouncing the effort as an attempt to delegitimize lawful border enforcement. In public statements and floor exchanges, House Republican leaders have signaled that should Green invoke privileged resolution rules to force an immediate floor vote, the GOP will instantly move to table the measure without debate, counting on their seven-vote cushion to shut down the proceedings.

Yet the primary institutional friction caused by H.Res. 1486 reverberates inside the Democratic cloakroom. The resolution represents the second major challenge to executive authority brought by rank-and-file Democrats in 2026, following Representative John Larson’s April resolution seeking to impeach Trump and Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth over military strikes in Iran—a challenge that ultimately propelled passage of the bipartisan Iran War Powers Resolution (H.Con.Res.86) in June. But unlike Larson, who lost his primary contest later that summer, and Green himself, who is serving out his congressional tenure without reelection vulnerability, frontline Democrats face an unforgiving electoral landscape.

House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries has spent two years crafting an electoral strategy centered exclusively on economic stabilization, housing affordability, and healthcare costs, seeking the net gain of just four seats required to recapture the Speaker’s gavel. For the roughly two dozen Democratic incumbents defending competitive suburban and split-ticket districts in states like Pennsylvania, Michigan, and Arizona, a mandatory floor vote on impeaching Trump over federal border operations represents an acute political risk. Frontline campaign operatives warn that forced roll-call votes would dominate local media markets, mobilize conservative turnout, and alienate undecided independents who favor stringent border enforcement even while questioning agency excess. 

Constitutional scholars remain split over the fundamental jurisprudence anchoring Green’s articles. Conservative and institutionalist legal analysts contend that statutory and administrative torts committed by subordinate agency personnel cannot constitutionally sustain impeachment of the chief executive absent verified documentary evidence of a direct presidential order commanding unlawful lethal force. Conversely, civil liberties scholars argue that when an administration constructs systemic enforcement quotas while simultaneously preventing statutory oversight and shielding agents from standard investigative procedures, the resulting breakdown of constitutional governance constitutes a textbook high crime under Article II, Section 4.

Across social media platforms and legal forums, the resolution has exposed deep ideological fractures. Grassroots progressive organizers have taken to X to demand that Democratic leaders back Green’s resolution rather than voting to table it as they did with earlier efforts, warning that dismissing civilian killings for poll-driven caution betrays the party's fundamental values. Simultaneously, conservative commentators have characterized H.Res. 1486 as an act of desperation by a lame-duck lawmaker intended to inject chaos into federal law enforcement on the eve of the midterms. 

With exactly nine weeks remaining before voters head to the polls to decide the composition of the 120th Congress, Green has declined to withdraw his privileged notice. The stalemate leaves Capitol Hill caught in an acute conflict: an unyielding demand for constitutional accountability over four civilian deaths on one side, and the ruthless arithmetic of a four-seat congressional majority on the other.
