# The Asymmetry of War: How Ukraine’s Deep Strikes Exposed a Domestic Munitions Crisis

In the late-night hours of August 27, 2026, a specialized unit from the Security Service of Ukraine (SBU) conducted a long-range drone operation deep within Russian territory. The SBU’s "Alpha" Special Operations Center piloted attack drones more than 600 kilometers from the border, striking the Engels-2 strategic airbase in the Saratov region. Satellite imagery and subsequent open-source intelligence analysis confirmed that a Russian Tupolev Tu-95MS strategic bomber sustained critical damage to its right wing. Up to one-third of the wing’s structure was torn away, leaving the Soviet-era airframe permanently disabled. Because mass production of the Tu-95 ceased decades ago, the aircraft cannot be replaced. This represented the second successful strike on a Tu-95MS at Engels-2 in recent weeks, following a July 16, 2026 attack that severed the tail section of another bomber. Combined with an August 2, 2026 strike that disabled the ELOU-AVT-6 crude oil distillation unit at the nearby Rosneft Saratov refinery, these operations have systematically targeted Russia’s strategic infrastructure, reducing Moscow's active fleet to an estimated 37 combat-ready Tu-95MS bombers.

However, less than twenty-four hours after the Engels-2 strike, a parallel military development inside Ukraine highlighted severe domestic vulnerabilities. On August 28, 2026, at approximately 8:10 p.m. local time, a Russian attack drone struck an ammunition warehouse in the village of Myla, located in the Bucha district west of Kyiv along the M-06 Kyiv-Chop highway. The warehouse was used by Ukrainian defense forces to store artillery shells, mortar mines, and drone components. The impact triggered massive secondary explosions that continued for several hours, dispersing shrapnel and shockwaves across the surrounding residential area.

By August 30, the death toll from the Myla explosion rose to 38 people, with 52 others injured, including five children, and four remaining missing. The majority of the casualties occurred at the "Dobryi Dim" nursing home, a facility for the elderly and disabled situated near the warehouse. The secondary detonations destroyed the care home, resulting in the deaths of 34 residents. The explosions also damaged approximately 300 structures in the vicinity, including over 200 houses and 14 apartment blocks, and forced the temporary closure of the Kyiv-Chop highway between the 15th and 32nd kilometer markers due to unexploded ordnance.

Taras Didych, the 69-year-old head of the Dmytrivka community who had led the local administration since 1994, was killed at the scene. Didych, who remained in the community during the 2022 Russian occupation, arrived at the warehouse shortly after the initial strike to coordinate the evacuation of the nearby nursing home and residential buildings. He died during the subsequent secondary detonations.

The incident has triggered a national scandal over official negligence. Ukrainian regulations prohibit the storage of military munitions and explosives near residential areas. The Myla explosion follows a similar disaster on July 6, 2026, in the town of Vyshneve, where a Russian Iskander-M ballistic missile struck an Ukroboronprom warehouse storing munitions just 18 meters from civilian homes. The Vyshneve explosion killed nine people, injured 29, and damaged over 280 homes, leading to the arrest of the state-owned defense enterprise's director general and his deputy.

The recurrence of a secondary detonation disaster in a residential zone less than two months later has sparked widespread public criticism. On Ukrainian Telegram channels and public forums, citizens and military commentators questioned why defense officials failed to relocate munitions storage sites after the Vyshneve incident. President Volodymyr Zelenskyy addressed the tragedy on August 29, characterizing the storage of weapons near civilian homes as "terrible negligence" and confirming that the State Bureau of Investigation (DBR) has opened a criminal inquiry.

In response, Ukraine’s Prime Minister, Serhii Koretskyi—who took office in July 2026 following a government reshuffle—ordered an immediate, comprehensive audit of all military storage facilities, temporary depots, and weapons workshops nationwide. Koretskyi, who previously managed state enterprises Naftogaz and Ukrnafta, faces growing demands to enforce compliance with safety protocols across the military logistics command. The contrast between the SBU’s sophisticated long-range operations and the domestic safety failures in Myla highlights the ongoing struggle to balance wartime military logistics with civilian protection.
