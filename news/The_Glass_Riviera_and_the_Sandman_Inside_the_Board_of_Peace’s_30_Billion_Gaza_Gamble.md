# The Glass Riviera and the Sandman: Inside the Board of Peace’s $30 Billion Gaza Gamble

On the afternoon of February 19, 2026, the "Gold Room" of the newly minted Board of Peace headquarters in Mar-a-Lago hosted what was meant to be a masterclass in post-UN diplomacy. President Donald Trump, flanked by former British Prime Minister Tony Blair and real estate mogul Steve Witkoff, sat at the head of a mahogany table designed to symbolize a "new world order" built not on resolutions, but on real estate. The agenda was the $30 billion "Master Plan" for Gaza—a vision of 180 skyscrapers, coastal tourism zones, and a Mediterranean "Riviera" that Jared Kushner had unveiled just weeks prior at Davos. But as the technical briefing on supply chain logistics with Dubai-based DP World reached its twentieth minute, the "Commander-in-Chief" became the "Commander-in-Sleep."

The footage, which would within minutes be christened the "Dozy Don" incident, showed the 79-year-old President with his eyes firmly shut, his chin dipping toward his chest as a slide deck detailed the construction of a deep-sea port. It was a momentary optical failure, but in the hyper-polarized lead-up to the 2028 election cycle, it became a geopolitical explosion. California Governor Gavin Newsom, widely seen as the Democratic frontrunner for the next cycle, was the first to strike. "That’s not a still photo of Dozy Don," Newsom’s press office posted on X. "He’s really just asleep while the world waits for peace."

The White House response was swift and characteristic of the second Trump administration’s combative press strategy. The White House Rapid Response account dismissed the viral video, replying to critics with a blunt: "He was blinking, you absolute moron." Later, Trump took to Truth Social to offer his own technical defense, claiming he was simply closing his "beautiful blue eyes" to "listen intensely" and "take it all in," an echo of his defense during his 2024 legal trials. Yet, the incident has peeled back the curtain on the exhaustion of an administration attempting to run a global "UN alternative" out of a private club.

The Board of Peace (BoP) itself is a radical departure from traditional diplomacy. Authorized by the ambiguous UN Security Council Resolution 2803 in November 2025, the BoP operates on what critics call a "pay-to-play" model. Permanent membership requires a $1 billion contribution, a fee already paid by the United States, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE. Trump, who serves as the BoP’s "Chairman-for-life," has positioned the board as a "nimble" fix for the gridlock in New York. However, the organization’s structure—managed by a board that includes Jared Kushner, Marco Rubio, and World Bank President Ajay Banga—has raised sharp questions about where diplomacy ends and private investment begins.

Tony Blair’s involvement has added a veneer of "technocratic" legitimacy to the board, though his appointment in January 2026 drew immediate fire from the UK’s Keir Starmer government, which has boycotted the BoP alongside France and Germany. Blair has publicly stated he is "honored" to serve, focusing on the BoP’s mandate to establish a National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG)—a body of 15 Palestinian technocrats designed to replace Hamas governance. But the tension is palpable; while Blair speaks of "lawful governance," the Kushner vision is undeniably commercial. The "New Gaza" plan aims to raise the territory’s GDP to $10 billion by 2035, featuring data centers and advanced manufacturing zones, yet the $50 billion to $70 billion reconstruction cost remains largely speculative as Hamas has yet to fully accept the board’s 15-point disarmament roadmap.

The "Dozy Don" incident has emboldened a growing coalition of domestic and international skeptics. In Washington, the narrative of a "fertility crisis" championed by HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. and the administration’s focus on radical deregulation have kept the GOP base energized, but Trump’s physical stamina has become the primary target for opponents. The "Dozy Don" label has stuck, appearing in over 4 million posts on X within 48 hours of the February meeting. For Newsom and the "shadow" Democratic campaign, the strategy is to mirror Trump’s own 2024 attacks on a "Sleepy Joe" Biden, turning the President’s age into a bellwether for his ability to manage the most complex reconstruction project in modern history.

Behind the memes, however, is a deeper conflict of interest. The BoP’s reliance on private entities like DP World and the proposed "trilateral crossing" at Rafah have led to friction with Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s office. In early 2026, Netanyahu’s aides signaled that the BoP’s inclusion of Turkey and Qatar was "not coordinated" with Israeli security policy. Trump’s ultimatum—that Hamas must disarm or be "blown away very quickly"—has maintained his "strongman" image, but the image of him nodding off during a briefing on Gaza’s $30 billion skyscrapers suggests a disconnect between the rhetoric of "winning" and the grueling, minute-to-minute reality of international administration. As the Board of Peace prepares for its next high-level summit in Dubai, the world is watching not just the maps of "New Gaza," but the eyelids of the man drawing them.
