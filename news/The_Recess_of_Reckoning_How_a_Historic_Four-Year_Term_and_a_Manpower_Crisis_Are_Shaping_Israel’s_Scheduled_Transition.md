# The Recess of Reckoning: How a Historic Four-Year Term and a Manpower Crisis Are Shaping Israel’s Scheduled Transition

On July 17, 2026, the 25th Knesset is scheduled to officially dissolve, launching Israel into a highly anticipated campaign season that will culminate in the national election on October 27, 2026. This legislative recess marks a rare milestone in the country’s modern political history. For the first time since 1988, an Israeli general election will take place exactly on its legally mandated date, concluding the first government to complete its full four-year term since 1973. Despite years of intense protests, regional conflict, and historic security failures, Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu’s right-wing coalition has defied expectations of an early collapse. The upcoming vote is widely framed as a national referendum on Netanyahu’s leadership and his coalition’s handling of the post-October 7, 2023 security and military actions. The political stakes are drawing close attention from international allies, including the United States, where the administration of President Donald J. Trump continues to monitor the democratic process in Jerusalem.

However, the path to the October election is far from smooth, as the coalition faces a severe internal crisis over military conscription. On July 12, 2026, the Knesset Foreign Affairs and Defense Committee, chaired by Likud MK Boaz Bismuth, approved a highly contentious bill designed to temporarily freeze the arrest of Haredi (ultra-Orthodox) draft evaders. The legislation grants immunity from detention to yeshiva students who fail to report for mandatory military service until November 30, 2026. Because the Knesset is scheduled to dissolve later this week, Article 38 of Israel’s Basic Law will likely extend this immunity for at least seven months, shielding draft dodgers throughout the election period. The bill also halts existing criminal proceedings and removes financial sanctions against yeshiva deans who falsely report student attendance.

This legislative maneuver has ignited a firestorm of opposition from the military establishment and legal advisors. IDF Chief of Staff Lt. Gen. Eyal Zamir has voiced unequivocal opposition to the bill, warning that a sectoral exemption would severely damage the legitimacy of the military and create national security risks during a period of prolonged conflict. The IDF faces an acute manpower shortage, requiring at least 12,000 combat and combat-support personnel. Despite this critical need, an estimated 80,000 Haredi men aged 18 to 24 are currently eligible for service but remain un-enlisted, and the military’s target of recruiting 4,800 Haredi soldiers for the 2024–2025 period fell heavily short. The Knesset committee's own legal team issued a warning that the bill creates "unlawful discrimination" and violates the fundamental principle of equality before the law.

The political survival of the coalition has rested on making these concessions to its ultra-Orthodox partners. Coalition Chairman Ofir Katz, who also chairs the Knesset House Committee, has worked behind the scenes to keep the ruling bloc unified ahead of the July 17 dissolution. The coalition's reliance on the ultra-Orthodox parties to pass the bill has sharpened electoral divisions, with opposition figures hoping to leverage secular resentment at the polls. Public discussions on social media are highly polarized. On platforms like X.com and Reddit’s r/politics, critics argue that the administration is prioritizing its own survival over national security, pointing to the exhausted state of reserve soldiers. Conversely, supporters of the coalition argue that completing the full four-year term demonstrates essential government stability and resilience under unprecedented external pressure.

Compounding the political tension are Netanyahu's own comments regarding his future. In an interview released on July 9, 2026, on the *Salon with Sharon* podcast, hosted by Sharon Gal of i24NEWS, the Prime Minister stated that he has a "suitable person" in mind to succeed him. Netanyahu claimed that he had discussed the transition with this individual and is "ready to give the keys to the country" to them, though he refused to name the person or specify a timeline for his departure. While Netanyahu is actively campaigning for re-election in October, the remarks have sparked intense speculation and debate. Under the Israeli political system, a prime minister cannot formally designate a successor, as leadership is determined through party primaries and Knesset coalition-building. 

The opposition is organizing to exploit these vulnerabilities. In April 2026, former Prime Ministers Naftali Bennett and Yair Lapid announced a unified list called "Together" (Beyachad), with Bennett serving as the leader. The alliance combines Bennett's "Bennett 2026" party and Lapid's "Yesh Atid" party on a platform centered on ending Haredi draft exemptions, establishing a state commission of inquiry into the October 7 failures, and setting term limits for the prime minister. Concurrently, the center-left has consolidated under the "Democrats" (HaDemokratim) party, formed in July 2024 by Yair Golan through the merger of Labor and Meretz. Boasting approximately 100,000 members by mid-2026, Golan, a retired IDF Deputy Chief of Staff, is mobilizing a large liberal-left bloc.

This political battle is occurring alongside an ongoing struggle over the country's legal institutions. Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara has remained a central target for the coalition. Since her appointment in February 2022, she has faced constant pressure, including a cabinet vote of no confidence in March 2025 and a formal ouster attempt in August 2025, which was subsequently annulled by the High Court of Justice. In July 2026, Baharav-Miara is actively fighting coalition-sponsored bills designed to split the office of the Attorney General and allow the cabinet to reject her legal opinions.

The national mood has shifted significantly since the height of the war. The immediate crisis of the hostages concluded after the release of the final living hostages in October 2025 and the recovery of the last remaining hostage’s body in January 2026. In early July 2026, Israelis marked 1,000 days since the October 7, 2023 attacks. In Tel Aviv, the area formerly known as "Hostages Square" was renamed "Memory Square" for the occasion, serving as a gathering point for thousands demanding an independent state commission of inquiry. As the Knesset prepares to dissolve on July 17, the country is no longer united by immediate existential defense, but is instead entering a fierce debate over accountability, the rule of law, and the political future of its longest-serving prime minister.
