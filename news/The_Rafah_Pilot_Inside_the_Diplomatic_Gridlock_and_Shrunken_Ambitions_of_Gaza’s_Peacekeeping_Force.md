# The Rafah Pilot: Inside the Diplomatic Gridlock and Shrunken Ambitions of Gaza’s Peacekeeping Force

The approval of a legal framework by the Israeli Security Cabinet on July 26, 2026, was presented by international mediators as a breakthrough for the long-stalled postwar governance of the Gaza Strip. Under the approved guidelines, the International Stabilization Force (ISF) is permitted to deploy into the enclave to support a pilot humanitarian zone near Rafah. Yet, the scale of this deployment reveals the gap between diplomatic framework goals and the physical realities on the ground. Established under United Nations Security Council Resolution 2803 in November 2025, the ISF was originally envisioned as a robust, 20,000-strong multinational peacekeeping force tasked with overseeing Gaza's demilitarization, reconstruction, and transition to a technocratic government led by the National Committee for the Administration of Gaza (NCAG). Instead, the pilot phase approved by Jerusalem permits a mere 200 troops from Morocco and Uganda to enter, restricted to non-combat infrastructure zones under strict Israel Defense Forces (IDF) coordination.

This limited deployment is the result of months of diplomatic gridlock and intense domestic political jockeying in Israel. The entry of even this limited contingent is subject to individual, case-by-case sign-offs by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu, Defense Minister Israel Katz, and Foreign Minister Gideon Sa'ar. The requirement underscores the deep skepticism within the Israeli security establishment, which fears that an international presence will restrict the IDF’s freedom of operation against residual militant cells. During the cabinet meeting, National Security Minister Itamar Ben-Gvir voted against the framework, declaring that because Hamas has not disarmed, Israel is under no obligation to honor its side of the plan. Ben-Gvir continues to advocate for the "voluntary emigration" of Palestinians from Gaza, a position that clashes directly with the U.S.-backed peace framework.

The primary operational obstacle is the "Yellow Line"—the ceasefire demarcation line established under the October 2025 ceasefire agreement. Initially intended as a temporary division of the territory that left approximately 53% of the Gaza Strip under Israeli control, the line has steadily crept westward. Today, the IDF maintains direct military control over more than 60% of the territory, transforming the areas near the Yellow Line into highly restricted buffer zones where approaching forces risk being targeted. For the ISF to operate, it must navigate these shifting boundaries. Under the cabinet’s terms, the peacekeeping troops are restricted to areas behind the Yellow Line, while the IDF maintains complete control over the strategic corridors and crossings.

For the contributing nations, the decision to participate is fraught with geopolitical risk. Morocco, which signed a bilateral participation agreement on July 15, 2026, in Rabat, has been the most proactive. It has committed gendarmerie forces, military personnel, and a field hospital, and has already dispatched a team of 30 officers to conduct site visits in southern Gaza. However, Rabat’s involvement has drawn fierce criticism from regional players and local factions, who view Arab participation under these terms as direct complicity in the entrenchment of Israeli military control. Critics argue that a force vetted and controlled by the IDF serves merely as an administrative manager for the occupation rather than a pathway to Palestinian sovereignty. Researchers at organizations like Al-Shabaka have warned that the NCAG risks functioning as a managerial apparatus without true political authority.

Other contributors are moving with extreme caution. The Ugandan military (UPDF) has given preliminary approval to deploy following a direct request from U.S. President Donald Trump. However, Ugandan military officials have stated they are awaiting formal strategic directives from the presidency before finalizing the size and mandate of their contingent. Kosovo and Albania, seeking to demonstrate alignment with U.S. foreign policy, have taken active steps; Kosovo’s parliament approved troop deployments in April 2026, and both nations have dispatched assessment teams, with some Kosovar logistics officers already on the ground. Meanwhile, Indonesia, which had initially pledged a large contingent of peacekeepers, suspended its participation entirely, citing the risk of regional escalation and intense domestic backlash.

The civilian governing body designed to succeed Hamas, the NCAG, is also caught in this web of security constraints. Chaired by Dr. Ali Shaath, a Khan Younis-born civil engineer with a doctorate from Queen’s University Belfast, the 15-member technocratic committee was established in January 2026. While Hamas announced the dissolution of its Government Emergency Committee in early July to facilitate the transition, Dr. Shaath and his colleagues have faced severe Israeli travel restrictions, preventing them from establishing a functioning presence inside the enclave. 

The operational viability of a 200-member peacekeeping force in an active conflict zone remains highly doubtful. With Hamas refusing to disarm and pointing to ongoing Israeli operations, and the IDF retaining veto power over every international soldier's movement, the pilot program risks becoming a symbolic exercise rather than a stabilization force. High Representative for Gaza Nickolay Mladenov, who leads the Board of Peace under President Trump's supervision, has warned that the stalled status quo and the permanent division of Gaza represent a "ticking time bomb." As Netanyahu travels to Washington today, July 27, to meet with President Donald Trump, the gap between the grand diplomatic vision of Resolution 2803 and the micro-contingent approved for Rafah highlights the limits of international peacekeeping in the face of strict security control.
